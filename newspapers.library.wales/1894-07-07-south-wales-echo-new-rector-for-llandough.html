    The Man About Town. I A few days back I spoke of Shakespeare and his treatment of Welsh characters. It forms an interesting literary question becauso it is not at first sight easy to under- stand his evident and pronounced partiality for Wales. A mere general sympathy with a country which was united with the kingdom in which he lived might have been expected. But Shakespeare's feelings were strong and almost biassed, and it will remain for us to seek some explanation for the phenomenon. Several curious points are involved in the evident partiality of Shakespeare for the Welsh character. Not only do we find him drawing largely upon that little Principality for some of his most carefully elaborated characters, but it is clear that he was 1 at considerable pains, amidst all the temptations of caricature, to express his admiration for their genius. It is the more striking because there is no reference of any importance to the Welsh race in the plays of his contemporaries. If we except the Welsh captain who makes a brief entry into Marlowe's Edward II. and is no more than a lay figure, it may be asserted that I Shakespeare alone of the Elizabethan dramatists gave Wales a place in literature. He had a peculiar affection for the race, and it is, at first, a little puzzling to account for it. Glendower, for instance, is not altogether an engaging being, with his portents and earthquakes and all the other dread portents at his birth. Hotspur ridicules him with hearty goodwill, and it would seem natural to the dramatist to set a trap for his overweening vanity and bring his vaunts to scorn. If we still further remem- ber that, after all this tall talk, Glendower does not in the end arrive upon the scene at the battle of Shrewsbury, it would seem almost inevitable that the poet would im- prove upon the occasion by timely references to his former arrogance, and hint of cowar- dice. » But it Is curious to note how careful Shakespeare is, amidst all the laughter at his irregular and wild Glendower," to make emphatic excuse for him. Mortimer, for instance, says, in reply to Hotspur In faith, he is a worthy gentleman, Exceedingly wel] read, and profited In strange concealments, valiant as a lion, And wondrous ntfable and as bountiful As mines of India. There was no historic necessity for this defence, nor does the play require it, for the gallant chieftain disappears forthwith. Shakespeare evidently had other motives for his friendly feeling toward a Welsh rebel. There are the same temptations to ridiculo in Henry V., where Fluellen appears. He has many foibles and eccentricities which might lead to an appropriate nemesis. He possesses even some of the arrogance of Glfindower, for he grumbles at the orders of war, and threatens "By Cheshu, I think a' will plow up all, if there is not better direc- tions. And although he thinks Macmorris to be "an ass, as in the world," and declares that he will verify as much in his beard," yet when the Scotchman appears he forgets his boast, and would have him stay to argue. He wants a few disputations with you, as partly touching or concerning the disciplines of the war, the Reman wars, in the name of argument, look you, and friendly communication." Later on, Mac- morris fancies there is a slight intended for his nationality and flies into a rage. What becomes of Fluellen's eagerness to hurl an ass, as in the world at his beard ? He mildly retorts Look you, if you take the matter otherwise than is meant, Captain Macmorris, peradventure I shall think you do not use me with that aff-ability as in dis- cretion you ought to use me being as good a man as yourself both in the disciplines of war, and in the derivations of my birth, and in other part iculari ties. This might very easily be mistaken for discretion and it needs a strong artistic self-restraint to avoid accentuating the situation, but Shakespeare is here as before at pains to insist on the courage of his hero, and Henry himself observes, as if to make the poet's intention quite clear: Though it appears a little out of fashion, liiero is much care and valour in this Welshman. Indeed, the King specially marks him out for distinction by giving him Alençon's gage, a trust of which he is clearly worthy. • In this case,too, it is clear that Shakespeare is at pains to emphasise the virtues of the Welsh character even when tempted to make ic the butt of his satire. In the jhwj Wives of Windsor there is small occa- [name] for partiality of this nature. And yet Sir Hugh Evans cannot be said to shine inconspicuously. A comedy that is through- out somewhat wild might offer licence denied to loftier drama; and there are occasions in which the discreet knight is in peril of his good name.
