THE CLOSE OF 1864. ANOTHER year is drawing to a close, and with its termination reflection steals on the imagination. Christmas is over and gone, carrying with it the joys and sorrows of millions of God's heavenward-trained children. The close of each year is an important event, full of weighty material for every thinking mind, preceded asit is by a festive season which, from tts peculiar nature, seems to raise man in the Christian scale. Christmas, to those who are free from care and poverty, comes like the visit of some good genius, elevating the moral character, making man better, wiser, and happier and its halo falls like a mantle of suow upon the rugged earth, covering with angel purity ttie dark spots of corruption which disfigures the true aspect of nature. It is a period when brethren meet together to relate their experience of the world-how they h ive suffered by man's duplicity, or have been raised by the hand of kindness. But it is also a period when the poor, the way-worn, the hungry, the half-starved, the ill-paid of our crowded cities crouch around the few smouldering embers still kindled in the grate, and feel the jealous pangs cf envy running riot in the hearts as their keen scent sniffs in the savoury odours which issue from the oft- opened portals of their mo; e fortunate human brother. The close of the year is the period when the man of money balances up his accounts, and finding himself a richer man, carries to his next year's capital a large addition of this world's goods. It is the period when the tradesman is called on to look more closely into his position, to ascertain whether he is solvent or no. It is a period when all who have taken an active part in any public movement, look back to see how far their own cause has surged onward in the stream of tiiri". It is a period of importance to the old, to the young, to the rich, to the poor, to the fortunate, to the unfortunate. To the statesman it is an epoch in the world's history that has planned or tested the success or failure of some national measure. To the local politician it forms another stage in the development of the re- sources of some particular spot, and shows whether the town has advanced or retrograded under the sway of the existing municipal government. And thus, step by step, sliding from the general to the particular, we are naturally led to look around and cast a retro- spective glance at our own town, and from a few of the facts, which as public journalists we see week by week passing before our gaze, gather an augury of future success or failure. CHRISTMAS CHURCH DECORATIONS. The manifestation of reverential feelings exhibits itself in various ways-a bequest for chruch endowment-a life spent in carrying out in its many ramifications one or more of the objects contem- plated in the establishment of any system of religion. In the pride exhibited in the adornment of the House dedicated to Him who implanted in the mind of his image the desire of worship. The Indian sets aside a particular spot for the exercise of his devotional feelings; the Mahometan looks with awe on the house of prayer, and carefully abstains from even casting a stain on the floor with sandaled feet; the Christian, not only looks with veneration on the build- ing dedicated to the worship of the Creator; but, as his system of religion points out certain periods in the year, marked by some event in the life of Him who offered himself a sacrifice for many, to be re- garded with more than usual feelings of reverence; so the day on which that Saviour was born, is looked upon as one of the most joyful periods in the Christian eraâone which calls for some particular manifestation both outwardly and inwardly. The Jews regarded the Feast of the Tabernacle as one of theirjmost particular festive gatherings, and decorated their tents with flowers and fruits. The Christian regards the birth of the Saviour as the grand event of the Christian era, and some of them mark that period by a desire to ornament with a few cheering devices the sacred building devoted to the worship of the Creator and Saviour. There is something peculiarly pleasing in this desire to decorate our sacred edifices. Cold and fatigue are braved by delicate hands unused to toil and hardships, and many an hour is occupied in a work for which perhaps the silent thanks of a few is the only recompense. It is a feeling akin to the strewing of the graves of departed relatives with flowers, and deserves every encouragement; and, that it is spreading year by year, is good evidence of the deep-seated religious feelings existing in the hearts of the lay portion of our congregations. LLANDAFF CATHEDRAL As the principal ecclesiastical structure in the locality, has received from the hands of the ama- teur church decorators a considerable share of at- tention. Foremost in this good work are the ladies of the congregation, Miss Olivant, the Misses Williams and Blosse, assisted by a numerous band of other ladies; and foremost among the group of gentlemen, who have been instrumental in giving a warmth of tone to the cold blocks of masonry, is Lieut,-{Jol. Hill. The decorations of holly, ivy, &c., have been but.sparinglyintroduced, compared with the space which required relief; but the effect of the whole of the decorations on the entry of the visitor is particularly pleasing. No effort has been made to place ornament for the sake of changing the appear- ance of the interior, but care has been taken to make each part serve some purpose in the general design. The lofty arches of the columns separating the aisles from the nave have each a band of ivy and laurel carefully fitted to the fillets, while the capitals have pendant foliage of a lighter character. The font is covered with a hollow octagonal dome, surmountod with a cross worked in flowers and moss, strings of the red berry of the holly serve to decorate it, while bands of the gold-edged holly I surround the rim and the base, forming a pleasing contrast to the pure Bath stone. A bed of moss form the ground for the pedestal to rest on. The ornamental details of the splendidly carved stone pulpit has received some delicate tracery; minute bands of myrtle and box entwine between the columns and the arches, while the balustrades are wreathed with ivy and holly. The Bishop's throne has also some ornament, and bears in the front a cross, worked in moss and flowers. The choir and organ are tastefully decorated with variegated holly, while the gas-burners are gracefully covered with evergreens. Behind the communion table is a large flat surface of moss, in the centre of which is worked in ecclesiastical characters one of the usual mottoes of this season, "Peace on Earth." Immediately over this, concealing a por- tion of the beautifully painted chancel window, is a cross worked, as the others, in moss and flowers, surmounting the motto, Unto us a child is born." Beneath the window, on each side, are the entwined circles, symbolical of the Trinity. Over the com- munion table the three arches are filled in with exquisite and appropriate paintings in oil; thus shewing the work of restoration to be progressing, both in the interior and exterior. The various tombs are also gracefully draped with laurel. The Lady Chapel is embellished with holly, yew, and laurel. The pulpit is very prettily decorated, and the arches over the communion table are filled with small branches of holly and fern, The tomb of the Rev. William Bruce Knight, M.A., is almost concealed by the drapery of laurel and ivy. The various portions of the original Norman structure still remaining unrestored are ornamented with evergreens. The design of the entire mass of decorations is pretty and effective; the object being to relieve the eye from the monotonous effect of the stone, and bring out in stronger relief the richly carved details of both nave and chancel. ST. JOHN'S. As the leading church, the extent to which the de- corations are carried is larger than any other each of the pillars in the spacious nave has a band of holly and ivy twirled round it in a spiral form. The capitals of the columns are sligthly ornamented with leaves. The fronts of the galleries are festooned in the same manner, and every gas burner presents an array of leaves and berries; the mottoes this year being entirely confined to the chancel. The spacious nave has received a mass of floral decoration, not equalled in preceding- years. The font is very prettily orna- mented, as well as the reading desk and pulpit. The chancel is very richly decorated, the columns have similar spiral bands to those in the nave; the capitals are also wreathed with holly, while bands of laurel are carefully worked in the fillets of the arches. This could not be attempted in the nave, from the great height of the columns. The east end, with the beautifully painted window, presents a very pretty appearance. Bands of holly surround the window, and are continued over the communion table. On each side of the window is worked one motto, the letters being separately placed on the wall, and tastefully formed with leaves of ivy and laurel, the text being, Unto you is born this day, within the City of David, a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord." And over the arch of the window another motto equally appropriate, Good tidings of great joy and on each side are stars, worked with the same material, enclosed in circles. From the spacious i character of the building, there is ample room for a very large mass of decorations without giving to it a crowded or unsightly appearance but from the green tone of the glass windows on the south and north sides, the best effect is undoubtedly obtained when the gas is lighted. This is another instance of the praiseworthy exertions of the ladies. We have been informed that the decorations are principally due to the Misses Grover, R. L. Reece, and Scale, under whose directions and assistance the various' mottoes, &c., were executed and placed in their respective positions. Too much praise cannot be accorded to these ladies for the taste displayed in the design and the points seized on for ornamentation. ST. MARY'S. The weather for the last few days has been, what pleasure-seekers could only wish it to be, fine, dry, and keen. A temperature a few degrees below the freez- ing point gives zest to the various games which cha- racterize a, Christmas holiday, and serve as a powerful aid to the alimentary organs of the juvenile portion of the community in digesting the store of rich materials which had been crowded into the stomach. Christmas this year hts, from the fineness of th'e weather, been almost exceptional, and the power of enjoyment seemed without bound. THE CIRCUSES. Later in the day, when exercise had lost some of its charms, throngs of people wended their way to Quaglieni s Circus and the Amphitheatre. Being a day performance, the preponderance was on the side of youth, groups of five and six being carefully chaperoned by the Mater Familias, Papa being on the occasion a supernumerary, to be put anywhere but in front. As a Christmas show, for children nothing can equal an equestrian entertainment. Children always delight in feats of horsemanship, be- yond any other kind of exhibition, especially when a pantomime is to be introduced. The doors both at Quaglieni's Circus and the Amphitheatre at the Music Hall were besieged by hundreds long before the time fixed for the opening, and very soon at the circus every available spot was occupied by clusters of merry sparkling eyes interspersed with an almost equal number of more sedate, but perhaps more boisterous juveniles of the other six. Gallery, pit, boies, and stalls presented a sea of heads, and the proprietor reluctantly was at last compelled to say, There's no more room The loud clapping of hands by the children shewed how heartily they enjoyed the entertainment: the pantomime being all that could be desired. The most popular foundation for the Christmas Pantomime of 1864, have been the old nursery rhymes which the oldest of us have listenedtoyearsago; and, while the public at "Astleys" are applauding, perhaps, in a more legitimate sphere The cat in the cupboard, and she can't see me," or The lion and the unicorn fighting for the crown," at her Majesty's," the younger scions of our own locality are equally loud in their plaudits at the spec- tacle of "Ride a cock-horse to Banbury Cross." On this day the whole strength of the company did their best to render the entertainment pleasing to young and old, and strangers were astonished at the daring and graceful male and female equestrians. The circus in the evening was equally crowded, and numbers were unable to obtain admission. The en- tertainment at the Music Hall, though perhaps not quite so fashionably attended, was well supported, and at each performance there was a "crowded house." The riders and the clowns did their best and as at such times human nature is not inclined to be hypercritical, they succeeded in drawing down repeated bursts of applause. Here again the per- formance was of a character well worthy of a visit, and many country cousins will return home with a long tale of what they saw and heard at the circus. It is somewhat unfortunate that at a time when the town is crowded with visitors, and a large por- tion of the inhabitants free from toil, that no other place of amusement was open. There is, however, a pleasing feature, that though the police court was closed on Monday and Tuesday, but few cases of any importance were reported at the police station, and the streets were not made the medium of exhibitions of drunkenness. As far as the holiday itself was concerned, everything passed over remarkably well;" and though the season was one' when the temptation to joviality is greater than, usual, the Monday and Tuesday were observed much more soberly than would have been expected a few years ago.
