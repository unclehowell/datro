    HEOLYCYW AND DISTRICT NOTES (By SILURIAN.) ————— ————— Few, if any, will disagree with the tren- chant criticism from the able pen of Mr. E. D. Lewis, St. Mary Hill Court, on our Guar- dians' extravagance, and which appeared in last week's "Gazette." The extravagance which characterises the administration of County and District Councils' business, and Board s of Guardians' work is simply deplor- able at all times, and staggering at such times as these, when thrift is so imperative. Some of our County and District Councillors readily admit that the burden of the rates has become well-night intolerable, but they declare that they are unable to stem the tide. Well, the poor ratepayers should look about for men to represent them who could and would stem the tide of waste. The Bridgend branch of the Soldiers' and Sailors' Families' Association paid last quarter nearly j69,000 in relief to de- pendents, and its administration expenses amounted to less than Hd. in the t. If we had .iv local authorities to administer their work on similar lines, our rates would soon drop 50 per cent. The remedy lies with the ratepayers themselves, who will be relieved as n a, ..qc-v to i>e relieved, but no sooner. m e 0 It is gratifying to remark that some slight differences between employers and employed at Haglan Collieries have just been satisfac- torily adjusted, and that work proceeds regu- larly and briskly, with a steadily increasing output. More men are also put on weekly at Werntaro, where the main heading is in more than 400 yards, and where the railway siding, over half a mile long, is nearing completion. All the houses at Heolycyw are not oc- cupied yet, but evidently the worst is over, and the village will soon assume its former prosperous condition in the palmy days of the esteemed Messrs.     I THE CARDIFF VACANCY. l FINDING A SUCCESSOR TO LATE LORD NINIAN STUART. So far no steps have been taken by the Conservative party in the Cardiff district constituency to select a candidate to fill the Parliamentary vacancy created by the death in action of Colonel Lord Ninian Crichton- Stuart. It is understood, however, that steps will be taken to approach certain gentlemen with a view to getting their consent, and that their names will be put torward as candidates at a future meeting. The final selection will be made at a full meeting of the Conservative Association, which is expect-ed to take place in about a week's time. Lord Colum Crichton-Stuart, brother of the late member, will not, it is understood, accept nomination owing to his position in the diplo- matic service. Among the likely candidates are Lord Windsor, who is now on active service; Mr. J. Herbert Cory (president of the Cardiff Con- servative Assciation), Sir John Courtis, Col. Frank Gaskell, and Dr. G. N. W. Thomas. I LIBERALS MEET. I The Executive Committee of the Cardiff Liberal Association met on Tuesday evening, under the presidency of Councillor G. F. Fors- dike. The Chairman said he knew he was acting in consonance with the wishes of all present in expressing their deep sympathy with Lady Ninian Crichton-Stuart and family in the great loss which they had sustained. At present every one in Cardiff united in a single sentiment; and they had, in common with the Conservatives, a feeling of deep gratitude to Lord Ninian that he came for- ward in the great crisis and took his place amongst the national defenders; and now he I had died for his country. He was a gentle- man of position and wealth, having in prospect a life blessed with promise of all those advantages which men sought so greatly to obtain; and yet he had entered the lists in national defence, and had suffered death as the result.. They were bound to admire the great part he had played as a gallant and courageous man; and something of the glory of his great deed reflected upon every citizen of Cardiff. (Applause.) Mr. C. F. Saunders, J.P.. seconded the resolution, which read as follows:— That this Executive Committee of the Cardiff Liberal Association desires to ex- press its sympathy with Lady Ninian Crichton-Stuart, and the members of the family of the late member for Cardiff, in his untimely and much lamented death. Mr. Sanders said that at a time like this the least that could be done by that committee, as representative of the party opposed to Lord Ninian, was to pass such a resolution. He joined with the chairman in his tribute to the late member, there being only one feeling in the minds of them all. Lord Ninian had many admirable qualities, and those who had met him, even but casually, or had no more than seen him in his public appearances, could not have failed to be impressed by his genial personality. He won great respect, not alone from his political friends and associates, but also from many outside their ranks. (Hear, hear.) The resolution was passed in silence, all the assembly rising to manifest support, and the secretary was instructed to forward a copy to Lady Ninian Stuart. The Chairman then submitted a recommen- dation from the General Purposes Committee on the subject of the Parliamentary represen- tation of the constituency, setting forth the circumstances both as to the national and local conditions in this period of truce, and he formally moved:— That as the death of Lord Ninian Stuart causes a vacancy in the representation of the United Boroughs in Parliament, it is the opinion of the members that the national party truce ought to be observed. In the national interest this Association follows the lead of other constituencies, and agrees that upon this occasion the Conservative candidate should be returned unopposed. The recommendation was accepted, and the resolution unanimously agreed to. It was announced that Mr. A. J. Howell, the secret.ary of the Young Liberals, had written from the Dardanelles stating that although he was in the thick of the fighting he was very well, and desired to be remem- bered to his friends on the Executive.

    BRIDGEND MAN IN THE I DARDANELLES. INTERESTING STORY. I Sapper C. G. Jones, R.E., formerly of the Bridgend Post. Office, writes an interesting letter home, describing life and its experiences in the Dardanelles. He says:- 'Tis a long way indeed. When I left my sorting-Tack on the first day of the magical year, 1915, I was enamoured by the pros- pects of doing my bit for my country, and imagined that ere three months had passed I should be actively engaged with the mighty army that in the spring was to stem the Ger- man tide and hurl back the ravaging masses of the Kaiser's hordes, even to the gates of Berlin itself. I kicked my heels about the Homeland's training centres, until on the 16th of last month my division actually sailed from a famous English port en route for the East. You realise how far you have to go, though when you see the last of good old England's shore fleeting away on your starboard, and the echoing sound of a final farewell is borne to your hyper-sensitive ear by the breeze blowing across the friendly Channel-how friendly you have never imagined before. It had rained incessantly all day, and we ha,d been as busy as bees loading the ship- hungry, rain-soaked, despondent. But it was marvellous what a change a meal wrought in us, and by the time our transport slipped her moorings we were in good spirits, although about to bid our country farewell. The rain had ceased also, the atmosphere cleared, and as if an augur of good luck, the sun shone serenely through the rapidly-dispersing clouds. Oh! what a send-off was ours. It made the heart beat quicker, and one felt proud of being a Britisher! Gradually we ploughed our way through the waters of the harbour, whilst our gallant little pilot pierced the air with insis- tent blasts from her siren. Our noble flag of red, white and blue fluttered majestically in the Channel breeze, and the array of ship- ping—merchant vessels, transports, warships —cheered us to the very echo. From shore to ship and from ship to shore the real good old British cheer rang, and with heart and voioe we responded, with our souls full of the gloriousness of being British subjects and of the pride of our deathless mission. Torpedo- boat and cruiser—aye, and battleship-in the Sound joined in the cheering, and as the dear old Isle died away behind us we felt that as long as there was a breath left in our body we should determinedly fight for that sacred land, those dear homes, those glorious tradi- tions of ours. Safely towed out of the har- bour, our little pilot left us, and our ship boldly set out on her own. Dusk fell, and we reoollected the terror of the submarine, but a shadowy object discer- nible on the horizon doad ahead inspired con- fidence-our convoy, one of Britain's sea- dogs! To describe the voyage would be futile, as over-exactitude would only invite the cen- sorious attention of a certain potent official! Life from day to day on board a troopship is crammed full with work, and we were soon [initiated into the mystery of deck-swabbing, stable work, and general fatigue work on board. The first and second days saw us all more or less suffering from mal-de-mer, but after the first effects had worn off we settled to and made the best of things. The food was good and wholesome, fresh air we had in abundance, and work to our hearts' desire. For days we saw no land, and to a land-lubber it is somewhat unusual to wake in the morning and see nothing but water every- where. How mysterious the deep! An end- less, fathomless expanse of sea. One would gaze on its waves and wonder. How wonder- full changeable-one moment as calm and as gentle as a child, the next a seething, vicious, relentless surface. To look at it just now not a rebel wave disturbed its calm. Then my thoughts wandered back to the shores of my island home, to a certain peaceful evening of last summer, when the same sea in its cruel relentlessness claimed a dear friend of mine as its victim; the angry waves beat down the feeble human resistance, and in a second the deep had closed over a gallant colleague! The SAME sea! (This refers to the drowning of Mr. Rowlands, a clerk at the Bridgend Post Office, at Ogmore, in August, 1914). We called at two famous ports, both im- portant strategic positions and gallant up- holders of our mighty Empire-branches of the living tree. To attempt to specify these again would only give scope for the ability of the wielder of a mighty blue pencil. We had ample op- portunity of re-assuring ourselves of the true efficiency of our Navy and the true value of our world-wide Empire's supremacy on the seas. We traversed hundreds of miles of water, and wherever we went we would in- variably come across one of Britain's indomi- table warships always on the alert. Now we know the true meaning of the term, "Our Navy has swept the seas," and our confidence in Albion's complete mastery of the ocean is greater than ever. I often thought, God help an enemy ship that would attempt to sail these seas! I can imagine one of Germany's light cruisers escaping the vigilance of our ring of steel in the North Sea by night, and with full steam ahead gaining the ocean be- yond. Can you see her? Ere she has left the Channel she is being pursued by two of the greyhounds of the home waters fleet. Maybe in virtue of her speed she outdistances them; but you wait! "Atlantic, Mediterranean," did I hear you say! Oh, yes; but even as our enemy gains her freedom two destroyers ap- pear here, a battle cruiser there-and she goes to sleep in the deep. All this is imagination, but after what I've seen I know what would happen. But I am wandering! After ten days' sail we arrived in the ex- quisite land of glorious sunsets, incomparable midday sun, and practically everlasting sum- mer—Egypt. We anchored in the harbour of its chief port prior to coming into dock, and for one wonderful week we saw visions of Eastern life and saw glimpses of scenery char- acteristic of the land of early civilisation. Ere long we became accustomed to the semi- tropical heat, the deep white complexion of our homeland clime became a deeper tan of sun-converted brown, and within less than two days the change of conditions was hardly noticeable at all. Thus time fled, and one calm evening this week we bade farewell to i ur brief haven of rest and turned yet east- wards. One cannot help realising there is a war out here, though as I write we have not yet come within sound of the guns. A hospital ship, groaning under a heavy load of wounded, flitted past us in a halo of lights as we loft the harbour. A grim grey cruiser sped by, also going in our direction—eastwards—as we gained momentum; and 'way back yonder in the heart of the harbour innumerable trans- port ships are loading their precious burdens of humanity who are ordained to make a stand in the greatest war of all times. And now we are actually in the war-zone. Where? Ah! Somewhere in the Mediterra- nean! The golden sun casts its final rays at the dose of another glorious Eastern day as it embraces the immobile waters of a quiet expanse of green waters, and we are again raising our anchor. The truly characteristic Mediterranean island slopes are disappearing from view. We are steaming away. 'Tis real business this time. Somewhere aft someone or other is bellow- ing the first few bars of the old tune, "On the way to Mandalay," in an unmusical voice, as if to echo, the same strains borne to my ears by the breeze from the bridge above. Hark to the rising and falling inflection of the voices from the bridge in harmony with the strumming of the ship's piano: "0-o-n the w-a-y to Ma-andala-ayl" The transport is ploughing its way speedily through the surf and under the cover of the cloak of falling darkness. Still eastwards. Not a hundred miles away i sthe scene of the fighting, the land of the desparate struggle! Not so far away now, either.
