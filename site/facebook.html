Peerless Jim Driscoll: “his first fight was in a barn in Great House Farm, Llandough”

"I have found it best to work only when you blooming well feel like it. When your bellows or your legs arent right they'll tell you. So, then what's the use of fooling yourself and wasting your time? When I need a bit of rest I take it, and when I feel like working I work. When I rest I build up."
- Jim Driscoll
James Driscoll (15 December 1880 – 30 January 1925), commonly known as Peerless Jim, was a Welsh boxer who learned his trade in the boxing ring and used it to fight his way out of poverty. Driscoll was British featherweight champion and won the coveted Lonsdale belt in 1910. He is a member of the Welsh Sports Hall of Fame, the Ring Magazine Hall of Fame, and the International Boxing Hall of Fame.
Growing up in poverty, Driscoll took employment while still a boy, becoming a printer's devil for the Evening Express in St. Mary Street in Cardiff.
Featherweight champion Abe Attell faced Driscoll in 1910; the Welshman dominated the fight, but with the "no decision" rule in place, without a KO he couldn't take the crown. Driscoll declined a rematch in order to attend an exhibition match in aid of the orphans of St. Nazareth House: "I never break a promise."
Driscoll never forgot his roots; According to the National Monuments Record for Wales, his first fight was in a barn in Great House Farm, Llandough. He was a faithful supporter of his church, remained close to his community, and had great affection for the Nazareth House Orphanage, for whom he once gave up the chance of becoming Featherweight Champion of the World.
Driscoll's boxing career was interrupted by World War I, where he was recruited as a physical training advisor, enlisting in 1914 in the Welsh Horse Yeomanry. In succeeding years, he continued to box despite failing health, relying on his skills to keep him out of trouble.
When he died in Cardiff of consumption at the age of 44, over 100,000 people lined the streets for his funeral. He is buried at Cathays Cemetery in Cardiff, Wales.

A Roman soldier buried in full armour, discovered and reported by our family in 1880; Guglielmo Marconi stayed with us while carrying out his pioneering wireless experiments in 1897; and Peerless Jim Driscoll fought his first bout in our barns.

There has also been little effort to formally investigate the monastery believed to lie beneath the land.
Today there is not even a plaque or information board explaining the historical and archaeological significance of the site. Church View Close now occupies the land with around 20 characterless houses.
For me, this is not just an injustice to my family, but to the history and heritage of the local community and of Wales itself.

Aberpergwm House thread: “the last of the Williams to be born at Great House Farm”

Aberpergwm House history and legends in Glynneath, Neath Port Talbot
I was born in Glynneath and after leaving school, moved to Bristol and then Germany. I haven‘t visited the old haunts for around 30 years. Something I‘m hoping to change next year.
Some stories I know, refer to the Aberpergwm House, now saddly destroyed. The daughter of the Family living there became pregnant by a local man, and died giving birth to a daughter. Her grave is in the form of a wedding cake, half on sacred church grounds and half on an ajoining field.
Origins: Built on the site of an older house called Neuadd Pergwm, it came into the possession of the prominent Williams family around 1560. [1] (https://www.derelictplaces.co.uk/.../aberpergwym-house.../)English Civil War: Legend says Oliver Cromwell spared the estate from destruction because of a supposed family relation. [1] (https://www.derelictplaces.co.uk/.../aberpergwym-house.../)Cultural Significance: The Williams family was notable for remaining faithful to the Welsh language, making the house a historic center for Welsh culture. [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ed3Gl9a6Fh0&t=1), [2] (https://www.derelictplaces.co.uk/.../aberpergwym-house.../)Architecture: The sandstone structure featured a two-story facade with seven bays, later remodelled and expanded in the 18th and 19th centuries. [1] (https://www.thedicamillo.com/house/aberpergwm-house/), [2] (https://aroundus.com/p/12888845-aberpergwm-house), [3] (https://www.derelictplaces.co.uk/.../aberpergwym-house.../)Decline: The estate is a Grade II listed building, but it has fallen into a severe state of ruin and decay over the late 20th century. [1] (https://kids.kiddle.co/Aberpergwm_House), [2] (https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/300011769-aberpergwm...), [3] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberpergwm_House)St. Cadoc's Church: The historic church of St. Cadoc, possibly of late medieval origin, sits directly within the grounds of the house.
