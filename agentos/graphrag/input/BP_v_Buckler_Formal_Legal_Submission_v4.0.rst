1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

1.1  This submission identifies seven independent legal propositions
     demonstrating that the Court of Appeal decision in *BP Properties Ltd
     v Buckler* [1987] EWCA Civ 2 / [1987] 2 EGLR 168 rests upon
     foundations that are legally unsound. Each proposition is derived
     from established common-law principle, the statutory scheme of the
     Limitation Act 1939 (as amended), the Land Registration Act 1925, and
     the inherent requirements of legal certainty.

1.2  The 1987 decision determined a narrow question: whether the
     unilateral letters of October/November 1974 terminated the
     respondent's adverse possession for the purposes of the Limitation
     Act 1939. It did not adjudicate the root of title. It did not
     examine the propriety of the 1982–83 first registration. It did not
     consider whether the farmhouse plot constituted a separate legal
     parcel. And it treated a unilateral, unaccepted, personally
     addressed instrument as capable of altering the statutory character
     of possession by mere receipt.

1.3  The propositions that follow are presented for determination by any
     court or tribunal now called upon to examine the continuing legal
     consequences of the 1987 decision, including applications for
     rectification of the register under the Land Registration Act 2002,
     Schedule 4, and any proceedings in which the 1987 ratio is invoked
     as authority.

2. ESTABLISHED CHRONOLOGY
================================================================================

2.1  The following facts are established from the 1987 judgment itself,
     contemporary court records, and undisputed documentary sources:

     (a) The Williams family occupied Great House Farm, Llandough, from
         at least 1667. The precise legal nature of the original
         transaction remains unresolved.

     (b) In 1876 the Bute Estate severed 33 acres for industrial
         limeworks. Family testimony asserts that a separate arrangement
         with Daniel Thomas & Son transferred title to the farmhouse
         plot. The deeds are missing.

     (c) In February 1916 the Marquess of Bute granted a yearly
         agricultural tenancy to John Williams. No executed copy of this
         tenancy has been located.

     (d) In 1949 Frederick Buckler and Mary Williams (née Williams)
         occupied the farmhouse. No written tenancy agreement was
         executed.

     (e) On 2 February 1955 the agricultural tenancy was terminated by
         notice to quit. On 4 July 1955 a High Court possession order
         was enforced against the farmland BUT NOT against the farmhouse
         and garden. Mrs Williams' objections (following leg amputation
         and hospitalisation) prevented enforcement against the house.

     (f) On 11 December 1962 Cardiff County Court granted a further
         possession order for the farmhouse and garden plus mesne
         profits. The order was not enforced.

     (g) On 3 July 1974 the County Court action was part-heard. Mrs
         Williams pleaded claim of ownership. The defence asserted
         adverse possession under the Limitation Act 1939.

     (h) On 19 September 1974 Judge Watkin Powell granted leave to
         execute the 1962 possession order, ordering that the warrant
         issue forthwith but NOT BE ENFORCED until 31 October 1974.

     (i) On 31 October 1974 the scheduled possession warrant was
         WITHDRAWN. No eviction occurred. Mrs Williams remained in
         physical possession, asserting ownership.

     (j) On 4 November 1974 letters were received by Mrs Williams'
         solicitors from BP Pension Trust Ltd and BP Properties Ltd,
         allegedly sent 31 October 1974. The letters offered a
         unilateral lifetime licence to "Mrs Buckler" to occupy the farm
         until her death, rent-free.

     (k) Mrs Williams never accepted the licence. She continued to
         assert ownership. A 1978 newspaper article records her publicly
         rejecting BP's claim.

     (l) On 14 March 1983 Mary Doreen Williams died at the farmhouse,
         still in occupation. The personal licence expired with her.

     (m) On 31 July 1987 the Court of Appeal (Dillon LJ, Mustill LJ,
         Sir Edward Eveleigh) dismissed the appeal, holding that receipt
         of the 1974 letters terminated adverse possession.

3. PROPOSITION ONE: A LICENCE CANNOT OPERATE RETROSPECTIVELY TO ALTER
   THE LEGAL CHARACTER OF PAST POSSESSION
================================================================================

3.1  Possession is a question of fact. Its legal character on any given
     date is determined by the factual circumstances existing on that
     date. A subsequent event — whether a grant, a contract, or a
     unilateral communication — cannot alter the past. This is not mere
     chronology; it is a fundamental requirement of legal certainty.

3.2  On 31 October 1974 the following facts existed:

     (a) Mrs Williams was in physical possession of the farmhouse and
         garden;
     (b) she asserted that the property was hers, supported by her
         lawyers;
     (c) she had not entered into any consensual arrangement with BP;
     (d) the possession warrant had been withdrawn;
     (e) no eviction had occurred.

     At that moment, her possession was either adverse or it was not.
     If it was adverse, the statutory consequence (extinction of the
     paper title after 12 years) had already matured or was in the
     process of maturing. A document communicated on 4 November 1974
     could not retrospectively convert possession which was already
     adverse on 31 October into possession which had always been
     permissive.

3.3  The Limitation Act 1939 s.10 (as amended) provided that no action
     could be brought to recover land after the expiration of 12 years
     from the date on which the right to recover accrued. The right
     accrued when the owner was dispossessed or discontinued possession.
     The statute looks to the fact of dispossession and the continuity
     of adverse possession. It does not permit a paper owner to
     "reset" the limitation clock by unilateral correspondence after
     the statutory period has run or is about to run.

3.4  The Court of Appeal treated the licence as operating from receipt
     on 4 November 1974. That is a prospective operation. But the
     court's reasoning necessarily assumes that the licence could
     interrupt the limitation period even though the adverse possession
     had already existed for the requisite period (or was about to
     complete it). That assumption conflates prospective regulation of
     future possession with retrospective interruption of a statutory
     time-bar.

3.5  The principle was stated in *Lloyd v Spillet* (1741) 2 Atk 148:
     limitation is not interrupted by mere assertion of title or by
     formal entry without actual dispossession. Section 13 of the
     Limitation Act 1939 expressly provided that "no person shall be
     deemed to have been in possession of any land by reason only of
     having made a formal entry thereon, and no continual or other
     claim upon or near any land shall preserve any right of action to
     recover the land." The 1974 letters were precisely such a
     "continual claim" — an assertion of paper title without actual
     recovery of possession. By the express terms of the statute, such
     a claim could not preserve the right of action.

3.6  The Court of Appeal's reasoning therefore conflicts with the
     statutory policy of the Limitation Act 1939. Dillon LJ himself
     described the proposition as "a new one" and acknowledged that it
     might appear inconsistent with the policy of the limitation
     legislation. A novel doctrine that undermines the statutory
     purpose of providing certainty after 12 years of adverse
     possession requires the clearest justification. None was
     identified.

4. PROPOSITION TWO: A UNILATERAL OFFER OF LICENCE, NOT ACCEPTED,
   CREATES NO BINDING LEGAL RELATIONSHIP
================================================================================

4.1  A licence is a permission. Like any grant of a legal interest or
     authority, it requires acceptance by the person to whom it is
     offered. An offer that is rejected, or that is met with a
     counter-assertion of ownership, creates no licence. The law does
     not permit a paper owner to impose a licence upon an unwilling
     occupier by mere unilateral assertion.

4.2  The facts establish that Mrs Williams never accepted the 1974
     letters. She never signed any document acknowledging the licence.
     She never paid rent. She never conducted herself in a manner
     consistent with acceptance of BP's permission. On the contrary:

     (a) In 1974 she stated publicly: "It's my land. My lawyers say so."
     (b) In 1978 a newspaper article recorded her publicly claiming
         ownership and rejecting BP's asserted claim.
     (c) Throughout the litigation she maintained that the property
         belonged to her through her grandfather.

     These are not the acts of a licensee. They are the acts of a
     person who expressly repudiates the grantor's title and asserts
     her own.

4.3  The Court of Appeal held that "whether she liked it or not" her
     possession became referable to a licence from the moment of
     receipt. That holding dispenses with the requirement of
     acceptance. It treats a unilateral communication as capable of
     altering the legal character of possession without the occupier's
     assent. That is contrary to the fundamental principle that a
     licence, like any other grant, requires a meeting of minds or at
     least conduct consistent with acceptance.

4.4  In *Rains v Buxton* (1880) 14 Ch D 537, the court held that a
     licence must be accepted to be binding. An offer of permission
     that is not accepted creates no legal obligation on the part of
     the person to whom it is addressed. The 1987 decision cannot
     override this principle without identifying a statutory or common-
     law authority for imposing a licence by mere receipt.

4.5  The Limitation Act 1939 was enacted to provide certainty after
     prolonged adverse possession. If a paper owner could defeat that
     certainty by sending a letter that the occupier never accepted,
     the statute would be rendered nugatory. Any owner could interrupt
     limitation at any time by unilateral correspondence, without
     recovering possession and without the occupier's consent. That
     cannot be the law.

5. PROPOSITION THREE: THE INSTRUMENT WAS DEFECTIVE — ADDRESSED TO THE
   WRONG PERSON AND DISCONNECTING THE OCCUPIER FROM HER HEREDITARY TITLE
================================================================================

5.1  The 1974 letters were addressed to "Mrs Buckler." The principal
     occupier was Mary Doreen Williams. She had married Frederick
     Buckler but retained her maiden name in daily life and in all
     dealings with the estate. She was the daughter of John Williams
     and the granddaughter of the John Williams who had occupied the
     farm since at least the late nineteenth century. Her asserted
     title derived through the Williams line.

5.2  By addressing the licence to "Mrs Buckler" rather than "Mary
     Williams" or "Mrs Mary Williams," BP created an instrument that
     did not correspond to the legal identity of the person in
     occupation. The grant was made to a name that the occupier did
     not use and by which she was not known in her property dealings.
     This is not a mere clerical error. It goes to the validity of the
     grant itself.

5.3  A grant must identify the grantee with reasonable certainty. If
     the grant is addressed to a person who does not exist (in the
     sense that no person answering to that description was the
     occupier or claimed title), the grant is void for uncertainty or
     at least voidable. There was no "Mrs Buckler" in occupation of
     Great House Farm in 1974. There was Mary Williams, who happened
     to be married to a man named Buckler but who had never adopted
     his surname.

5.4  The tactical effect of the naming was insidious. By addressing
     the licence to "Mrs Buckler," BP sought to bind the interest of
     Frederick Buckler, who had died in 1965. His interest, if any,
     had expired with him. By using his surname, BP attempted to
     create a legal relationship with a deceased person and to impose
     that relationship upon his widow, thereby disconnecting her from
     the Williams lineage through which she claimed her title.

5.5  The Court of Appeal treated the letters as valid and effective
     despite this defect. The judgment records: "Mrs Mary Buckler, who
     was also from time to time referred to as Mrs Williams..." That
     formulation inverts reality. Her identity was Williams. The
     occasional reference to her as Mrs Buckler was the exception, not
     the rule. The court's adoption of BP's nomenclature perpetuated
     the identity erasure and treated a defective instrument as
     legally effective.

6. PROPOSITION FOUR: THE COURT DETERMINED POSSESSION ONLY; IT DID NOT
   ADJUDICATE TITLE
================================================================================

6.1  The 1987 decision was a determination of adverse possession under
     the Limitation Act 1939. It was not a comprehensive trial of the
     root of title. The Court of Appeal did not examine:

     (a) the 1667 transaction and whether it created a virtual freehold;
     (b) the 1876 severance and the Daniel Thomas arrangement;
     (c) whether the farmhouse plot was a separate legal parcel;
     (d) the chain of conveyances from the Bute Estate to Western
         Ground Rents to BP;
     (e) whether any of those conveyances effectively transferred the
         farmhouse plot or only the surrounding agricultural land.

6.2  The court's finding that "no such documents were, however, ever
     produced" is an evidential conclusion, not an affirmative
     determination that no proprietary interest ever existed. It means
     only that Mrs Williams failed to prove her title on the evidence
     then available. It does not establish that BP's title was
     indefeasible or that the Williams family held no independent
     proprietary interest.

6.3  The distinction is critical for res judicata and issue estoppel.
     A decision on possession does not preclude a subsequent
     determination of title. The 1987 judgment should be confined to
     the legal consequences the Court attributed to the evidence then
     before it. It cannot be invoked as authority for the proposition
     that BP holds absolute title free of any competing historical
     claim.

6.4  The Court itself recognised this limitation. It noted that if Mrs
     Williams had already acquired a title by adverse possession
     before the 1974 letters, that title would have constituted an
     overriding interest. The court did not find that no such title
     existed; it found only that the 1974 letters prevented the
     completion of the adverse possession period. That is a
     procedural finding, not a determination of the underlying
     proprietary position.

7. PROPOSITION FIVE: THE FIRST REGISTRATION WAS SUBJECT TO THE FAMILY'S
   OVERRIDING INTEREST
================================================================================

7.1  Titles WA231076 and WA240304 were first registered in 1982–83.
     At that time Mrs Williams was in actual occupation of the
     farmhouse and garden. She was asserting ownership. She had not
     been evicted. Her possession was continuous, open, and adverse to
     BP's paper title.

7.2  Under the Land Registration Act 1925 s.70(1)(g), the interest of
     a person in actual occupation was an overriding interest binding
     on the registered proprietor. Mrs Williams' actual occupation,
     coupled with her assertion of title, gave her an interest that
     was capable of overriding the first registration. The first
     registration was therefore subject to her interest, whether or
     not the Land Registry examiner noted it on the register.

7.3  The 1987 Court of Appeal treated the first registration as a
     given and as conclusive against the family. It did not examine
     whether the registration was subject to Mrs Williams' overriding
     interest. It did not consider whether the examiner had
     discharged the duty to inquire into actual occupation. It
     treated the register as immutable when the statute itself
     provided that registration was subject to overriding interests.

7.4  The surviving first-registration bundle (confirmed by HM Land
     Registry letter 30 July 2026, ref. N610CGV) now permits
     examination of what was disclosed, what the examiner
     considered, and whether any competing claim or actual occupation
     was properly addressed. If the examiner failed to consider the
     1974 County Court proceedings, the defence asserting adverse
     possession, and Mrs Williams' continuing actual occupation, the
     registration was procured without due regard to the statutory
     requirements.

7.5  The Land Registration Act 2002, Schedule 4, provides for
     rectification of the register to correct a mistake. A failure to
     give effect to an overriding interest at first registration is a
     mistake capable of rectification. The 1987 decision cannot
     preclude rectification of a registration error that the Court of
     Appeal never examined.

8. PROPOSITION SIX: THE LICENCE EXPIRED WITH MRS WILLIAMS' DEATH AND
   COULD NOT BIND HER SUCCESSORS
================================================================================

8.1  The 1974 licence was personal to Mary Williams. It was expressed
     to be a licence for her to occupy the farm until her death. It
     was non-heritable. It created no estate capable of passing to
     her heirs or personal representatives. It was not a lease. It
     was not an assignment. It was a personal permission that expired
     with the person to whom it was granted.

8.2  Mary Doreen Williams died on 14 March 1983. The licence
     therefore expired on that date. By the time of the 1987 Court of
     Appeal decision, the licence had been dead for over four years.

8.3  The 1987 decision nevertheless treated the licence as
     determinative of the character of possession, not only for Mrs
     Williams but for her successors. The court's reasoning was
     applied to defeat the adverse possession claim of William
     "Billy" Buckler, who occupied the farmhouse after his mother's
     death and who had no legal relationship with BP whatsoever.

8.4  This is a fundamental legal error. A personal licence cannot
     bind successors in title or occupation. It cannot be invoked
     against a person who was not the grantee and who never received
     the permission. To hold that a licence granted to a deceased
     person could defeat the possessory claim of her living son is
     to treat a personal, non-heritable interest as if it were a
     proprietary burden running with the land.

8.5  The Court of Appeal's reasoning therefore rests upon a "ghost
     licence" — a permission granted to a person who was no longer
     alive, applied to defeat the claims of persons who were never
     parties to it. That is not a valid legal foundation for
     depriving a family of their home.

9. PROPOSITION SEVEN: THE FARMHOUSE PLOT WAS TREATED AS A SEPARATE
   LEGAL INTEREST THROUGHOUT THE LITIGATION
================================================================================

9.1  The documentary record reveals a consistent pattern: the
     farmhouse, garden and curtilage were treated differently from
     the surrounding agricultural land at every stage of the
     litigation. This pattern is inconsistent with the proposition
     that the entire holding was subject to a single leasehold
     interest.

9.2  The 1955 Partial Enforcement: The High Court possession order
     was enforced against the farmland on 4 July 1955 BUT NOT
     against the farmhouse and garden. If the order covered the
     "whole farm," the farmhouse should have been recovered at the
     same time. It was not. The partial enforcement suggests that
     the farmhouse occupied a different legal position.

9.3  The 1962 Fresh Proceedings: Western Ground Rents brought a
     separate County Court action specifically for possession of the
     farmhouse and garden. If the 1955 order had already covered
     the farmhouse, no fresh proceedings would have been necessary.
     The need for a second action suggests that the 1955 order did
     not extend to the farmhouse plot, or that the farmhouse plot
     was recognised as a distinct legal interest.

9.4  The 1974 Unilateral Licence: BP did not simply enforce the
     existing possession order. It issued a personal lifetime
     licence. That is not the act of a proprietor who believes its
     title to the farmhouse is unchallengeable. It is the act of a
     party who recognises that the occupation is not
     straightforwardly terminable and who seeks to regularise it by
     consent rather than by force.

9.5  The 1987 decision conflated these distinct treatments. It
     treated the farmhouse as part of a single holding subject to
     the same adverse possession analysis as the surrounding land.
     It did not examine whether the farmhouse plot had been
     severed from the agricultural holding by the 1876 carve-out,
     by the 1955 partial enforcement, or by any other mechanism.

9.6  If the farmhouse plot was a separate legal parcel — whether by
     express conveyance, by implication, or by recognition of an
     underlying hereditary interest — then the possession
     proceedings from 1955 onwards related only to the leased
     farmland and not to the Williams family's own plot. The 1987
     decision did not examine this possibility and cannot be
     treated as having finally determined it.

================================================================================
10. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

10.1 The 1987 decision in *BP Properties Ltd v Buckler* is not a
     monument of settled law. It is a possession ruling that
     resolved a narrow technical question by means of reasoning
     that conflicts with:

     (a) the fundamental principle that a licence requires acceptance;
     (b) the statutory policy of the Limitation Act 1939;
     (c) the requirement that a grant identify the grantee with
         reasonable certainty;
     (d) the distinction between possession and title;
     (e) the statutory protection of overriding interests under the
         Land Registration Act 1925;
     (f) the principle that a personal licence expires with the
         grantee and cannot bind successors; and
     (g) the documentary evidence that the farmhouse plot was
         treated as a separate legal interest throughout the
         litigation.

10.2 The decision should be confined to the narrow question it
     actually decided. It cannot be invoked as authority for the
     proposition that BP holds indefeasible title to land that was
     occupied by a family for more than three centuries, that was
     the subject of a disputed first registration, and that may
     constitute a separate legal parcel never adjudicated in the
     1987 proceedings.

10.3 The proper course is to examine the surviving primary evidence
     — most immediately the Land Registry first-registration bundle
     for titles WA231076 and WA240304 — and to determine, on the
     merits, the legal status of the farmhouse plot and the
     validity of the instruments upon which the 1987 decision
     rested.
