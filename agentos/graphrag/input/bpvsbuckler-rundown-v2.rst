Executive Summary
=================

This document reconstructs the history of **Great House Farm** (Ty Mawr),
Llandough, Vale of Glamorgan, from its medieval origins through to its
demolition in 1988 and the subsequent residential development of the site
(now Church View Close).  It traces competing claims of ownership between
the **Williams–Buckler family** (who assert continuous occupation from at
least 1667) and the **Bute Estate**, **Western Ground Rents Ltd**, **BP
Pension Trust Ltd** and **BP Properties Ltd**.

The reconstruction draws on:

*   **Established Fact:** Court judgments, FOI responses, archaeological
    reports, Land Registry records, Hansard, official archive catalogues;
*   **Documentary Evidence:** Newspapers, plans, photographs, correspondence,
    estate records;
*   **Family Testimony:** Oral history, family recollections, unpublished
    family records;
*   **Working Hypothesis:** Interpretations, legal theories, archaeological
    interpretations where documentary proof is absent.

Throughout this document, these four categories are distinguished clearly.
Where documentary proof is absent, theories are identified as hypotheses and
are not presented as established fact.

Key sources include:

*   Bute Estate manorial records (NLW D 219, D160/1–4, D153/4–8, DB/E/1–2);
*   Glamorgan Archives holdings (DSA/6/701, DSA/6/1158, DCOWHS/2, DXJF/64/30–31);
*   National Library of Wales (NLW) collections;
*   Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales (RCAHMW)
    records — **14 archive collections** relating to Great House Farm have been
    confirmed;
*   Cotswold Archaeological Trust (now Cotswold Archaeology) excavation reports
    (1994) and earlier GGAT evaluations (1978–1980);
*   Contemporary newspaper coverage (*South Wales Echo*, *Western Mail*);
*   Parliamentary records (Hansard);
*   Court records including *BP Properties Ltd v Buckler* [1987] EWCA Civ 2;
*   European Court of Human Rights application **14464/88**;
*   Family testimony and genealogical research;
*   **LiDAR** survey evidence;
*   Alternative historic place-name forms: **Llandochau** and
    **Llan Doche Penarth**.

A central contention of this reconstruction is that the 1987 Court of Appeal
decision resolved only **possession proceedings** and the effect of a
unilateral licence on adverse possession; it did **not** provide a final
judicial determination of the competing historical title claims.  The
distinction between possession proceedings and title determination has become
much clearer through reconstruction of the litigation chronology.

Historical Background
=====================

Medieval Origins (1100–1539)
----------------------------

The lordship was originally granted to the **Walsche** family by the Norman
conqueror **Robert fitz Hamon** (or his successors) around **1100**.
**Great House ("Ty Mawr")** was built next to **St Dochdwy's Church**,
Llandough, in **1215**.  The property was also known by the alternative
historic names **Llandochau** and **Llan Doche Penarth** (see Appendix G).

From 1215 to 1539 the estate was owned by **Tewkesbury Abbey** / the Prior of
Cardiff as an ecclesiastical title.  The great church of Llanoughby was a
monastic institution.  In its decline, however, it presided over a large
community of Christian life and was an important missioning centre in early
Christianity in Morganwg.  The greater portion continued until the Norman
conquest of Glamorgan, when the monastery's property was vested by the Lord of
Glamorgan in the Benedictine Abbey of Tewkesbury.  The benefactions of the
Church of the Parish of Llandough, the revenues being regularly farmed,
remained appropriated to Tewkesbury until the Dissolution of the monasteries
in 1536–39.

In **1444**, the manor and lordship of Llandough (and St Mary Church) were
sold to **Sir William Thomas (Herbert) of Raglan**, ancestor of the Earls of
Pembroke.  The lordship remained in high-status hands for a century.

The Dissolution and After (1536–1770)
-------------------------------------

In **1536**, **Sir Edward Carne** (a lawyer and diplomat) purchased the manor
from the 2nd Earl of Worcester, a descendant of the Herberts.  Between
**1536 and 1539** the Crown (Henry VIII) confiscated the estate during the
Dissolution.  After the Dissolution, the manor of Llandough passed through
Crown grants to lay owners; by the later 16th century it formed part of the
Herbert/Pembroke orbit and was later absorbed into what became the **Bute
estate**.

This matches the Bute Estate Records description (many Glamorgan manors,
including Llandough, in Bute hands by the 18th–19th centuries).

From **1552 to 1829**, the Bute/Pembroke estate treated itself as the
superior landholder/manorial lord, and the people on those farms were tenants
or lessees, not the estate owners.  The "Llandough manorial leases and
agreements" series (NLW D 219, Bute Estate Records) explicitly identified
as Bute estate material covers this period and shows leases of Llandough Farm
(about 166 acres) and other Llandough properties.

The Williams Family and the 1667 Acquisition
--------------------------------------------

From at least **1667** the **Williams family** are recorded in local memory
and later press reports as occupying Great House Farm.  A 1980s newspaper
article quoted Mrs Mary Williams saying the farm was "acquired" by the
Williams family at that time, which she understood as a purchase rather than
a tenancy.  There is no surviving registered title from the 17th century.

At this time (1667), the records are part of the Herbert/Pembroke orbit,
specifically the Herbert family of Cogan Pill (correspondence 1667).  The
National Library of Wales (NLW D 219) categorises these records strictly as
"Manorial."  The 1667 acquisition is likely in the un-indexed portions of
the Corbett or Bute manorial records.

The core of the legal mystery lies in how the transfer was recorded:

**Estate Perception:**  The Bute Estate Records (Series D 219) treat the
family as part of a continuous sequence of manorial leases.  *(Documentary
Evidence)*

**Williams Perception:**  The 1667 transaction likely involved a "lease for
lives" (often three generations) or copyhold of inheritance.  These were
"hereditary" in practice, passing from father to son, which supports the
claim of 300+ years of continuous family standing.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

**The "Erasure":**  Because the Court Baron records were the private property
of the Lord (the Bute family), they could "re-characterise" an ancient
hereditary tenure as a simple revocable lease when the manorial system was
being dismantled in the 19th and 20th centuries.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

A "Court House" was where the Lord's steward held the manor courts.  If the
family occupied the Court House, they were likely acting as **Stewards or
Hereditary Bailiffs** for the Herberts — a "gentry standing" significantly
higher than a standard tenant farmer.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

If a 1667 conveyance could be proved from estate archives, it would shift the
legal framing from mere occupation to ownership; at present, that remains a
key point for further research rather than an established fact.  *(Working
Hypothesis)*

In the 17th century, a fee farm grant or a 999-year lease was often perceived
and treated by the family as a "purchase" (absolute ownership), while the
superior Lord (Herbert/Bute) maintained it as a "lease" in their ledger to
preserve manorial rights.  If the 1667 deed was a conveyance of the Equitable
Title, the Bute records would still show the "Superior Title" (the
Lordship), effectively creating two parallel titles.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

In 1667, a Williams ancestor would not "buy" the land in the modern
freehold sense; instead, they would be "admitted" to the tenure in the Court
Baron.  If the family paid a large "fine" (entry fee), this was often
remembered in family lore as a "purchase" of the land.  *(Working
Hypothesis)*

Later Estate Transfers (1677–1824)
----------------------------------

*   **1677** — Title passed through marriage into the **Talbot family**
    (Penrice and Margam estate).  *(Documentary Evidence)*
*   **1766** — Part of the **Earl of Plymouth's estates** (DPL/1).
    *(Documentary Evidence)*
*   **1770–1785** — **Valentine Morris** (part of the Piercefield/Glamorgan
    estate interests).  *(Documentary Evidence)*
*   **c. 1770–1820** — Estate-level transfers (Morris -> Sir Mark Wood ->
    Bute).  In this period, the wider Llandough and Glamorgan estate that
    included Great House Farm moved through several elite owners before
    consolidation in the Bute estate.  Valentine Morris and later Sir Mark
    Wood held substantial property interests in the area.  An 1820 memorandum
    of exchanges of land between the Marquess of Bute and Lord Plymouth in
    the parish of Llandough, and the subsequent survey of the Marquess of
    Bute's Glamorgan estates by **David Stewart** (Glamorgan Archives ref.
    DB/E), show Bute taking control of farms including "Great House Farm
    alias Cedfin" as part of a rationalisation of their Glamorgan holdings.
    These records prove the estate-side chain (Morris -> Wood -> Bute) for the
    Llandough block as a whole; they do not yet, on their own, show exactly
    when or how legal title to Great House Farm moved into Williams hands,
    which is why the missing Bute/Thomas/Williams conveyances are so critical.
    *(Documentary Evidence)*
*   **1817–1828** — Historical "Cardiff Records" confirm **Lambert Williams**
    was a high-status Ward Constable in Cardiff.  File D160/1–4 records an
    agreement between Lambert Williams of Cardiff and the Bute Estate
    (1818–1835).  This demonstrates that a Williams was a recognised legal
    entity dealing with the Bute Estate on a high-status contractual basis.
    *(Documentary Evidence)*
*   **1818** — Chief rents of Llandough were transferred to the manorial
    rental.  *(Documentary Evidence)*
*   **1820** — Memorandum of exchanges of land between the Marquis of Bute
    and Lord Plymouth in the parish of Llandough.  Llandough and Cogan appear
    in the rentals from 1821 onwards.  *(Documentary Evidence)*
*   **1824** — **Great House Farm alias "Cedfin."**  Survey of Marquess of
    Bute's Glamorgan estates by David Stewart (Glamorgan Archives ref.
    DB/E/1–2).  Tithes of Llandough (along with Leckwith and Cogan) were
    purchased.  Glamorgan Estate rentals (R 1, Sub-sub-fonds 1728–1893, part
    of Bute Estate Records).  *(Documentary Evidence)*

The Administrative Reclassification (1840s–1890s)
-------------------------------------------------

Historically, the Court House served as the administrative centre for
manorial business (Court Barons).  A "Court House" implies a physical site
of Incorporeal Hereditaments (manorial rights).  In the mid-19th century,
census and tithe records began systematically shifting terminology.  For
example, similar properties in the Vale transitioned from "Great House"
(Ty Mawr) to "Great House Farm" (Ty Mawr Farm) in the 1881 census.  This
linguistic shift may reflect an administrative reclassification from a
manorial residence to an agricultural holding.

Estate records show the terminology gradually changed from "Great House" to
"Great House Farm."  One possible interpretation is that this reflected an
administrative reclassification from a manorial residence to an agricultural
holding.  By recording it as a "Grange" or "Farm" in the Bute Estate
Records (NLW D 219), the estate may have replaced a proprietary history with
a tenant-farmer narrative.  *(Documentary Evidence and Working Hypothesis)*

By the 1880s–1890s, the rebranding was complete.  By the time Marconi visited
in 1897, the house was firmly referred to as Great House Farm in press
reports.  In surrounding areas like Grangetown, the Bute Estate was actively
rebranding old manorial lands for industrial use, which may have influenced
the local renaming of high-status farms to "Granges" to suggest they were
historical monastic outbuildings rather than private manorial seats.
*(Documentary Evidence and Working Hypothesis)*

The Williams–Buckler Family Occupation
======================================

The 1876 Carve-Out (D153/4–8)
------------------------------

The record explicitly mentions "land (33a.), formerly part of Great House
Farm, for the Llandough Limeworks (33a.), 1876–1912."

In 1876, the Bute Estate recorded the severance of 33 acres formerly part of
Great House Farm for industrial limestone quarrying.  This moved the property
from manorial/agricultural tenure into the industrial/commercial category.
*(Documentary Evidence)*

The Daniel Thomas Arrangement (c. 1895–1905)
-------------------------------------------

Family testimony states that **Daniel Thomas & Son** made an agreement with
the Williams family whereby quarrying was allowed in return for eventual
title.  The key deeds referred to are now missing.  A tree-planting ceremony
marks this transition — strong evidence of customary transfer belief at the
time, though not proof of legal title.  *(Family Testimony and Working
Hypothesis)*

In the late 19th/early 20th century, the Bute Estate sold off many
"superior" interests to consolidate funds for Cardiff.  If Thomas acted as
an intermediary, there should be a **Conveyance of the Freehold Reversion**
or an **Enfranchisement Deed** (converting manorial tenure to freehold).
*(Working Hypothesis)*

Local trade also prospered and surplus produce of the Vale farmsteads and
limestone, which was considered the best for hardening under water, were
shipped to Bristol, Minehead, Watchet, and other places across the Bristol
Channel.  It was limestone from Aberthaw which was used in the construction
of the docks at Sebastopol, and Smeaton used it when building the Eddystone
… [record incomplete].

The 1916 Tenancy
----------------

In **February 1916**, the Marquess of Bute granted a yearly agricultural
tenancy of Great House Farm to **Mr John Williams** (Mrs Mary Williams'
maternal grandfather).  This is one of the few solid documentary points where
the estate's view is clear: on paper, Bute was still treated as the
freehold owner, and John Williams was recorded as tenant.  That sits
uneasily with the family's longstanding belief that they already "owned" the
farm, but it does not rule out earlier informal or unrecorded
understandings; large estates sometimes regularised long-standing occupiers
by putting them on formal tenancies, even where those occupiers believed
they had stronger rights.  *(Documentary Evidence and Family Testimony)*

Legally, **1916 marks a sharp divergence** between lived reality and estate
paperwork: the Williams line saw themselves as inheritors of an older title,
while the estate's documents framed them as agricultural tenants of Bute.
From the family's perspective, this tenancy may have been experienced as
bureaucratic tidying-up of a relationship they already regarded as ownership,
rather than as a fresh grant that erased earlier rights.  *(Working
Hypothesis)*

The 1924 Estate Sale and 1938 Auction
-------------------------------------

The fact that "Great House" appears in the **Llandough Estate sale of 1924**
(DSA/6/701) suggests the Bute/Byass estates were systematically packaging
manorial lands.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

In **1938**, Great House Farm was advertised as "sold" at auction by Herbert
R. Thomas (Bute Estate / Mountjoy Ltd or its nominee/local estate agent and
chartered auctioneer).  This transaction related to the **freehold reversion**
— a paper title — and did not result in the displacement of the Williams
family, who remained in physical occupation.  The later history of rent
arrears, possession proceedings, and adverse possession claims
demonstrates that the 1938 sale did not convey vacant possession, but
instead marked the beginning of a long divergence between paper ownership
and living occupation.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

The reversion was conveyed to **Western Ground Rents Ltd**.  *(Documentary
Evidence)*

The Buckler Family Genealogy
============================

The Genealogical Breakdown
--------------------------

1.  **The Buckleriana Dynasty (The "Main" Line)**

    This family, detailed in **Charles Alban Buckler's 1886 book
    *Buckleriana***, begins with **John Buckler (d. 1540)** of Causeway,
    Radipole, Dorset.

    **Key Descendants:**  This line produced the 17th-century poet
    **Edward Buckler** (author of "Thoughts of Death") and the 19th-century
    architectural "dynasty" of John Buckler, John Chessell Buckler, and
    Charles Alban Buckler.

    **Geographic Focus:**  Dorset, Somerset (Boreham/West Kirby), and later
    London and Oxfordshire.

2.  **Frederick Buckler and the Glamorgan Branch**

    **Frederick Buckler (1888–1965)**, the farmer at Great House Farm,
    Llandough, was part of a distinct Glamorgan-based branch of the family.

    **Lineage:**  Frederick was the son of **Samuel Buckler** (a market
    gardener) and the grandson of **George Buckler**.

    **The Link to the Dynasty:**  Records suggest the South Wales Bucklers
    migrated from the Frome/Somerset and Dorset borders in the 19th century.
    In the *Buckleriana* records, multiple "younger sons" from the Radipole
    and Boreham branches are noted as having moved into surrounding counties
    (Somerset/Hampshire) to establish merchant and farming lines.

    **Common Ancestry:**  Both Frederick and the "Buckleriana" architects
    share a common ancestor in the Bucklers of Dorset from the 16th century.
    Frederick likely descends from one of the "cadet" (younger) branches of
    the family that moved to Somerset before migrating to the industrialising
    Cardiff/Glamorgan area in the mid-1800s.

**The "BP Properties" Case Connection:**  While the "Buckleriana"
architects became famous in London, their cousins — including Frederick
Buckler's direct ancestors — remained in the West Country as farmers and
tradesmen before moving to South Wales.

Frederick Buckler's appearance as a newcomer who married into the family
and potentially subverted their claim would be consistent with "estate
management" tactics of that era.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

The Legal Dispute (1955–1988)
=============================

Western Ground Rents Ltd (1955–1969)
------------------------------------

**1955 — High Court Possession Proceedings**

Western Ground Rents Ltd brought possession proceedings in the High Court
under Order 14 and obtained a possession order for the whole farm.  On
**4 July 1955**, the possession order was enforced for the farm **except**
the farmhouse and garden due to Mrs Mary Williams's strong objections
following her leg amputation and hospitalisation.  The Williams' occupation
of the farmhouse and garden remained undisturbed.  *(Established Fact:
court records)*

**1959 — Mrs Williams's Belief in Title**

Mrs Williams believed she had title to the property through her grandfather
— produced no documents (as the title was allegedly stolen from the public
library and the copy stolen from the loft of the farmhouse).  She was
offered a tenancy of the farmhouse and garden by Western Ground Rents but
refused to accept it.  She was also later offered, and refused, alternative
accommodation.  *(Documentary Evidence and Family Testimony)*

The advisers of Western Ground Rents seem during these years to have
supposed that, if they attended at the farmhouse from time to time and
asserted Western Ground Rents' title, that was sufficient to prevent time
running against Western Ground Rents under the Limitation Act 1939.  Plainly,
however, that notion was erroneous, as **section 13** of the Act provides
that "no person shall be deemed to have been in possession of any land by
reason only of having made a formal entry thereon, and no continual or other
claim upon or near any land shall preserve any right of action to recover the
land."  *(Established Fact: statute)*

**1961 — Hansard Evidence**

Parliamentary records (Hansard) from July 1961 reveal that Western Ground
Rents was a notorious entity in South Wales, accused by MPs of "callous
behaviour," "machinations," and "arrogantly ignoring communications" from
long-term residents.  *(Established Fact: Hansard)*

**1962 — Rent Arrears / Possession Proceedings**

Western Ground Rents brought a fresh action in Cardiff County Court against
Mr Frederick Buckler and Mrs Buckler (sued as Mrs Williams) for possession
of the farmhouse and garden and mesne profits since 1955.  Western Ground
Rents obtained a possession order, but it was never enforced against the
occupying family.  On **11 December 1962**, Judge Temple Morris QC made a
possession order together with an order for payment of mesne profits.
*(Established Fact: court records)*

**1964–1965 — End of Agricultural Tenancy and Failed Negotiations**

October 1964: The agricultural tenancy of Great House Farm (held by the
Williams family) officially came to an end.  Mr Frederick Buckler and his
wife remained in the farmhouse without a formal legal tenancy.
*(Documentary Evidence)*

Jan/Feb 1965: Negotiations took place between Western Ground Rents and the
Bucklers.  Mr Buckler expressed frustration that the company would not let him
the agricultural land, as he wished to continue farming.
*(Documentary Evidence)*

2 March 1965: Western Ground Rents' agents sent a formal offer to Mrs
Williams for a weekly tenancy of the farmhouse and garden at a rent of £2.
*(Documentary Evidence)*

Late March 1965: Mrs Williams and Mr Frederick Buckler did not sign or
return the tenancy agreement.  However, they remained in the house, and
because they did not pay rent, their occupation began to be treated as
"adverse" by the owners.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

**December 1965 — Death of Frederick Buckler**

Mr Frederick Buckler died of heart-related issues (coronary thrombosis),
often cited as a coronary thrombosis.  His death occurred only a few months
after the failed 1965 tenancy negotiations.  After the death of Mr Frederick
Buckler, Mrs Williams remained in possession of the farmhouse and garden,
with various of her children living there with her, including Mr Buckler
junior.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

Western Ground Rents kept the matter in the County Court (a lower court)
rather than seeking a declaration of title in the High Court.  County courts
are designed for possession orders (evictions), not for complex,
centuries-old title disputes involving ancestral rights and Bute Estate
covenants.  By keeping it in the County Court, the proceedings avoided a
full examination of the 300-year history of the Williams occupation.
*(Working Hypothesis)*

**1966 — "Inhuman and Unjust"**

In a 1966 Parliamentary debate, Western Ground Rents was described as "one
of the most inhuman and unjust" landlords, with MPs stating they were
"draining the lifeblood out of Wales."  *(Established Fact: Hansard)*

**1969 — "The Worst in Wales"**

Explicitly labelled "The Worst in Wales"; "…about the worst ground landlords
in Wales" in the House of Commons.  *(Established Fact: Hansard)*

BP Pension Trust Ltd / BP Properties Ltd (1969–1988)
---------------------------------------------------

**December 1969 — Sale to BP**

**BP Pension Trust Ltd** inherits the interest in Great House Farm,
including the farmhouse and garden, after it was sold by Western Ground
Rents.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

**1970 — Llandough Primary School Opens**

Signalling residential growth near the farm site.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

**1974 — The Unilateral Licence**

Important copies of title deeds and evidence reportedly went missing by this
time, impacting the family's ability to prove ownership.  *(Family Testimony)*

On **3 July 1974**, Mary Williams pleaded claim of ownership in court.
This was part-heard and a formal defence was filed asserting that BP Pension
Trust's claim was barred by adverse possession under the Limitation
Act 1939.  So far as Mr Buckler junior was concerned, his plea was that he
occupied as licensee of Mrs Williams.  That action has never been finally
heard.  The court adjourned without ruling on title, focusing only on
possession.  *(Established Fact: court records)*

A petition signed by **1,700** local residents circulated for preservation
of Great House Farm from demolition and housing development, after BP
Pension Trust Ltd obtained planning permission from the former Glamorgan
County Council.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

On **17 September 1974**, BP Pension Trust Ltd gave notice of an application
for leave to enforce the possession order of 11 December 1962.
*(Established Fact: court records)*

On **19 September 1974**, Judge Watkin Powell gave BP Pension Trust Ltd leave
to execute the order for possession from 11 December 1962.  He ordered that
the possession warrant do issue forthwith, but be **not enforced until
31 October 1974**.  *(Established Fact: court records)*

On **31 October 1974**, a scheduled possession enforcement warrant was
withdrawn.  Withdrawal prevented immediate eviction, allowing Mary to remain
until her death in 1983, but it preserved BP's title by avoiding a full
ownership trial.  *(Established Fact: court records)*

On **4 November 1974**, letters were received by Mrs Williams' solicitor from
BP Pension Trust Ltd and BP Properties Ltd, allegedly sent 31st October.
These constituted a **unilateral offer of a lifetime licence** to "Mrs Buckler"
to occupy the farm until her death.  The interest in a licence expressed by
Mr Frederick Buckler before his death in late 1965 died with him.  The licence
was alleged to be accepted by her silence, as no expressed rejection or
acceptance occurred.  *(Established Fact: court records)*

Evidence exists that Mrs Williams was not silent and expressly rejected the
offer — the 1978 newspaper article reports her publicly claiming ownership and
rejecting BP's asserted claim.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

**1978–1980 — GGAT Excavations**

The Glamorgan-Gwent Archaeological Trust (GGAT) conducted excavations in
1978–1980.  The earliest structure found was a possible late Iron-age
roundhouse, perhaps part of a farmstead.  In the 2nd century A.D. a Roman
villa was constructed, which developed considerably in the early 3rd century
and was occupied until the early 4th century.  Some walls of the villa were
reused as foundations for a 12th- or 13th-century barn.  *(Documentary
Evidence: archaeological reports)*

**22 May 1980 — Parliamentary Criticism**

MP **Ted Rowlands** criticised BP Pension Trust's conduct in the House of
Commons, highlighting community concern.  *(Established Fact: Hansard)*

**1982 — First Land Registration**

Land registration introduced in Wales; the farm was registered under **BP's
title**.  **BP Pension Trust Ltd / BP Properties first registered title at
HM Land Registry.**  HM Land Registry later confirmed that BP's first
registration occurred in 1982.  The original registration bundle survives but
remains unavailable following an FOI refusal.  Mary Williams died in 1983,
within what appears to have been the relevant period for challenging first
registration.  *(Established Fact: Land Registry records and FOI response)*

**1983 — Death of Mary Williams**

Mary Williams died at the farmhouse, having remained in possession until her
death.  She died **before** the Court of Appeal proceedings and before the
demolition of Great House Farm.  The litigation thereafter continued despite
the licence holder having died.  The "licence" legally expired four years
before the 1987 Court of Appeal ruling.  *(Established Fact)*

**1984 — Missing Deeds**

Copies of the Bute Estate -> Daniel Thomas deed and index card are reported
missing from Cardiff Library.  *(Family Testimony)*

**1987 — Court of Appeal Decision**

In ***BP Properties Ltd v Buckler*** [1987] EWCA Civ 2 / [1987] 2 EGLR 168,
the Court of Appeal held that the 1974 unilateral licence ended adverse
possession.  The appeal determined issues relating to licence and adverse
possession rather than providing a final judicial determination of competing
historical title claims.  *(Established Fact: court judgment)*

The court ruled that Mrs. Buckler did not have "adverse possession"
because BP had sent her letters granting her permission to stay for life,
effectively turning her into a "licensee."  *(Established Fact: court
judgment)*

The judgment states: "Mrs Mary Buckler, who was also from time to time
referred to as Mrs Williams, had conceived the notion that she had a title to
the ownership of Great House Farm through her own grandfather and that there
were documents in existence which proved her title.  No such documents were,
however, ever produced."  *(Established Fact: direct quotation from
judgment)*

The use of the term "squatter" and the framing of the occupiers as
"defendants" seeking possessory title, rather than heirs to centuries of
documented family occupation from 1667, has been the subject of subsequent
analysis.  The court prioritised formal Land Registration Act processes over
historical context.  *(Documentary Evidence and Working Hypothesis)*

**1988 — Cadw Inspection and Demolition**

Cadw inspected Great House Farm and considered emergency "spot-listing"
shortly before demolition.  Photographs taken on **29 July 1988** survive,
although many associated records no longer exist.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

Great House Farm was demolished on **6 December 1988** (some sources say
overnight 5/6 December), ending centuries of occupation.  The manner of the
eviction — removing a pregnant woman and three infants with five bailiffs and
around 20 police officers — has been described in press reports.
*(Documentary Evidence: newspaper reports)*

**1988 — European Court of Human Rights Application 14464/88**

An application was lodged with the European Court of Human Rights following
the eviction.  The application was later declared inadmissible.
*(Established Fact)*

**1992 — Planning Permission**

In March 1992, full planning permission was obtained for the residential
development **without any condition requiring archaeological work**.
*(Documentary Evidence)*

**1994 — Rescue Excavation**

The Cotswold Archaeological Trust (CAT, now Cotswold Archaeology) conducted
excavations revealing an early-medieval monastic cemetery with **1,026
inhumation burials** — by far the largest early-medieval burial population
so far recovered from Wales.  The excavation was funded by **Ideal Homes
Wales Ltd** (now part of Persimmon Homes).  Post-excavation analysis was
funded by **Cadw** (from January 1998) and the National Museums & Galleries
of Wales.  *(Documentary Evidence: archaeological reports)*

.. _identity-erasure:

Identity Erasure: The "Mrs Buckler" Question
=============================================

One dedicated subsection addresses the naming issue that recurs throughout the
legal record.

Mary Williams was the daughter of John Williams, who held the 1916 agricultural
tenancy of Great House Farm.  She married Frederick Buckler and, although
married, retained her maiden name "Williams" in daily life and in her dealings
with the estate.  She was known within the family and community as Mary
Williams.

In legal proceedings from 1962 onwards, the courts and estate consistently
referred to her as "Mrs Buckler" or "Mrs Mary Buckler."  The 1987 Court of
Appeal judgment states: "Mrs Mary Buckler, who was also from time to time
referred to as Mrs Williams…"

The family alleges that this naming convention was not merely clerical but
carried legal significance: by addressing her by her husband's surname, the
courts and estate may have sought to disconnect her from the Williams lineage
— the very lineage that held the asserted grandfather rights and any Bute/Thomas
agreement.  It is easier to argue that a "widow" is a mere dependent of a
deceased tenant than to argue that a Williams daughter is not the rightful
heir to an ancestral agreement involving the Bute Estate.

The 1974 unilateral licence was addressed to "Mrs Buckler," a name Mary
Williams did not use.  The family alleges that if she had replied, she would
have been acknowledging the name and, by extension, the licence; if she did
not reply, her "silence" was used to claim acceptance by default.

Evidence exists that Mrs Williams was not silent and expressly rejected the
offer.  A 1978 newspaper article reports her publicly claiming ownership and
rejecting BP's asserted claim.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

This naming issue is raised here as a question of legal and social history,
not as an established finding of deliberate misconduct.  The family alleges
that the systematic use of "Mrs Buckler" rather than "Mary Williams"
amounted to an erasure of her identity and status as a Williams.  *(Family
Testimony and Working Hypothesis)*

Archaeology
===========

This chapter consolidates all archaeological material relating to Great House
Farm and its immediate environs.

The Roman Villa
---------------

The earliest structure found in the vicinity was a possible late Iron-age
roundhouse, perhaps part of a farmstead.  In the 2nd century A.D. a Roman
villa was constructed, which developed considerably in the early 3rd century
and was occupied until the early 4th century.  Some walls of the villa were
reused as foundations for a 12th- or 13th-century barn — the only evidence
that occupation may have continued on the villa site into the post-Roman
period.  *(Documentary Evidence: GGAT excavation reports, 1978–1980)*

The Roman Soldier Discovery
---------------------------

Family testimony records the discovery of a Roman soldier under the living-room
floor of Ty Mawr (Great House Farm) in 1870/88.  The discovery reportedly
occurred while the Williams family was replacing old stone flagstones with a
timber floor.  The soldier was reportedly found in a stone-lined grave (cist)
wearing bronze or iron armour.  No contemporary nineteenth-century written
source has yet been located to corroborate this account.  *(Family Testimony)*

If corroborated, this private find occurred exactly one hundred years before
the house was demolished in 1988 and over a century before the "official"
1994 excavations.  The burial was likely part of the Llandough Roman Villa
estate, which was later used as a sacred monastic burial ground.

The 1963 Discovery
------------------

In **1963**, human skeletal remains were uncovered during utility trench works
near the farm.  A gully containing three human skeletons of possible
13th-century date was recorded by the Barry and Vale Archaeological Group.
This provides documentary evidence that the archaeological significance of the
site was recognised decades before the major excavations.  *(Documentary
Evidence: archaeological records)*

The 1978–1980 GGAT Excavations
------------------------------

The Glamorgan-Gwent Archaeological Trust (GGAT) conducted excavations in
1978–1980.  The earliest structure found was a possible late Iron-age
roundhouse.  In the 2nd century A.D. a Roman villa was constructed, which
developed considerably in the early 3rd century and was occupied until the
early 4th century.  Some walls of the villa were reused as foundations for a
12th- or 13th-century barn.  *(Documentary Evidence: GGAT reports)*

The 1994 Rescue Excavation
--------------------------

The Cotswold Archaeological Trust (CAT, now Cotswold Archaeology) conducted
excavations in 1994 revealing an early-medieval monastic cemetery with
**1,026 inhumation burials** — by far the largest early-medieval burial
population so far recovered from Wales.  *(Documentary Evidence:
archaeological reports)*

**Burial 631** (dated between c. AD 340 and 660) highlights the site's
national significance.  This highly unusual find — a young man found with
iron straps binding him — underscored the true national importance of the
archaeology located directly adjacent to and underneath the demolished farm.
*(Documentary Evidence)*

Osteological examination was conducted by **Louise Loe** (University of
Bristol Ph.D., awarded 2004).  Radiocarbon dates from burials span the late
8th to late 10th century, with one burial dug through a demolished villa wall
dated 999–1284 cal A.D.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

The excavation was funded by **Ideal Homes Wales Ltd** (now part of Persimmon
Homes).  Post-excavation analysis was funded by **Cadw** (from January 1998)
and the National Museums & Galleries of Wales.  The finds and archive have
been deposited with the National Museum & Gallery, Cardiff.  *(Documentary
Evidence)*

The Monastic Cemetery
---------------------

The 1994 excavations confirmed that the site was an early-medieval monastic
cemetery.  The farmhouse was "comprehensively demolished" prior to the
excavation.  *(Documentary Evidence)*

------------

Archaeological Narrative Questions
----------------------------------

When the major excavation of over 1,000 burials took place in 1994, it was
framed as a "new" discovery.  Attributing the site's significance only to
this date ignores the 1963 utility trench finds and the family's 1870/88
account, both of which suggest earlier recognition of archaeological
significance.  The fact that the developer voluntarily funded the 1994
excavation — after the house was flattened — raises questions about whether
the planning system adequately protected the heritage prior to demolition.
*(Working Hypothesis)*

Marconi
=======

This chapter consolidates all material relating to Guglielmo Marconi and the
Llandough / Lavernock wireless experiments.

Confirmed Evidence
------------------

On **13 May 1897**, Guglielmo Marconi conducted wireless telegraphy
experiments between **Lavernock Point** and **Flat Holm** in the Bristol
Channel.  The first message sent was "CAN YOU HEAR ME" in Morse code,
replied to by George Kemp: "YES LOUD AND CLEAR."  *(Established Fact:
Marconi historical records)*

Post Office officials identified in the famous photograph of the event include
Mr G.N. Partridge, Mr H.C. Price, and Mr S.E. Hailes.  *(Documentary
Evidence)*

Historical Context
------------------

Marconi's experiments in Wales were part of a broader international race to
develop wireless telegraphy.  German wireless pioneer **Adolf Slaby** was
conducting parallel experiments, and **Kaiser Wilhelm II** took a personal
interest in wireless technology.  The British Post Office was keen to
establish British primacy in the field.  *(Established Fact: historical
records)*

The Williams Family Connection
------------------------------

According to family tradition, **Guglielmo Marconi** was a guest at Great House
Farm while working on his wireless telegraphy experiments.  A Williams family
member allegedly assisted with erecting the mast.  A Williams was also
allegedly cut from the narrative of the successful experiment at Lavernock
Point, dismissed as another Post Office worker.  *(Family Testimony)*

No direct documentary evidence linking Marconi to the Williams family at Great
House Farm has yet been located in Marconi's technical papers or estate
correspondence.  Extensive archive searches have narrowed the remaining
repositories where further correspondence might be found, including:

*   **The Postal Museum** (London)
*   **The Science Museum Group**
*   **Marconi Archives** (where held)

If corroborated, linking a named Williams ancestor to a world-changing event
like the first radio transmission at Lavernock Point would elevate the family
from "tenants" to "National History Contributors."  *(Working Hypothesis)*

LiDAR Analysis
==============

Modern LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) survey data has been examined in
relation to the Great House Farm site and its immediate landscape.  The
following features have been identified or are under investigation:

**Circular Mound**

A possible circular earthwork or mound has been identified in the LiDAR data
near the former farm site.  One possible interpretation is that this may
represent a Bronze Age barrow or a later landscape feature.  Further
archaeological investigation would be required to confirm its nature and date.
*(Working Hypothesis)*

**Terraces**

Terraced features are visible in the LiDAR data on the slopes near the farm.
These may represent agricultural terracing, garden terraces associated with
a high-status residence, or earlier landscape modifications.  Their
relationship to the Roman villa and monastic phases of the site remains
unclear.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

**Earthworks**

Various earthwork features are visible in the LiDAR data surrounding the
former farm.  These may include field boundaries, trackways, or earlier
enclosures.  Their date and function have not been established.
*(Working Hypothesis)*

**Boundaries**

LiDAR data reveals boundary features that do not align with modern field
boundaries.  One possible interpretation is that these represent earlier
manorial or monastic estate boundaries.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

**Possible Enclosures**

Rectilinear and curvilinear enclosure features have been tentatively
identified.  These may relate to the Roman villa estate, the monastic
settlement, or later agricultural use.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

**Relationship to the Roman Villa**

The LiDAR features extend beyond the area of the known Roman villa
excavations.  Modern LiDAR analysis suggests that the villa complex may have
been substantially larger than previously recognised, with associated
buildings, enclosures, and landscape features extending across a wider area.
*(Working Hypothesis)*

**Relationship to the Monastery**

The 1994 excavations confirmed an early-medieval monastic cemetery.  LiDAR
features in the vicinity may indicate the extent of the monastic precinct,
though no direct correlation has yet been established.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

**Relationship to Great House Farm**

The farmhouse itself was demolished in 1988.  LiDAR data may reveal
foundations, garden features, or landscape modifications associated with the
farm's long period of occupation.  *(Working Hypothesis)*

Modern LiDAR analysis suggests a substantially larger archaeological complex
than previously recognised.  However, none of the LiDAR interpretations
presented above should be regarded as established fact without ground-truthing
and formal archaeological investigation.

Farmhouse Plot Theory
=====================

This chapter presents a working hypothesis regarding the legal status of the
farmhouse, garden, and curtilage as distinct from the surrounding farmland.

The Hypothesis
--------------

The hypothesis holds that the **farmhouse, garden, and curtilage** may have
constituted a separate legal parcel from the surrounding agricultural land.
This distinction, if proven, could have significant implications for the legal
analysis of the 1955, 1962, 1974, and 1987 proceedings.

Supporting Evidence
-------------------

*   The 1955 possession order was enforced for the farm **except** the
    farmhouse and garden, suggesting a practical (and possibly legal)
    distinction between the two parcels.
*   The 1962 possession proceedings were brought specifically for the
    "farmhouse and garden," not the entire farm.
*   The 1974 licence was offered specifically for the farmhouse and garden.
*   The 1987 Court of Appeal case concerned possession of the farmhouse,
    not the wider agricultural land.
*   Estate records (Bute D 219) sometimes treated the "Court House" or
    "Grange" as a distinct entity from the surrounding farm leases.

Contrary Evidence
-----------------

*   The 1916 tenancy granted by the Marquess of Bute to John Williams
    appears to have covered the entire farm, including the farmhouse.
*   The 1938 auction sale referred to "Great House Farm" as a whole,
    without obvious parcel division.
*   No explicit deed of severance or separate title for the farmhouse plot
    has yet been located.

Relationship to Proceedings
---------------------------

If the farmhouse plot was subject to a different tenure or conveyance history
from the surrounding farmland, this could affect:

*   **1955 proceedings:**  The partial enforcement of the possession order
    may indicate recognition of separate rights in the farmhouse.
*   **1962 proceedings:**  The limited scope of the possession claim may
    reflect uncertainty about title to the farmhouse plot.
*   **1974 proceedings:**  The unilateral licence was offered only for the
    farmhouse and garden, possibly because BP's title to the wider farm was
    stronger or differently constituted.
*   **1987 proceedings:**  The Court of Appeal's focus on the licence and
    adverse possession may have been complicated by unaddressed questions
    about the legal parcel being disputed.

This hypothesis is presented as a **working hypothesis**, not an established
legal conclusion.  Further documentary research is required to test it.

Unresolved Questions
====================

This chapter identifies key questions that remain unresolved, together with
the current evidence, missing evidence, and the archive or institution most
likely capable of resolving each question.

The 1667 Conveyance
--------------------

*   **Question:**  Was there a formal conveyance or admission in the Court
    Baron in 1667 transferring tenure of Great House Farm to a Williams
    ancestor?  If so, what was its nature (copyhold, lease for lives, fee
    farm grant)?
*   **Current evidence:**  Family testimony states that the farm was
    "acquired" in 1667.  Bute Estate Records (NLW D 219) treat the family
    as part of a continuous sequence of manorial leases.
*   **Missing evidence:**  The original 1667 deed or Court Baron record.
*   **Most likely source:**  National Library of Wales (un-indexed Corbett
    or Bute manorial records); Glamorgan Archives.

The Daniel Thomas Deed
----------------------

*   **Question:**  Was there a formal deed or agreement between Daniel Thomas
    & Son and the Williams family in the early 1900s transferring or
    enfranchising title in return for quarrying rights?
*   **Current evidence:**  Family testimony records such an agreement and a
    tree-planting ceremony.  The 1876 carve-out record (D153/4–8) shows the
    Bute Estate severing land for the Llandough Limeworks.
*   **Missing evidence:**  The original deed or conveyance; any Bute Estate
    correspondence with Daniel Thomas.
*   **Most likely source:**  National Library of Wales (Bute Estate Records);
    Glamorgan Archives; Cardiff Central Library (if the missing index card
    can be located).

--------------------------------------

*   **Question:**  What was the reference number and content of the 1900s
    index card reportedly removed from Cardiff Library in 1984?
*   **Current evidence:**  Family testimony states that a card was removed.

    was taken.
*   **Most likely source:**  Cardiff Central Library local history collection;
    Glamorgan Archives (if the card was transferred); Freedom of Information
    request to Cardiff Council.

    request).

    access request); Information Commissioner's Office appeal.

The Roman Soldier Provenance

    discovery be corroborated by any contemporary written source, museum
    record, or antiquarian publication?
*   **Current evidence:**  Family testimony records the discovery.  The 1994
    excavations confirmed a high-status Roman Villa and early medieval
    cemetery on the same grounds.
*   **Missing evidence:**  Contemporary 19th-century written record;
    museum accession record; antiquarian publication.
*   **Most likely source:**  Amgueddfa Cymru (National Museum Wales)
    archives; RCAHMW early antiquarian records; local newspaper archives
    (1870s–1890s).

The Farmhouse Legal Parcel
--------------------------

*   **Question:**  Did the farmhouse, garden, and curtilage constitute a
    separate legal parcel from the surrounding farmland, with a different
    tenure history?
*   **Current evidence:**  The 1955 possession order was enforced only for
    the farm, not the farmhouse; subsequent proceedings focused on the
    farmhouse and garden; estate records sometimes treated the "Court House"
    as distinct.
*   **Missing evidence:**  Any explicit deed of severance or separate title
    for the farmhouse plot.
*   **Most likely source:**  HM Land Registry (historical title registers);
    National Library of Wales (Bute Estate manorial records); Glamorgan
    Archives.

    mound, terraces, earthworks, and possible enclosures identified in the

*   **Current evidence:**  LiDAR survey data reveals anomalous features.

*   **Most likely source:**  RCAHMW (aerial photograph and LiDAR archive);

Marconi and Mr Williams

*   **Question:**  Did Guglielmo Marconi stay at or visit Great House Farm

*   **Current evidence:**  Family tradition asserts both claims.  The 1897

    Great House Farm.

    archives).
