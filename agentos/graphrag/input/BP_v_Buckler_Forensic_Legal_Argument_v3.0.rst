IMPORTANT FOREWORD: REALISTIC LEGAL PROSPECTS
================================================================================

This memorandum identifies six independent but mutually reinforcing forensic
pillars upon which the foundations of *BP Properties Ltd v Buckler* [1987]
EWCA Civ 2 / [1987] 2 EGLR 168 may be scrutinised. It does NOT assert that
the 1987 decision can be directly reopened or set aside through ordinary
appellate or civil process. The legal regime governing reopening of final
Court of Appeal determinations after nearly four decades is deliberately
restrictive and prioritises finality of litigation.

DIRECT REOPENING IS NOT REALISTIC
---------------------------------

  • CPR 52.30 (*Taylor v Lawrence* [2002] EWCA Civ 90): The Court of Appeal
    will not reopen a final determination unless (a) necessary to avoid real
    injustice, (b) circumstances are exceptional, and (c) no alternative
    effective remedy exists. Fresh evidence, a wrong result, or important
    points of law are NOT sufficient (*Municipio de Mariana v BHP Group*
    [2021] EWCA Civ 1156). Paradigm cases involve fraud, bias, or the judge
    having read the wrong papers.

  • Set-aside for fraud (*Takhar v Gracefield Developments Ltd* [2019] UKSC
    13): Requires proof of conscious and deliberate dishonesty that was
    material to the decision. The evidential bar is high. Mere
    incompleteness of the evidential picture or novel legal reasoning does
    NOT equate to fraud.

  • Fresh evidence (*Ladd v Marshall* [1954] 1 WLR 1489): After 39 years,
    the criteria (evidence not obtainable with reasonable diligence;
    important influence; apparently credible) are almost impossible to
    satisfy in practice.

  • Limitation and laches: Any fresh proprietary claim faces severe
    limitation and equitable delay obstacles, especially after physical
    demolition and residential development.

THE REALISTIC FORENSIC ROUTES
-----------------------------

  1. LAND REGISTRATION ACT 2002, s.65 AND SCHEDULE 4 — RECTIFICATION
     Examination of the surviving first-registration bundle (titles
     WA231076 / WA240304, confirmed by HM Land Registry letter 30 July 2026,
     ref. N610CGV) for possible mistake at the 1982–83 first registration.
     This is the strongest (though still difficult) forensic route.

  2. PRESSURE FOR PUBLIC OR INDEPENDENT INQUIRY
     Where direct judicial remedies are foreclosed by limitation, finality,
     and third-party purchaser protection, the proper forum for examining
     systemic questions of record destruction, heritage suppression, and
     procedural irregularity is a public or independent inquiry with power
     to compel production of closed files.

  3. CONTINUED ARCHIVAL RECOVERY AND FORENSIC RECONSTRUCTION
     Systematic examination of primary evidence to test the six pillars
     identified below, with a view to supporting either rectification
     proceedings or inquiry evidence.

This memorandum therefore confines itself to:
  (a) identifying the legal and evidential foundations of the 1987 decision;
  (b) distinguishing what the Court of Appeal actually decided from matters
      that were not adjudicated;
  (c) placing those foundations under rigorous forensic scrutiny;
  (d) mapping the surviving legal routes with honest assessment of prospects.

PART I — THE CHRONOLOGY OF PROCEDURAL IRREGULARITY
================================================================================

The following timeline is established from court records, family testimony,
press reports, and archival documents. It is not disputed; what is disputed
is the legal significance of the events.

  c.1667             — Williams family acquires interest in Great House Farm.
                       Family testimony: a 999-year lease (virtual freehold).
                       Estate records: manorial lease. Original deed missing.

  1876               — Bute Estate severs 33 acres for Llandough Limeworks.
                       Family testimony: agreement with Daniel Thomas & Son
                       whereby quarrying permitted in exchange for eventual
                       title. Deeds now missing.

  1916               — Marquess of Bute grants yearly agricultural tenancy
                       to John Williams. No signed copy of this tenancy has
                       been located.

  1938               — Great House Farm advertised as "sold" at auction.
                       Williams family remains in physical occupation.

  1949               — Frederick Buckler and Mary Williams occupy farmhouse.
                       No executed written tenancy agreement identified.

  1953               — Last rent payment (under judgment for arrears).

  2 February 1955  — Agricultural tenancy formally terminated.

  4 July 1955        — High Court possession order enforced for farm EXCEPT
                       farmhouse and garden. Mrs Williams' objections
                       (following leg amputation) prevent enforcement
                       against the house.

  1959               — Mrs Williams offered tenancy of farmhouse and garden;
                       she refuses, asserting ownership through her
                       grandfather.

  11 December 1962   — Cardiff County Court: Judge Temple Morris QC grants
                       possession order for farmhouse and garden plus mesne
                       profits. Order not enforced.

  2 March 1965       — Western Ground Rents offers weekly tenancy at £2.
                       Agreement sent but never signed or returned.

  December 1965      — Frederick Buckler dies.

  December 1969      — Western Ground Rents sells to BP Pension Trust Ltd.

  3 July 1974        — Cardiff County Court hearing: Mary Williams pleads
                       claim of ownership. Court adjourns part-heard.
                       Defence asserts adverse possession under Limitation
                       Act 1939.

  19 September 1974  — Judge Watkin Powell grants leave to execute the 1962
                       possession order; orders warrant issue forthwith but
                       NOT ENFORCED until 31 October 1974.

  30 October 1974    — Mrs Williams' solicitors issue notice of appeal.

  31 October 1974    — Possession warrant scheduled for this date is
                       WITHDRAWN. No physical eviction occurs.

  4 November 1974    — Letters received by Mrs Williams' solicitor from
                       BP Pension Trust Ltd and BP Properties Ltd,
                       allegedly sent 31 October 1974. These letters offer
                       a unilateral lifetime licence to "Mrs Buckler" to
                       occupy the farm until her death, rent-free.

  1978               — Newspaper article reports Mary Williams publicly
                       claiming ownership and rejecting BP's asserted claim.

  1982               — Land registration introduced in Wales. Farm registered
                       under BP's title (WA231076 / WA240304).

  23 February 1983   — Land Registry application WA240304 submitted.

  14 March 1983      — Mary Doreen Williams dies at the farmhouse, still in
                       occupation. The personal licence expires with her.

  1984               — Bute Estate → Daniel Thomas deed and index card
                       reported missing from Cardiff Library.

  31 July 1987       — Court of Appeal (Dillon LJ, Mustill LJ, Sir Edward
                       Eveleigh) dismisses Buckler's appeal. Holds that the
                       1974 unilateral licence ended adverse possession.

  6 December 1988    — Great House Farm demolished.

  1988               — ECHR Application 14464/88 lodged; later declared
                       inadmissible. File subsequently destroyed.

  15 May 1992        — William "Billy" Buckler dies.

PART II — PILLAR ONE: THE TEMPORAL SEQUENCE AND THE UNILATERAL LICENCE
================================================================================

2.1  THE COURT'S OWN FINDING

BP accepted, and the Court proceeded on the basis, that Mrs Williams was in
adverse possession up to 31 October 1974. That acceptance is significant.
It establishes that the occupation was not characterised throughout as
permissive. The legal sequence was therefore:

  adverse possession → 31 October 1974 → subsequent legal event →
  cessation of adverse character thereafter.

The critical question is the legal effect of the event that occurred after
31 October 1974.

2.2  THE TEMPORAL DISTINCTION: 31 OCTOBER AND 4 NOVEMBER 1974

The letters were dated 31 October 1974 but received by Mrs Williams'
solicitors on 4 November 1974. The Court held that, from receipt,
possession became referable to a licence and was therefore no longer adverse
within the meaning of section 10 of the Limitation Act 1939.

The ratio is prospective from receipt. The forensic question that remains
is the precise doctrinal authority by which a unilateral, unaccepted
communication, unsupported by consideration, was held capable of altering
the statutory character of possession for the purposes of the Limitation
Act. Dillon LJ himself described the proposition as "a new one" and
recognised that it might appear inconsistent with the policy of the
limitation legislation (including the restriction on continual claim and
formal entry). That novelty and the acknowledged policy tension remain
legitimate subjects of legal analysis.

2.3  NO RETROSPECTIVE CREATION OF AUTHORITY

The fundamental objection is one of legal authority. BP cannot lose,
exhaust or allow to expire a particular procedural mechanism for obtaining
possession and then, several days later, create a new instrument which
retrospectively supplies the authority that was absent on the critical date.

The subsequent instrument must therefore be examined independently. The
question is:

  "What legal authority permitted BP, after 31 October 1974, to alter the
   legal character of possession which had already existed before the
   licence was communicated?"

If the answer is that the licence merely operated prospectively, it cannot
explain or retrospectively legitimise the possession existing on 31 October.

If the answer is that it operated retrospectively, the precise statutory or
common-law authority for that retrospective effect must be identified.

2.4  THE CRITICAL DISTINCTION: PROSPECTIVE LICENCE V. RETROSPECTIVE
      RECHARACTERISATION

Proposition A — Prospective licence:

  "From the point at which the licence became legally effective, Mrs
   Williams was permitted to occupy and therefore her subsequent possession
   was no longer adverse."

That is the ratio of the 1987 decision.

Proposition B — Retrospective recharacterisation:

  "Because BP subsequently sent a licence, Mrs Williams' possession BEFORE
   the licence was communicated must retrospectively be treated as
   possession by permission."

That proposition requires separate legal authority. It does not follow
merely from the existence of the later document. The distinction is
especially important because the limitation consequences depend upon the
character of possession during the relevant period.

The argument therefore does not require pretending that the Court of Appeal
did not rule against the adverse-possession claim. Instead, it identifies a
narrower question:

  "Did the Court of Appeal's reasoning lawfully permit a subsequent
   instrument to alter the legal character of possession existing before
   the instrument was communicated, and, if so, what was the precise legal
   source and temporal operation of that rule?"

2.5  MRS WILLIAMS DID NOT ACCEPT BP'S ASSERTED OWNERSHIP

The factual evidence is inconsistent with the proposition that Mrs Williams
understood herself to be occupying Great House Farm merely by BP's
permission. In 1974 she expressly stated to the press:

  "It's my land. My lawyers say so."

That statement demonstrates an express rejection of the very premise upon
which the purported licence depended: namely, that BP was the party entitled
to give her permission to remain. She was not asking BP for permission to
occupy. She was asserting ownership.

2.6  EXCESS OF AUTHORITY

The issue is whether BP attempted to exercise a power it no longer
possessed under the expired possession process, and then relied upon a
subsequently generated instrument to establish a different legal basis for
the continuing occupation.

If BP's original authority ended on 31 October, then any subsequent
intervention required a new and independently lawful source of authority. It
could not simply be assumed that the expired authority continued in another
form. Nor could a later instrument automatically acquire retrospective legal
effect merely because BP wished to prevent the limitation period from
running.

The principle can be stated:

  "An owner cannot necessarily convert an expired procedural remedy into a
   continuing substantive power merely by issuing subsequent correspondence
   purporting to alter the legal character of an occupier's possession.
   The legal source, scope and temporal operation of that power must be
   demonstrated."

2.7  CONCLUSION ON PILLAR ONE

The central issue is not whether BP had a paper title. Nor is it simply
whether BP could ever have granted Mrs Williams permission to occupy. The
critical issue is when and by what lawful authority her possession could be
characterised as permissive.

On the presently identified chronology:

  31 October 1974 — BP's possession mechanism reaches its deadline.
  31 October 1974 — Mrs Williams remains in possession.
  31 October 1974 — Mrs Williams' position is that the land is hers.
  4 November 1974 — The purported licence is communicated afterwards
                     and is addressed to "Mrs Buckler".

The resulting legal question is:

  "Can an instrument created or communicated after the expiry of the
   relevant possession mechanism retrospectively convert possession which
   was already adverse on 31 October into possession by permission,
   thereby interrupting or neutralising limitation?"

If so, the precise legal authority for that retrospective consequence must
be identified. If not, then the licence can operate, at most, from the
point at which it validly came into existence and cannot retrospectively
explain the character of Mrs Williams' possession before that date.

PART III — PILLAR TWO: THE IDENTITY ERASURE AND DEFECTIVE INSTRUMENT
================================================================================

3.1  THE NAMING CONVENTION AS A LEGAL MECHANISM

Mary Doreen Williams (born 10 August 1913, died 14 March 1983) was the
daughter of John Williams. She married Frederick Buckler yet retained her
maiden name in daily life and in dealings with the estate. From 1962
onwards, the courts and estate consistently referred to her as "Mrs
Buckler". The 1974 licence letters were addressed to "Mrs Buckler".

The 1987 judgment records: "Mrs Mary Buckler, who was also from time to time
referred to as Mrs Williams, had conceived the notion that she had a title
to the ownership of Great House Farm through her own grandfather..."

The court's use of "conceived the notion" implies the title was imaginary.
The phrase "from time to time referred to as Mrs Williams" inverts reality:
her title was Williams, and the courts' use of "Mrs Buckler" was the
occasional occurrence.

3.2  THE LEGAL SIGNIFICANCE OF THE NAMING CONVENTION

The family asserts that the consistent use of "Mrs Buckler" in legal
documents disconnected her from the Williams lineage that underpinned her
asserted grandfather rights. If the Separate Plot Hypothesis (see Part VI)
depends on a title derived through the Williams line, the naming convention
obscured the lineage through which that title was claimed.

The 1987 Court of Appeal case consistently referred to her as "Mrs.
Buckler." This was not merely a naming error; it was a legal mechanism to
disconnect her from the Williams family's hereditary tenure, which dates
back to at least 1667.

3.3  THE LICENCE ADDRESSED TO THE WRONG PERSON

The 1974 letters were addressed to "Mrs Buckler" when the principal
occupier was Mary Williams. The interest in a licence expressed by Mr
Frederick Buckler before his death in late 1965 died with him. Mrs
Williams was addressed as "Mrs Buckler" in order to bind that earlier
expressed interest and Buckler family title to the unilateral licence.

The licence was alleged to be accepted by her silence, as no expressed
rejection or acceptance occurred. However, evidence exists that Mrs Williams
was not silent: the 1978 newspaper article reports her publicly claiming
ownership and rejecting BP's asserted claim.

3.4  THE TRAP OF THE UNILATERAL LICENCE

The tactical effect of the naming was:

  • If she replied, she would be acknowledging the name and, by extension,
    the licence.
  • If she didn't, BP used her "silence" to claim she accepted the licence
    by default.

This was not a clerical error; it was a tactical erasure of her identity as
a Williams, the holder of the 320-year-old possessory title.

3.5  TWO SEPARATE ENTITIES ISSUING LETTERS

Two separate entities — BP Pension Trust Ltd and BP Properties Ltd —
issued letters simultaneously or in sequence to "corroborate" a narrative.
This raises questions of corporate collusion. In 1974, BP Properties likely
did not yet have the paper title from the Pension Trust. They issued
"licences" for land they didn't technically own on paper yet, while the
Williams Family actually possessed it.

The judiciary treated them as separate entities to facilitate the 1987
ruling, but then treated them as the "same" to dismiss William Buckler Jnr's
appeal. This is a violation of the principle of Equity, where the law should
be applied consistently.

PART IV — PILLAR THREE: POSSESSION WAS ADJUDICATED; TITLE WAS NOT
================================================================================

4.1  THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN POSSESSION AND TITLE

The Court of Appeal in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 (31
July 1987) did not determine that BP (or its predecessors) held absolute
ownership free of any competing historical claim. It determined only that
the Williams/Buckler family's possession after the October/November 1974
unilateral letters was no longer "adverse" for the purposes of the Limitation
Act.

Key points from the judgment:

  • BP Properties Ltd held the paper title and had been registered
    proprietors since November 1982 following first registration.
  • The family's claim was framed as adverse possession of the farmhouse
    and garden from February 1955.
  • Possession proceedings in 1962 produced an order that was not enforced
    for many years.
  • In October 1974 BP Pension Trust Ltd / BP Properties Ltd wrote
    unilateral letters offering Mrs Buckler (addressed as such) a rent-free
    life licence. She never accepted or rejected them in writing.
  • The Court of Appeal held that, whether she liked it or not, from
    receipt of those letters her possession was by licence and therefore
    ceased to be adverse. Limitation therefore stopped running.
  • The court expressly noted that if Mrs Buckler had already acquired a
    title by adverse possession before the 1974 letters, that title would
    have constituted an overriding interest to which the later registration
    would have been subject, and her personal representatives could have
    sought to protect it.

4.2  CONSEQUENCE: THE 1987 DECISION IS A POSSESSION RULING ONLY

The 1987 decision is a possession ruling resting on the legal effect of the
unilateral 1974 letters. It is not a ruling that extinguished any pre-1974
historical title or that finally determined the root of title in favour of
BP. The family's long-asserted claim (including the Separate Plot
Hypothesis and the 1667 transaction) was never adjudicated on the merits of
title.

4.3  THE COURT'S BURDEN WAS DIFFERENT

The estate was not required to prove every historical conveyance from the
seventeenth century. Instead, it relied upon documentary title and
subsequent tenancy arrangements which the courts accepted as sufficient.
That is procedurally different from proving that no earlier proprietary
rights ever existed.

4.4  THE DOCUMENTARY GAP AFTER 1876

The chronology contains no continuous chain of rent records after 1876. This
does not prove rent ceased. Equally, it does not prove rent continued. If a
landlord alleges a continuing tenancy across many decades, surviving rent
ledgers, rent books, annual accounts or demands would ordinarily be valuable
corroborative evidence. Their apparent absence in the present record is an
evidential gap, not proof of either position.

4.5  NO EXPRESS CONVEYANCE AWAY BY THE FAMILY

Within the material presently available there is no identified document in
which the Williams or Buckler family:
  • sold the freehold;
  • executed a conveyance away;
  • executed a release of ownership;
  • expressly disclaimed ownership.

That absence is not proof that no such document exists, but it is relevant
to further archival investigation.

PART V — PILLAR FOUR: THE FIRST-REGISTRATION CIRCULARITY
================================================================================

5.1  THE 1983 FIRST REGISTRATION AND THE 1987 CIRCULARITY

HM Land Registry first-registered the relevant titles (WA231076 / WA240304)
in 1982–83. Mary Doreen Williams died on 14 March 1983 while still in
occupation. The family's position is that her claim and any supporting
material were not properly brought to the attention of, or were disregarded
by, the Land Registry examiner at the critical moment.

The 1987 Court of Appeal decision later treated the registration as a given.
Subsequent Land Registry correspondence has been understood by the family
as relying, at least in part, on the existence of the 1987 ruling to justify
the propriety of the 1983 registration. That creates a temporal
circularity:

  • In 1983 the Land Registry could not have known the outcome of a 1987
    Court of Appeal decision.
  • Any examiner in 1983 who had access to the 1974 County Court papers
    (the part-heard possession action, the defence asserting adverse
    possession, and the subsequent unilateral letters) was under a duty
    to consider whether a competing claim or overriding interest existed.
  • The family's case is that the examiner either did not see, or wilfully
    ignored, the live challenge embodied in the 1974 proceedings and Mary
    Williams's continuing occupation and assertion of ownership.

5.2  THE SURVIVING REGISTRATION BUNDLE

The survival of the first-registration bundle (confirmed by HM Land
Registry letter 30 July 2026, ref. N610CGV) makes it possible, for the
first time, to test this allegation directly by examining the examiner's
papers, the documents lodged, the Linklaters & Paines correspondence, and
the A13/DL schedules.

5.3  RECTIFICATION UNDER LRA 2002, SCHEDULE 4

This is the strongest (though still difficult) forensic route. Under the
Land Registration Act 2002, s.65 and Schedule 4, rectification of the
register is possible to correct a mistake. Where the registered proprietor
(or successor) is in possession, rectification requires consent, or that
the proprietor caused or substantially contributed to the mistake by fraud
or lack of proper care, or that it would be unjust not to rectify.

Exceptional circumstances may justify refusal. Indemnity may be available
under Schedule 8.

The 1982–83 first registration of titles WA231076 / WA240304 is the most
realistic target, particularly if the surviving bundle reveals failure to
consider actual occupation or a competing claim.

However, the land has since been developed into residential housing (Church
View Close). Third-party purchasers for value without notice enjoy strong
protection; rectification against them is exceptional. This is a
significant practical obstacle that must be honestly acknowledged.

5.4  THE 1982 REGISTRATION AND MRS WILLIAMS' DEATH

Mary Williams died shortly after the first registration, during what
appears to have been the critical period for any objection to first
registration. The litigation thereafter continued despite the licence holder
having died. The "licence" legally expired four years before the 1987 Court
of Appeal ruling.

PART VI — PILLAR FIVE: THE SEPARATE PLOT HYPOTHESIS
================================================================================

6.1  THE CENTRAL RESEARCH QUESTION

Was the farmhouse, garden and curtilage a separate legal parcel from the
surrounding agricultural holding, such that the possession proceedings from
1955 onwards related only to the leased farmland and not to the Williams
family's own plot?

This question — the Separate Plot Hypothesis — is examined through the
chronology of ownership, estate transfers, litigation, and post-eviction
events.

6.2  EVIDENCE SUPPORTING THE HYPOTHESIS

Evidence 1: The 1876 Severance of 33 Acres

In 1876, the Bute Estate recorded the severance of 33 acres formerly part
of Great House Farm for the Llandough Limeworks (D153/4–8). This removed a
significant portion of the farm from agricultural tenure.

Question: Did this severance create an opportunity for a separate conveyance
of the farmhouse, garden and curtilage? If the industrial carve-out broke
the unity of the original holding, did the remaining farmhouse plot acquire
a separate legal identity — whether by express conveyance, by implication,
or by recognition of an existing underlying interest?

Family testimony: After the quarrying/limeworks, the family is believed to
have gained absolute title/freehold to the plot of land the house and garden
occupied. Any mention of rent and tenure thereafter referred to the
surrounding farmland still on the lease agreement.

Evidence 2: The 1955 Partial Enforcement

On 4 July 1955, the possession order was enforced for the farm EXCEPT the
farmhouse and garden due to Mrs Mary Williams's strong objections following
her leg amputation and hospitalisation. The Williams' occupation of the
farmhouse and garden remained undisturbed.

Question: If the 1955 possession order already covered the farmhouse and
garden, why was it not immediately recovered along with the rest of the farm?
Was the partial enforcement a pragmatic accommodation of Mrs Williams's
medical condition? Or was it legally necessary because the farmhouse and
garden occupied a different legal position that could not be swept into the
same possession order?

Evidence 3: The 1962 Fresh Proceedings

In 1962, Western Ground Rents brought a fresh action in Cardiff County
Court against Mr Frederick Buckler and Mrs Buckler (sued as Mrs Williams)
for possession of the farmhouse and garden and mesne profits since 1955.

Question: Why was a second possession action required for the farmhouse and
garden if the 1955 High Court order had already covered the "whole farm"?
Was it because the 1955 order had not in fact covered the farmhouse plot?
Or was it because the 1955 order required separate enforcement proceedings
for the remaining portion?

Evidence 4: The 1974 Unilateral Licence

If BP believed they already possessed absolute title to the farmhouse and
garden, why issue a personal licence? Why negotiate at all? Why not
simply enforce the existing possession order? Does the act of offering a
licence suggest that BP recognised the occupation was not
straightforwardly terminable?

Evidence 5: The 1971 Ordnance Survey

Did the Ordnance Survey alter the descriptive identity of the property
between the 1962 possession order (which concerned surrounding farmland) and
later legal documents? If so, did later legal documents rely on those
altered descriptions, potentially conflating two distinct parcels?

Evidence 6: The 1983 Land Registry Merger

BP Pension Trust Ltd / BP Properties first registered title at HM Land
Registry in 1982 (title numbers WA231076 and WA240304). On 23 February
1983, application WA240304 was submitted. Was the first registration based
upon one title or merged interests? If the Williams family held a separate
freehold to the farmhouse plot, did the 1982–1983 registration process
conflate that freehold with the surrounding leasehold or reversionary
interest?

6.3  COMPETING EXPLANATORY MODELS

Model A: The Entire Farm Remained Leased
  Great House Farm (including farmhouse, garden and curtilage) remained
  subject to a single leasehold interest from 1667 through 1988. The
  Williams/Buckler family were tenants throughout.

Model B: The Farmhouse Became a Separate Freehold
  After the 1876 severance, the farmhouse, garden and curtilage became a
  separate freehold held by the Williams family, while the surrounding
  farmland remained on lease.

Model C: Administrative Mistakes Gradually Merged Two Titles
  A separate freehold or long leasehold interest in the farmhouse plot may
  have existed historically, but administrative errors in mapping,
  registration, and litigation progressively obscured that distinction.

Model D: Separate Title Existed and Was Progressively Obscured
  The Williams family held a separate freehold or virtual freehold to the
  farmhouse plot. Subsequent mapping, registration, and litigation
  progressively obscured that distinction through a combination of
  administrative error, documentary loss, and deliberate legal strategy.

6.4  CONCLUSION ON PILLAR FIVE

The Separate Plot Hypothesis remains a Working Hypothesis. It is
consistent with a significant body of evidence, but it is contradicted by
other documented facts. The hypothesis cannot be tested conclusively
without:

  1. Access to the original HM Land Registry first-registration bundle
     (WA231076 / WA240304).
  2. Examination of the 1971 Ordnance Survey records.
  3. Location of any surviving copy of the 1667 transaction deed or the
     Bute Estate → Daniel Thomas conveyance.
  4. Independent corroboration of the reported missing deeds.

At present, the evidence is insufficient to distinguish conclusively between
administrative error, the confusion of two legal interests, and deliberate
concealment. The question remains open and testable.

PART VII — PILLAR SIX: THE HUMAN RIGHTS DIMENSION (LIMITED BUT RELEVANT)
================================================================================

7.1  THE LIMITATION OF HUMAN RIGHTS ARGUMENTS

The Human Rights Act 1998 is NOT generally retrospective for acts occurring
before 2 October 2000 (*Wilson v First County Trust Ltd (No 2)* [2003]
UKHL 40). Article 8 ECHR and Article 1 of Protocol No. 1 arguments cannot
be used to reopen the 1987 decision or the 1988 eviction in the manner of a
direct challenge. Private parties such as BP are not "public authorities"
for most HRA purposes.

This pillar is therefore of LIMITED direct legal utility for reopening the
1987 decision. Its relevance lies in:

  (a) framing the severity of the 1988 eviction and demolition for any
      public or independent inquiry;
  (b) informing the assessment of whether the 1982–83 first registration
      engaged any public-law duties;
  (c) supporting any argument that the State (as a shareholder in BP at the
      material time, with approximately 31.5% residual holding) had a
      distinct public-law responsibility that may have been engaged.

7.2  THE ECHR APPLICATION 14464/88

An application was lodged with the European Court of Human Rights following
the 1988 eviction and demolition. The application was later declared
inadmissible on 14 April 1989. The original case file has subsequently been
destroyed under the Court's records policy (circa 2000).

7.3  STRUCTURED CRITIQUE OF THE ECHR COMMISSION DECISION

The analytical table prepared by the family identifies potential failures,
omissions or points of under-analysis. The most material are:

  • Minimal engagement with the Article 6 fairness complaint and the
    alleged internal contradiction in treatment of related BP entities.
  • No granular review of the domestic evidence assessment; the complaint
    was rejected as unsubstantiated without showing why the evidence was
    insufficient.
  • No consideration of whether concealment or missing documents upset the
    balance between the parties.
  • Acceptance of the domestic finding that the applicant had no right to
    occupy, without independent scrutiny of whether that finding itself
    rested on a circular or incomplete factual basis.
  • No analysis of the severity of eviction and demolition, the family-life
    dimension of multi-generational occupation, or the cultural/historical
    significance of the site.
  • No exploration of possessory or equitable interests that might exist
    independently of formal paper title.
  • Summary disposal under the "manifestly ill-founded" label despite the
    seriousness and factual complexity of the case.

7.4  THE HUMAN CONTEXT

The application of the possession and licence regime occurred against a
background of marked vulnerability. Mary Williams (the principal adverse
possessor) was elderly, had suffered serious ill-health including amputation,
and died in 1983 while still in occupation. The subsequent enforcement and
demolition in 1988 took place against William "Billy" Buckler (then in
declining health), his pregnant wife Branwen Buckler, and their three young
children. The family were required to respond to dealings framed by reference
to Mary Williams' personal licence, a personal and non-heritable interest
that had expired on her death four years earlier.

This submission does not invite the Court to decide the case on sympathy.
It records that the strict application of the possession and licence rules
produced results of exceptional severity for persons of limited means and
impaired health, and that the legal characterisation of the family's
occupation shifted repeatedly (yearly tenancy, notice to quit, mesne profits,
weekly tenancy proposal, personal lifetime licence) while physical occupation
remained continuous. Those facts form part of the factual matrix against
which the foundations of the 1987 decision fall to be assessed.

PART VIII — SYNTHESIS: WHY THE 1987 DECISION IS JURISPRUDENTIALLY VULNERABLE
================================================================================

8.1  THE SIX PILLARS AS A UNIFIED FORENSIC ARGUMENT

The six pillars are not alternative theories. They are mutually
reinforcing strands of a single forensic case that the 1987 decision:

  (1) Rests on a licence whose temporal operation was novel and whose
      retrospective effect was not clearly established by reference to
      any identified statutory or common-law authority.

  (2) Was addressed to the wrong person, using a name the principal
      occupier never used, thereby disconnecting her from the Williams
      lineage that underpinned her asserted title.

  (3) Determined only possession, not the competing historical title
      claims, leaving open the question of whether the family held a
      separate freehold or virtual freehold to the farmhouse plot.

  (4) Was circularly dependent upon a 1983 first registration that may
      itself have been procedurally defective, and which occurred while
      the principal adverse possessor was still alive and asserting her
      claim.

  (5) Failed to examine the possibility that the farmhouse plot was a
      separate legal parcel from the surrounding agricultural land,
      despite significant documentary anomalies pointing in that direction.

  (6) Engaged the family's rights under the European Convention on Human
      Rights in circumstances of acute personal vulnerability, though
      direct HRA challenge is foreclosed by non-retrospectivity.

8.2  THE "GHOST LICENCE" PROBLEM

Mary Williams died on 14 March 1983. The 1987 Court of Appeal ruling
treated her as a licensee whose possession was not adverse. But the licence
was personal and non-heritable. It expired with her death. By the time of
the 1987 decision, the licence had been dead for over four years. The
court's reasoning therefore relied on a permission granted to a deceased
person to defeat the adverse possession claim of her living successors.

This is not merely a technical point. It goes to the heart of whether the
1987 decision can legitimately be treated as having finally determined the
title to land that was occupied by a new generation (Billy Buckler and his
family) who had no legal relationship with BP whatsoever.

8.3  THE RECORD DESTRUCTION PATTERN

The density of "not held / destroyed under retention / no records on
current systems" replies across independent bodies (police, courts,
archives, museums, Cadw-related files) creates an evidentiary vacuum that
itself supports an inference of deliberate concealment or multi-generational
institutional amnesia favourable to the commercial interest.

Key records confirmed destroyed or missing:
  • ECHR Application 14464/88 file — destroyed under Court's retention
    schedule (circa 2000).
  • RCAHMW correspondence (C.N. Johns / Mrs Buckler) — lost since 1986/87.
  • Cardiff County Court records — not held on current electronic systems.
  • South Wales Police operational records — stated as not held.
  • Original court bundles, pleadings, exhibits, witness statements —
    location unknown or destroyed.

8.4  THE ARCHAEOLOGICAL ERASURE

The site's known archaeological significance — early-medieval monastic
cemetery of St Dochdwy (1,026 burials excavated 1994), possible medieval
core of the house, Roman finds, reported armour under the floor, Marconi's
1897 stay — was downplayed, incompletely recorded, or overridden to permit
rapid demolition and later residential development.

The 1994 cemetery excavation was framed as a "new" discovery, ignoring the
family's 1870/88 notice. This potentially protected the parties involved in
the 1988 demolition from liability for destroying a known monument.

CLOSING STATEMENT
================================================================================

The 1987 decision in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler is not a monument of
settled law that brooks no further examination. It is a possession ruling
that resolved a narrow technical question about the effect of unilateral
licence letters on adverse possession. It did not determine the root of
title. It did not examine the Separate Plot Hypothesis. It did not address
the retrospective temporal problem with the rigour the doctrine requires.
It did not consider the identity erasure. It did not adjudicate the human
rights dimension. And it rested upon a first registration whose propriety
has never been independently verified.

Direct reopening of the 1987 judgment is not realistic under CPR 52.30,
Ladd v Marshall, or Takhar v Gracefield. The realistic routes are:

  1. Forensic examination of the 1982–83 first-registration bundle for
     possible rectification under LRA 2002 Sch 4.
  2. Pressure for a public or independent inquiry with power to compel
     production of closed files.
  3. Continued archival recovery to reconstruct the historical record.

The family does not ask for special treatment. It asks for the law to be
applied correctly, for the evidence to be examined fully, and for the truth
about Great House Farm to be established — not buried under a housing
development and a file destruction schedule.

The stones are on a tip. But the truth is not.
