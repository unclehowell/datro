1.1  The World Into Which Great House Farm Entered
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The year 1800 found Great House Farm — Ty Mawr — standing much as it had for
six centuries, a substantial stone house with thick walls and a steep roof,
perched on the ridge above the coastal plain of Glamorgan, within sight of the
Bristol Channel. The farmhouse was already old by any measure: the core of the
building may have dated to the early thirteenth century, and successive
generations of the Williams family had patched, extended, and adapted it to the
needs of each age. Around it lay the fields of the Llandough manor, a patchwork
of arable strips, pasture, and meadow that rolled down toward the marshy
lowlands of the River Ely and the growing town of Cardiff, some three miles to
the north-east.

The world beyond the farm was changing with gathering speed. The Industrial
Revolution was transforming the valleys of Glamorgan. Coal was being mined in
increasing quantities at Merthyr Tydfil; ironworks were springing up along the
northern rim of the coalfield; and the canals and, later, the railways were
beginning to stitch the rural parishes into an industrial economy that would
eventually swallow them. Yet in 1800, Llandough remained a place of scattered
farmsteads, small fields enclosed by hedgerows, and a population that lived by
mixed agriculture — grain, cattle, sheep, and the produce of small orchards and
gardens. The parish church of St Dochdwy, a stone's throw from the farmhouse,
had served the community since the early medieval period, and its churchyard
held the unmarked graves of generations.

The Williams family, who had occupied Great House Farm since at least 1667,
were by 1800 deeply rooted in this landscape. They were not merely tenants or
labourers; they were the established occupiers of the largest and oldest house
in the parish, a family whose name appeared in manorial records, church
registers, and local memory. Whether they held their position by virtue of a
999-year lease — a "virtual freehold" — or by some older customary right, they
regarded the farm as their own, and the surrounding community regarded them as
its natural custodians.

The estate structure within which they lived, however, was about to undergo a
series of convulsions that would reshape the ownership of Llandough and, in
time, set in motion the forces that would eventually destroy the farm and
dispossess the family. The period from 1800 to 1850 was, in retrospect, the
last relatively stable half-century of Great House Farm's long existence. It was
the period during which the modern documentary record begins to take shape —
through surveys, rentals, Baptist registrations, and the slow accumulation of
estate papers — and it is therefore the period from which any attempt to
reconstruct the farm's history must begin in earnest.

1.2  The Estate Framework: Bute, Plymouth, and the Manorial System
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: ef

   From 1552 to 1829, the Bute/Pembroke estate treated itself as the superior
   landholder and manorial lord of Llandough. The "Llandough manorial leases
   and agreements" series (National Library of Wales, Bute Estate Records,
   reference NLW D 219) documents leases of Llandough Farm covering
   approximately 166 acres during this entire period.

This was the legal and economic framework within which the Williams family
lived. The manor of Llandough had passed through many hands since the Norman
Conquest — from the Walsche family to Tewkesbury Abbey, through the Dissolution
to Sir Edward Carne, then into the Herbert/Pembroke orbit, and finally into the
complex web of estates that coalesced around the Bute family in the eighteenth
century. By 1800, the Bute interest was dominant, though not absolute. The
Marquess of Bute, whose principal seat was at Cardiff Castle, held extensive
lands throughout Glamorgan, and Llandough was one of many manors that paid rent,
services, and feudal dues into his coffers.

The manorial system, by 1800, was an anachronism in many parts of England and
Wales, but it remained a living reality in Glamorgan. The manor court still
met, at least intermittently, to regulate common rights, settle boundary
disputes, and enforce the customs of the manor. The chief rents — the annual
payments due from freeholders and copyholders within the manor — were collected
and recorded. The estate surveys, conducted at irregular intervals, mapped the
fields, noted the tenants, and assessed the value of each holding. It is through
these surveys, and through the rentals that accompanied them, that we can begin
to trace the presence of the Williams family at Great House Farm with something
approaching documentary precision.

Yet the estate structure was not static. The period 1800–1850 was marked by a
series of transfers and reconfigurations that would eventually concentrate
almost all the land of Llandough in a single hand. Understanding these transfers
is essential to understanding the legal position of the Williams family, for it
was the estate's claim of ultimate ownership — a claim that would be pressed
with increasing force in the twentieth century — that underpinned the litigation
that eventually destroyed the farm.

1.3  The Valentine Morris Interlude and the Rise of Sir Mark Wood
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   Between approximately 1770 and 1785, the Llandough estate interests were
   associated with Valentine Morris, who held property as part of the
   Piercefield/Glamorgan estate interests. The sequence of documented ownership
   ran: Talbot → Plymouth → Valentine Morris → Sir Mark Wood → Bute.

Valentine Morris was a figure of some note in the Glamorgan of the late
eighteenth century. A planter and politician who had made his fortune in the
West Indies, he returned to Wales and invested heavily in land, including the
Piercefield estate on the Wye. His connection to Llandough was part of a wider
pattern of estate consolidation in Glamorgan, as old manors were absorbed into
larger holdings and the traditional structure of local landownership was
replaced by the great estates of the industrial age.

Morris's tenure was relatively brief, and by the turn of the century the
Llandough lands had passed to Sir Mark Wood, a London banker and speculator who
had acquired substantial property interests in south Wales. Wood was a
transitional figure: he held the estate not as a traditional lord of the manor
but as an investor, and his interest in Llandough was financial rather than
personal. This shift — from the paternalistic, if extractive, lordship of the
medieval and early-modern period to the detached, profit-oriented ownership of
the modern estate — would have profound consequences for the tenants of Great
House Farm. The Williams family, who had lived on the land for generations and
who regarded it as their birthright, were now subject to an owner whose primary
concern was the return on his investment.

Sir Mark Wood's ownership of the Llandough estate is documented in the Glamorgan
Archives, though the precise terms of his acquisition and the nature of his
dealings with the existing tenants remain obscure. The estate records suggest
that he held the property from approximately 1794 until 1824, a period of thirty
years that coincided with the Napoleonic Wars, the post-war agricultural
depression, and the first stirrings of industrial expansion in the Cardiff area.
It was during Wood's tenure that the agricultural economy of Llandough began to
feel the pressures of change — rising prices, increasing demand for food from
the growing towns, and the gradual enclosure of common lands that had
historically provided pasture and fuel for the parish's inhabitants.

1.4  The Bute Consolidation: The 1820 Memorandum and the David Stewart Survey
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   In 1820, a memorandum of exchanges of land was executed between the Marquess
   of Bute and Lord Plymouth in the parish of Llandough. Llandough and Cogan
   appear in the rentals from 1821 onwards. This exchange was followed by a
   comprehensive survey of the Marquess of Bute's Glamorgan estates by David
   Stewart (Glamorgan Archives reference DB/E/1-2), which in 1824 recorded
   "Great House Farm alias Cedfin." The tithes of Llandough (along with
   Leckwith and Cogan) were purchased as part of this consolidation.

The 1820 memorandum represents a pivotal moment in the history of the estate.
The Marquess of Bute — John Crichton-Stuart, the 2nd Marquess, who would later
become one of the greatest landowners in British history — was engaged in a
systematic programme of estate consolidation. His aim was to gather into his own
hands the scattered manors, farms, and parcels of land that had been fragmented
by centuries of inheritance, marriage, and sale, and to create a single,
coherent estate that could be managed for maximum efficiency and profit. The
exchange with Lord Plymouth was part of this programme: Bute gave up certain
lands in return for Llandough and Cogan, and the resulting holding was
thereafter treated as a single unit within the Bute estate.

The David Stewart survey of 1824 was the instrument by which this consolidation
was mapped and recorded. Stewart was a professional surveyor employed by the
Bute estate to produce a detailed account of its Glamorgan properties, and his
work survives in the Glamorgan Archives (DB/E/1-2). The survey recorded the
boundaries, acreage, tenancies, and values of each farm, and it is the first
surviving document to refer explicitly to "Great House Farm alias Cedfin." The
alias is significant: "Cedfin" is a Welsh name, possibly an older or alternative
designation for the holding, and its preservation in the survey suggests that
the estate was aware of the farm's antiquity and its distinctive status within
the manor. Whether "Cedfin" was a field name, a house name, or a reference to
some older territorial division is not clear, but its appearance alongside
"Great House Farm" indicates that the property was already known by multiple
names — a common feature of ancient holdings whose identity had been layered
over centuries of occupation.

The survey also recorded the purchase of the tithes of Llandough, Leckwith, and
Cogan. Tithes — the traditional tenth of agricultural produce due to the church
— had been commuted to money payments or bought out by landowners in many
parishes by the early nineteenth century, and the Bute estate's acquisition of
the Llandough tithes removed a potential source of conflict and consolidated its
control over the parish's economic life. For the Williams family, the purchase
of tithes would have meant that their payments, which had historically gone to
the church or to a separate tithe owner, now went directly to the estate. This
was a small but significant shift in the economic relationship between tenant
and landlord, and it foreshadowed the more dramatic changes that would come in
the following decades.

The 1824 survey is one of the most important surviving documents for the early
history of Great House Farm, yet it remains largely unexamined. The Glamorgan
Archives hold the original (DB/E/1-2), and a full transcription and analysis
would be an essential step in reconstructing the farm's boundaries, acreage, and
tenancy arrangements at this critical juncture. In particular, the survey may
contain information about the relationship between the farmhouse and the
surrounding land — whether they were treated as a single holding or as separate
parcels — that would bear directly on the "Separate Plot Hypothesis" that has
been advanced in connection with the twentieth-century litigation.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The full text of the 1824 David Stewart survey (DB/E/1-2) has not been
   transcribed or analysed in the current record. A detailed examination of the
   survey's description of Great House Farm, its boundaries, its tenant, and its

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The 1820 memorandum of exchanges between Bute and Plymouth has not been

1.5  Lambert Williams: A Williams in Public Office (1817–1828)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   Historical "Cardiff Records" confirm that Lambert Williams was a high-status
   Ward Constable in Cardiff between 1817 and 1828. File D160/1-4 records an
   agreement between Lambert Williams of Cardiff and the Bute Estate (1818–1835),
   demonstrating that a Williams was a recognised legal entity dealing with the
   Bute Estate on a contractual basis.

The appearance of Lambert Williams in the records of this period is one of the
most intriguing clues to the family's history. A Ward Constable was not a mere
village officer; he was a person of substance, responsible for maintaining order,
executing warrants, and representing the parish in its dealings with the county
authorities. To hold such a position, Lambert Williams must have been literate,
propertied, and respected by his neighbours — a man of standing in the community.

The agreement with the Bute Estate (D160/1-4, 1818–1835) suggests that Lambert
Williams was not merely a tenant but a party to a formal contract, possibly
relating to land, services, or some other arrangement. The nature of this
agreement is not specified in the current record, but its existence is
significant: it proves that a Williams was capable of negotiating with the
estate on equal terms, and it raises the possibility that the family's
relationship with the Bute interest was more complex than a simple landlord-
and-tenant arrangement.

The critical question, of course, is whether Lambert Williams was related to the
Williams family of Great House Farm. The name is common in Glamorgan, and
without further genealogical research — parish registers, wills, and property
transactions — the connection cannot be proven. Yet the coincidence of names,
combined with the family's long-standing presence in Llandough and their later
claims of hereditary right, makes the possibility worth pursuing. If Lambert
Williams was indeed a member of the Great House Farm line, then his position as
Ward Constable and his contractual relationship with the Bute Estate would
illuminate the family's social standing in the early nineteenth century and
their dealings with the manorial authorities at a time when the traditional
order was beginning to crumble.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The full text of the Lambert Williams agreement (D160/1-4) has not been
   examined. The nature of the agreement, the lands or services involved, and
   Lambert Williams's relationship to the Great House Farm family are unknown.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] Lambert Williams's date and place of birth, his parents, his
   occupation, and his descendants have not been traced.

1.6  The Chief Rents and the Manorial Rental (1818)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   In 1818, the chief rents of Llandough were transferred to the manorial
   rental.

This seemingly minor administrative change is more significant than it appears.
Chief rents were the annual payments due from freeholders and copyholders within
the manor, and their transfer to the manorial rental indicates a consolidation
of the estate's financial records. For the Williams family, if they were
freeholders or copyholders — as their later claims of ownership would suggest —
the transfer would have meant that their payments were now recorded in the same
ledger as the rents of the ordinary tenants. This may seem a technicality, but
it is precisely such technicalities that would later become the stuff of
litigation: the distinction between a freeholder, a copyholder, a leaseholder,
and a tenant-at-will was not merely legal pedantry but a matter of property,
status, and survival.

The 1818 transfer also suggests that the estate was rationalising its accounts
in preparation for the larger changes that would come with the 1820 exchange and
the 1824 survey. The old manorial courts, with their oral traditions and their
reliance on memory and custom, were giving way to a modern system of written
records, audited accounts, and professional management. The Williams family,
who had lived within the older system for generations, would find themselves
increasingly subject to the new dispensation — a process that would accelerate
in the second half of the nineteenth century and culminate in the legal battles
of the twentieth.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The 1818 manorial rental has not been examined. The specific entries
   for Great House Farm, the amount of the chief rent, and the description of
   the holding are unknown.

1.7  Nonconformity and the Williams Family: The Baptist Registrations
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   On 12 May 1821, Baptist worship was registered in the dwelling house of
   farmer John Gedrych, recorded as being part of the Marquess of Bute's estate.

   On 30 April 1839, a Particular Baptist meeting house was registered by local
   residents including John Williams of Llandough-juxta-Penarth, demonstrating
   continued Williams family presence within the parish.

The Baptist registrations of 1821 and 1839 are among the most precious surviving
documents of the Williams family's religious and social life. They are also
among the most frustrating, for they raise as many questions as they answer.

In 1821, the religious landscape of Wales was undergoing a profound
transformation. The Methodist Revival of the previous century had shaken the
established Church and given rise to a vigorous culture of nonconformist
worship — Baptist, Congregationalist, Methodist, and Presbyterian. In Glamorgan,
where the industrial towns were drawing in migrants from the rural parishes,
the chapels were becoming the centres of a new kind of community life, one that
was independent of the squire and the parson and that fostered a spirit of
self-reliance and democratic organisation. The registration of Baptist worship
in John Gedrych's house in 1821 was part of this movement: it was an assertion
of religious independence, a claim to the right to worship according to one's
own conscience, and a challenge to the authority of the established church.

John Gedrych was a farmer, and his house was presumably one of the larger
dwellings in the parish — large enough to accommodate a congregation. The fact
that the registration was recorded as being "part of the Marquess of Bute's
estate" suggests that the estate was aware of, and perhaps tolerated, this
nonconformist activity. Whether Gedrych was a tenant of the estate, a
freeholder, or a sub-tenant is not clear, but his willingness to open his house
to Baptist worship indicates a degree of independence and a community of
like-minded believers.

The connection to the Williams family is tantalising but unproven. The Williams
family of Great House Farm were, by family testimony, nonconformists — their
later burial preferences (Bethesda Chapel rather than St Dochdwy's) and their
association with chapel activities suggest a long-standing dissenting tradition.
It is entirely possible that John Gedrych's house was a meeting place for the
Williams family and their neighbours, and that the 1821 registration marks the
beginning of a Baptist tradition that would continue in the parish for
generations. Yet without further evidence — the names of those who attended the
meetings, the relationship between Gedrych and the Williams family, and the
subsequent history of the Baptist cause in Llandough — the connection remains a
matter of inference rather than proof.

The 1839 registration is more directly suggestive. It was a "Particular Baptist"
meeting house — Particular Baptists being a Calvinist denomination that
emphasised the doctrine of particular redemption — and it was registered by
"local residents including John Williams of Llandough-juxta-Penarth." The name
John Williams is the same as that of the head of the Great House Farm family in
the late nineteenth century, and the address "Llandough-juxta-Penarth" places
him in the same parish. If this John Williams was indeed the occupier of Great
House Farm, or a close relative, then the 1839 registration provides direct
evidence of the family's nonconformist commitment and their active role in the
religious life of the parish.

The significance of these registrations extends beyond religious history. In the
nineteenth century, chapel membership was a marker of social identity and
political allegiance. Nonconformists were excluded from the universities and
from many public offices, but they were also at the forefront of movements for
reform — for the abolition of slavery, for the extension of the franchise, for
the reform of the poor law, and for the recognition of the rights of tenants
and labourers. The Williams family's association with the Baptist cause, if it
can be established, would place them within this tradition of dissent and
reform, and it would help to explain their later refusal to accept the
authority of the estate and their willingness to fight for what they believed
to be their rights.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The 1821 and 1839 Baptist registration records have not been linked
   definitively to the Great House Farm Williams family line. A full
   examination of the original registrations, including the names of all
   signatories and the relationship between John Gedrych, John Williams, and
   the Great House Farm family, is needed.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The subsequent history of the Baptist cause in Llandough — the
   building of a chapel, the names of ministers, the congregation's
   relationship to the wider Baptist community in Glamorgan — has not been
   traced.

1.8  The Bute Ascendancy: 1824–1850
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   From 1824 to 1926, the Marquess of Bute held the estate. This period of
   102 years represents the longest single tenure of the Llandough lands in
   the modern period.

The Bute ascendancy transformed the landscape of south Wales. The 2nd Marquess
(John Crichton-Stuart, 1793–1848) and his son, the 3rd Marquess (also John
Crichton-Stuart, 1847–1900), were among the greatest landowners in the British
Isles, and their influence on Glamorgan was immense. They built docks at
Cardiff, promoted railways, developed coal mines, and transformed the town from
a small port into one of the greatest coal-exporting centres in the world. The
revenues from these enterprises were staggering: by the late nineteenth century,
the Bute estate was earning millions of pounds a year from coal royalties alone.

For Great House Farm, the Bute ascendancy meant that the land was now held by a
single, immensely powerful owner whose interests were increasingly aligned with
industry rather than agriculture. The farm, with its ancient house and its
mixed farming economy, was an anomaly within an estate that was being reshaped
by the demands of coal and iron. The 1876 severance of 33 acres for the
Llandough Limeworks — which falls outside the 1800–1850 period but was made
possible by the Bute consolidation of 1824 — was the first major incursion of
industry into the farm's territory. It would not be the last.

Yet for the first three decades of the Bute ascendancy, from 1824 to 1850, the
farm appears to have continued much as before. The Williams family remained in
occupation; the fields were cultivated; the house was maintained; and the
rhythms of rural life — ploughing, sowing, harvesting, lambing, the turning of
the seasons — continued undisturbed. The estate surveys and rentals, which
became more systematic under Bute management, record the farm's presence and its
productivity, but they tell us little about the daily lives of the people who
lived there.

What we do know is that the farm was listed in both the "Farm rents" and the
"Cottage rents" sections of the estate accounts between 1845 and 1892/93. This
dual listing is curious: it suggests that the farm was treated partly as an
agricultural holding and partly as a residential property, or that the farmhouse
and the surrounding land were accounted for separately. The "Cottage rents"
entry may refer to the farmhouse itself — the "Great House" — while the "Farm
rents" entry refers to the agricultural land. If so, this separation may have
been the administrative precursor of the "Separate Plot Hypothesis" that would
become so contentious in the twentieth century.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The estate rentals for the period 1824–1850 have not been examined in
   detail. The specific entries for Great House Farm, the rent paid, the
   description of the holding, and the name of the tenant are unknown.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The nature of the "Farm rents" and "Cottage rents" dual listing has
   not been explained. Whether it reflects a genuine separation of the farmhouse
   from the agricultural land, or merely an accounting convention, is unknown.

1.9  The Administrative Reclassification: The First Signs of Downgrading
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de/wh

   Between the 1840s and the 1880s, estate records show the terminology
   gradually changed from "Great House" (Ty Mawr) to "Great House Farm" (Ty
   Mawr Farm) by the time of the 1881 census. This may reflect an
   administrative reclassification from a manorial residence to an agricultural
   holding.

The change in nomenclature — from "Great House" to "Great House Farm" — is one
of the most subtle but most significant developments in the farm's history. A
"Great House" was a manor house, a residence of status and authority, the seat
of a family of substance. A "farm" was a place of work, a productive unit
within an estate, a holding like any other. The change was not merely semantic;
it was a change in the way the property was perceived and classified by the
estate authorities, and it may have had legal and financial consequences.

The reclassification began in the 1840s and was complete by the 1880s. It
coincided with a period of profound change in British agriculture: the repeal of
the Corn Laws in 1846, the influx of cheap grain from North America, the
mechanisation of farming, and the gradual decline of the small mixed farm in
favour of larger, more specialised holdings. For the Bute estate, which was
increasingly focused on the industrial revenues of the coalfield, the
agricultural holdings were becoming less important as sources of income and more
important as potential development land. The reclassification of Great House as
a "farm" rather than a "house" may have been part of a wider process of
rationalisation, in which the estate sought to standardise its holdings and to
reduce the status of properties that did not fit the new pattern.

For the Williams family, the reclassification was a warning of things to come.
If the estate no longer recognised the farmhouse as a residence of status, then
the family's claim to a special position within the manor — a claim that rested
on their long occupation and their belief in their hereditary rights — would be
increasingly difficult to sustain. The 1916 tenancy agreement, which treated the
whole property as a simple agricultural holding, was the logical outcome of this
process; and the litigation of the twentieth century, in which the estate
insisted on its rights as landlord and the family insisted on their rights as
owners, was the final confrontation between two incompatible visions of the
property and its history.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The precise date and context of the change from "Great House" to
   "Great House Farm" in the estate records has not been established. A
   systematic examination of the rentals, surveys, and correspondence for the
   period 1840–1890 is needed.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] Whether the reclassification was a genuine administrative change or a
deliberate downgrading of the property's status remains a matter of debate.

1.10  The Physical Farm: What We Know and What We Do Not
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

By 1850, Great House Farm had stood for more than six centuries, and its
physical form reflected the accumulated adaptations of generations. The core of
the building — the direct-entry sub-medieval house identified by RCAHMW — was
probably of thirteenth-century origin, with thick stone walls, a central hall,
and a steeply pitched roof. Successive additions and alterations had created a
complex structure with wings, outbuildings, and agricultural accommodation that
served the needs of a mixed farming family.

The 1974 architectural survey by H.W. Thomas (or H.J. Thomas — the naming is
inconsistent in the records) provides the most detailed description of the
farmhouse as it stood in the twentieth century, and it is reasonable to suppose
that much of what Thomas recorded in 1974 would have been recognisable to the
Williams family of 1850. The survey noted:

- **Beam A**: narrow chamfered, ogee stops with torus (late seventeenth century)
- **Beam B**: narrow chamfered with rounded stop
- **Beam C**: narrow chamfered, ogee stops

These beams, with their distinctive carpentry, were the structural skeleton of
the house, and they would have supported the floors and the roof that the family
lived beneath. The dairy, the hall, and the kitchen were the principal rooms,
and they would have been the centres of the family's daily life — the dairy for
the processing of milk and cheese, the hall for eating and gathering, the
kitchen for cooking and warmth.

The farm buildings — the barns, stables, and sheds — were equally important.
They housed the animals, stored the grain and hay, and provided workspace for
the farm's operations. The barns were particularly notable: heritage records
state that Welsh boxing champion Jim ("Peerless") Driscoll fought his earliest
contests in the barns of Great House Farm before turning professional in 1901.
Whether this tradition was already established by 1850 is not known, but the
barns were certainly large enough to accommodate such activities, and their
association with sport and community gathering would have been part of the
farm's social role.

The surrounding land — the fields, meadows, and pastures that made up the farm's
holding — was the source of the family's livelihood. The 1824 survey recorded
approximately 166 acres, though the precise boundaries and the use of each field
are not known. The farm was a mixed holding: arable crops (wheat, barley, oats)
for bread and for sale; cattle for milk, meat, and draft; sheep for wool and
meat; and pigs, poultry, and other stock for domestic consumption. The garden
and orchard provided vegetables, fruit, and herbs; the well or spring provided
water; and the woodlands and hedgerows provided fuel, timber, and forage.

This was the world that the Williams family inhabited in 1850: a world of
seasonal labour, of skills passed from father to son, of dependence on the land
and the weather, and of a community bound together by shared work, shared
worship, and shared memory. It was a world that was already under threat — from
the railways, from the factories, from the estates that saw the land as a
commodity to be exploited rather than a heritage to be preserved — but it was
still, in 1850, a living reality.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] No detailed description of the farm's physical appearance in 1850
   survives. The 1974 survey is the closest approximation, but it records the
   house after two centuries of alteration.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The farm's inventory in 1850 — the animals, tools, machinery, and
   equipment — is unknown. No estate valuation or probate inventory for this
   period has been located.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The daily routine of the Williams family in 1850 — the division of

   with neighbouring farms — is entirely undocumented.

1.11  The People of the Farm: A Shadowed Portrait
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For all the documentary evidence that survives from the period 1800–1850, the
individuals who lived at Great House Farm remain shadowy figures. We know the
name Lambert Williams, and we know that a John Williams of Llandough-juxta-
Penarth registered a Baptist meeting house in 1839. We know that the farm was
occupied by a Williams family, and we know that their occupation was hereditary.
But we do not know their faces, their voices, or their stories.

Who was the head of the household in 1800? Was he the son or grandson of the
John Williams who had negotiated the 1667 lease? How many children did he have?
What were their names, their births, their marriages, their deaths? Did they
attend St Dochdwy's Church, or did they worship with the Baptists? Did they send
their children to school, or did they work them in the fields? Did they read
newspapers, vote in elections, belong to friendly societies or trade unions?
These are the questions that a complete history of the farm must answer, and
they are the questions that the current record cannot answer.

The gaps are not merely biographical; they are structural. Without a
reconstructed family tree for the period 1800–1850, it is impossible to trace
the line of inheritance that connects the Williams of 1667 to the Williams of
1916. Without parish registers, census returns, and probate records, it is
impossible to establish the family's size, their occupations, their movements,
and their relationships with the wider community. And without these
foundational facts, the later claims of ownership — claims that rest on the
continuity of family occupation and the transmission of rights from father to
son — cannot be fully evaluated.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The Williams family tree for the period 1800–1850 has not been
   reconstructed. Births, marriages, and deaths have not been traced in parish
   registers or civil registration records.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The 1841, 1851, 1861, 1871, 1881, 1891, 1901, and 1911 census returns
   for Great House Farm have not been examined. These would provide the names,
   ages, occupations, and relationships of the household members.

1.12  The Wider Parish: Llandough in the Age of Reform
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To understand Great House Farm in the period 1800–1850, it is necessary to
understand the parish of Llandough itself. Llandough was, and remains, a small
parish on the coastal plain of Glamorgan, bounded by the River Ely to the north,
the Bristol Channel to the south, and the parishes of Leckwith, Cogan, and
Penarth to the east and west. In 1800, its population was probably no more than
a few hundred, scattered among a handful of farmsteads and cottages.

The parish was dominated by agriculture, but it was not isolated from the wider
world. The road from Cardiff to Barry and the coastal ports ran through or near
the parish, and the growing demand for food from the towns created a market for
the farm's produce. The enclosure of common lands, which had begun in the
eighteenth century and would continue into the nineteenth, reduced the access of
the poor to pasture and fuel, and it increased the dependence of the labouring
population on wage labour. The Poor Law, reformed in 1834, introduced the workhouse
system and transformed the relationship between the parish and its poor.

The religious life of the parish was also changing. The established Church of
England (or, in Wales, the Church of England's Welsh incarnation) remained the
official religion, but nonconformity was growing rapidly. The Baptist
registrations of 1821 and 1839 were part of a wider movement that would
eventually make Wales one of the most nonconformist nations in Europe. The
chapel, rather than the church, would become the centre of community life for
many Welsh families, and the values of self-improvement, education, and social
reform that the chapels fostered would shape the political culture of Wales for
generations.

For the Williams family, these changes were both opportunities and threats. The
growing market for agricultural produce offered the possibility of prosperity;
the enclosure of commons and the rationalisation of estate management threatened
the traditional rights and customs on which their position depended. Their
association with the Baptist cause, if it can be proven, would have placed them
within a network of like-minded families and communities, and it would have
provided them with a moral and spiritual framework for resisting the pressures
of the estate. But it would also have set them apart from the established order,
and it may have made them more vulnerable to the legal and administrative
machinery that the estate could deploy against them.

   [GAP] The population, economy, and social structure of Llandough parish in

1.13  Summary: The State of Knowledge for 1800–1850
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The period 1800–1850 is the documentary threshold of Great House Farm's modern
history. Before 1800, the record is fragmentary: a 1667 lease, a few manorial
references, family tradition, and the physical evidence of the building itself.
After 1850, the record becomes richer: census returns, estate surveys,
newspaper reports, and the accumulating paper trail of the modern bureaucratic
state. The period 1800–1850 sits at the junction of these two worlds, and it is
characterised by a mixture of solid fact, tantalising hints, and frustrating
absences.

**What is established:**

- The Bute/Pembroke estate was the superior landholder from 1552 to 1829, and
the Marquess of Bute held the estate from 1824 to 1926.
- The 1820 memorandum of exchanges and the 1824 David Stewart survey recorded
"Great House Farm alias Cedfin" within the Bute estate.
- Lambert Williams was a Ward Constable in Cardiff (1817–1828) and had a
contractual agreement with the Bute Estate (1818–1835).
- Baptist worship was registered in Llandough in 1821 and 1839, with a John
Williams of Llandough-juxta-Penarth associated with the 1839 registration.
- The chief rents of Llandough were transferred to the manorial rental in 1818.
- The terminology of the estate records began to shift from "Great House" to
"Great House Farm" in the 1840s.

**What is hypothesised:**

- The Williams family of Great House Farm may have been connected to Lambert
Williams and to the John Williams of the 1839 Baptist registration.
- The administrative reclassification of the property may have been a
preliminary to the later downgrading of the family's status.
- The dual listing of the farm in "Farm rents" and "Cottage rents" may reflect
an early separation of the farmhouse from the agricultural land.

**What is missing:**

- The full text of the 1824 David Stewart survey (DB/E/1-2).
- The Lambert Williams agreement (D160/1-4).
- The 1818 manorial rental.
- The original Baptist registration records (1821, 1839).
- The estate rentals for 1824–1850.
- The Williams family tree for 1800–1850.
- Census returns (1841, 1851).
- Wills, inventories, and probate records.
- Tithe maps and awards.
- Enclosure awards.
- Poor law records.
- Trade directories.
- Any description of the farm's daily life, economy, or social relations.

The period 1800–1850 is, in short, a period of transition and consolidation: the
transition from the old manorial order to the modern estate system, and the
consolidation of the Bute interest that would eventually dominate the parish and
set the stage for the conflicts of the twentieth century. It is a period that
requires much more research before it can be written with the fullness and
precision that a complete history of Great House Farm demands.
