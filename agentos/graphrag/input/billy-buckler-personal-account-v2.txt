THE PERSONAL ACCOUNT OF WILLIAM "BILLY" BUCKLER
Great House Farm, Llandough
14 March 1983 – 24 March 1989

14 March 1983

My mam Mary Williams died four years before they took the farm.
She was a disabled widowed pensioner who had fought BP and their
predecessors for thirty-three years. She died during the two weeks
she had to challenge BP registering our home in their names.

She contested BP's 1974 claim for possession despite no ownership ruling.
Their court later based their ruling on a 1983 Land Registry registration
that failed to mention her claim or her long standing occupancy.
Instead they alleged she was their tenant, which was a lie.

They based their ruling on a thirteen-year-old unlicenced BP licence offer
designed to portray BP as owner and her as tenant. She rejected their claim
and her name was not even on the letter. It was only produced after BP lost to her in 1974.

To this day our ownership claim has never been ruled against, sold by us
or extinguished. No court has ever ruled BP own the land. Their courts
have only ruled in favour of themselves taking possession from us.
It was heads they win, tails we lose.

29 April 1988

The bailiffs returned today with dozens of police.
My heart was bad but I dared not leave for hospital in case they took the house.
The officers said I hurt them with my words.
The South Wales Echo reported I only defended my home.

11 May 1988

Five bailiffs and about twenty police arrived to keep the peace, they said.
I blocked the drive with the tractor, locked every door and barred every window.
They smashed the gate lock and came at the doors with poleaxes.
My three infant children Hywel, Rhys and Amy were right behind the door.

I started the chainsaw and pushed it through the doorway.
They backed off then. My pregnant wife Branwen fled with the children
from what the papers called a warzone battleground siege on our home.
I told the reporters: I am here to stay and I will fight this to the death.

This farm has been in the family for nearly four hundred years.
They have got no documents for it. If it is theirs, why have I never
paid a penny in rent for it in my life? I remain adamant I am the owner
despite any court ruling in favour of BP Properties.

29 November 1988

My heart gave way and I woke up in Llandough Hospital.
It must have been around the time I drove through a bailiff's van
blocking my driveway. Police stood guard and arrested me without charge
while photographs and papers were taken from our home.

30 November 1988

Before dawn the bailiffs smashed their way in.
Branwen and the children were turned out into the cold.
Power and telephone were cut. Our tenants Paul Groves and Rod Gotze were evicted.
Our possessions were stripped and half the roof torn away.

1 December 1988

The High Court gave us breathing space with an injunction.
Branwen tried serving the writ; the bailiffs threw her about
and dislocated her shoulder. She still called it a small victory.
We were allowed back only long enough to gather what little we could carry.

3 December 1988

They brought me from my hospital bed to face assault charges on two bailiffs.
Cadw was considering whether Great House Farm should be saved.
We knew if the application failed on Monday the bulldozers would come.
I had been in hospital for three days since the eviction.

5 December 1988

They came. Before breakfast the bulldozers flattened the farmhouse.
I was kept half a mile away, forced to watch generations of my family
disappear into dust. My children stood homeless.
My young son asked how Father Christmas would find us without a chimney.

6 December 1988

The machines carried on and nobody stopped them.
The papers called it "Bulldozed Before Breakfast".
Aunt Janet Harris spoke out and a councillor called it disgusting.
Branwen stood among the rubble with the children as though a war had passed.

9 December 1988

The village protested against what BP had done.
I was released on bail on 13 December and walked back to broken stone.
Thirty thousand pounds' worth of our lives had been taken from the site.
I told the South Wales Echo I owned only the clothes I stood up in.

6 January 1989

My bail conditions were eased a little.
I remained in Penarth, barred from my land, while strangers walked
where my family had lived for centuries. The April charge was later
dropped as out of time at Barry magistrates court.

Late January 1989

At Barry magistrates court the prosecutor Mr Jeff Clarke told the bench
the April summons was legally out of time and could not proceed.
I was living in Penarth under court order not to go within half a mile
of the Great House Farm site. They could not touch me for April.

3 February 1989

I was due in court to face charges of assault and wanton
and furious driving arising from the events at the farm in November.
These were the charges they had held over me to force my hand.
I walked in knowing they held every card in the deck.

11 February 1989

The council ordered BP to clear the site they had made a battleground.
Bulldozers had flattened the building and now the council wanted it tidy.
The legal battle was over but the insult continued.
They erased us and then demanded the mess be swept away.

20 March 1989

Lorries moved onto the site at half past seven in the morning.
W. J. Davies, the biggest contractor around, carted the last stones
of Great House Farm away to a refuse tip. I wrote in my notebook
that a thousand years of history was being lost on a tip.

23 March 1989

I stood in Cardiff court while my machinery was loaded for scrap.
The police brought the case again and threatened me with three years.
Under that pressure I admitted two of five charges against the bailiffs.
I left with a £375 fine and ten days in jail already served.

24 March 1989

They had taken the farm but they had not taken me.
Branwen was expecting Peris, our fourth child.
I bought an old bus called Sarge and turned it into our home.
Parked on borrowed land we began again, carrying the memory with us.
