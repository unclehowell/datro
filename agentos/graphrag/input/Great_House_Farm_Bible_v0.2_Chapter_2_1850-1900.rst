2.1  The Farm at Mid-Century: An Agricultural Holding in an Industrial Age
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The year 1850 found Great House Farm at a turning point. The Bute consolidation
of 1824 had placed the estate under a single, powerful owner whose interests
were increasingly aligned with the industrial transformation of Glamorgan. The
Marquess of Bute — the 2nd Marquess until 1848, then the 3rd Marquess, John
Crichton-Stuart, who would become one of the greatest landowners in British
history — was building docks at Cardiff, promoting railways, and developing coal
mines. The revenues from these enterprises would eventually dwarf the income
from agriculture, and the estate's attitude toward its farms would shift
accordingly: from paternalistic landlordism to detached, profit-oriented
management.

For the Williams family, the 1850s and 1860s were a period of relative stability
punctuated by the distant rumblings of change. The farm continued to operate as
a mixed holding: arable crops on the lighter soils, pasture for cattle and
sheep, meadow for hay, and the orchard and garden providing fruit and vegetables
for the household. The house itself, with its thick stone walls and steep roof,
was a fortress against the Welsh weather, and the barns and outbuildings
provided shelter for animals, storage for grain, and workspace for the seasonal
labour of threshing, shearing, and cheese-making.

The parish of Llandough was changing around the farm. The population was
gradually increasing, drawn by the expanding industries of Cardiff and Barry.
The railway from Cardiff to Barry would eventually cut through the parish,
bringing with it new residents, new markets, and new pressures on the land. The
old self-sufficient economy of the rural parish — in which each farm produced
most of what it needed and traded the surplus in local markets — was giving way
to a more specialised, market-oriented system in which the farm's produce was
sold to the growing towns and its labour was supplemented by hired hands from
the neighbouring cottages.

Yet in 1850, these changes were still in their early stages. The Williams family
remained the dominant presence in the parish, and Great House Farm remained the
largest and most ancient holding. The family's belief in their hereditary right
to the property — a belief that would sustain them through the legal battles of
the next century — was already well established, and it was reinforced by the
physical evidence of their long occupation: the ancient beams, the worn
flagstones, the barns that had sheltered generations of animals, and the fields
whose names and boundaries were known by heart.

2.2  The 1870 Discovery: A Roman Soldier Under the Floor
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: ft

   Family testimony, repeated in contemporary press, records the discovery
   around 1870 of a Roman soldier under the living-room / dining-room floor of
   Ty Mawr while the Williams family was replacing stone flagstones with a
   timber floor. The soldier was reportedly found in a stone-lined grave
   (cist), wearing bronze or iron armour, with horse, lance and shield. No
   contemporary nineteenth-century written source has yet been located to
   corroborate this account beyond later press reports that themselves rely on
   family recollection.

The discovery of 1870 is one of the most remarkable episodes in the long
history of Great House Farm, and it is also one of the most frustratingly
elusive. According to family tradition, which was repeated in newspaper reports
of the late twentieth century, the Williams family were replacing the stone
flagstones of their dining-room floor with a timber floor when they uncovered
the body of a Roman soldier. He was not merely a skeleton: he was fully
armoured, buried with his horse, his lance, and his shield, in a stone-lined
grave (a cist) that had been hidden beneath the flagstones for more than a
thousand years.

The story is extraordinary, and it raises a host of questions. Was the soldier
buried there during the Roman occupation of Britain, in the second to fourth
centuries A.D.? Or was he buried later, in some post-Roman or early medieval
ritual that reused Roman artefacts? Was the grave deliberately placed beneath
the floor of what would become the farmhouse, or was it a coincidence — a burial
that happened to lie on the site of a later building? And why was he buried
with his horse, a practice that was rare in Roman Britain and that suggests a
high-status individual, perhaps a cavalry officer or a tribal chieftain?

The archaeological context makes the story at least plausible. The Glamorgan-
Gwent Archaeological Trust (GGAT) excavations of 1978–1980 confirmed the
existence of a Roman villa on the site, dating from the second to fourth
centuries A.D., with walls that were later reused for a twelfth or
thirteenth-century barn. The confirmed Roman villa lies immediately adjacent to
the farmhouse footprint, making the reported nineteenth-century discovery of
Roman remains beneath the house geographically plausible. If the villa extended
beneath the area of the later farmhouse — as villa complexes often did, with the
main residence surrounded by outbuildings, workshops, and burial grounds — then
the discovery of a burial under the floor is entirely consistent with the
archaeological evidence.

Yet the absence of a contemporary written record is a serious obstacle. The
1870s were a period of intense interest in Roman Britain, and the discovery of a
Roman soldier in armour would have been a sensational find, reported in the
local and national press and investigated by antiquarians and archaeologists.
The fact that no such report has been found — despite searches in newspaper
archives, county histories, and antiquarian journals — suggests either that the
discovery was not reported at the time (perhaps because the family wished to
keep it quiet, or because they did not recognise its significance), or that the
family tradition has been embellished over time, and that the original discovery
was less dramatic than the later accounts suggest.

The artefacts themselves — the armour, the horse's remains, the lance, the
shield — have not been located. If they were removed from the grave in 1870,
they may have been kept by the family, sold to a collector, or dispersed among
local museums. The National Museum of Wales holds medieval ironwork from the
site, but the Roman material, if it exists, has not been identified. The
possibility that the "armour" was not Roman at all but medieval — a
misidentification by the family or the press — is raised by the Heneb/Archwilio
record, which notes that "armour" found under the rear-wing floor around 1880
was later identified with medieval ironwork at the National Museum.

.. class:: gap

2.3  The 1876 Carve-Out: Thirty-Three Acres for Industry
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   In 1876, the Bute Estate recorded the severance of 33 acres formerly part
   of Great House Farm for the Llandough Limeworks (Glamorgan Archives
   reference D153/4-8). The limeworks operated from 1876 to 1912.

The year 1876 marked a watershed in the history of Great House Farm. For more
than two centuries, the farm had been a single, coherent holding: the farmhouse,
the outbuildings, and the surrounding fields, all managed as a unit, all
occupied by the same family, all subject to the same manorial and estate
jurisdiction. The 1876 severance broke this unity. Thirty-three acres — a
significant portion of the farm's total holding — were carved out for industrial
use, and the landscape of Llandough was permanently altered.

The Llandough Limeworks were part of the industrial expansion that was
transforming the coastal plain of Glamorgan. Limestone was a vital raw material
for the building industry, for agriculture (as a soil improver), and for the
manufacture of cement and mortar. The Llandough limestone was of high quality,
and the limeworks that exploited it were well connected to the railway network
that was being built across south Wales. Contemporary advertisements described
D. Thomas & Son as manufacturers of "widely celebrated" Llandough lime, and they
claimed that their product was used in the New General Post Office in St
Martin's Le Grand, London; in waterworks; in mines; in railways; and in
collieries. The lime was advertised as possessing cementitious qualities
approaching those of Portland cement — a claim that suggests a sophisticated
manufacturing process and a substantial market.

The severance was recorded in the Bute Estate papers (D153/4-8), and it was
presumably accompanied by a formal deed or agreement that conveyed the 33 acres
to the limeworks operators. The Williams family, as the occupiers of the farm,
would have been affected by the severance in several ways. They lost a
significant portion of their agricultural land, reducing the farm's productivity
and its value. They gained new neighbours — the limeworks, with its quarries,
its kilns, its railway sidings, and its workforce — who transformed the rural
character of the parish. And they were exposed to the dust, noise, and
environmental degradation that industrial operations inevitably brought.

Yet the severance may also have created an opportunity. According to family
testimony, the Williams family later reached an agreement with Daniel Thomas &
Son, the operators of the limeworks, under which the quarrying was allowed in
return for eventual title to the remaining land. This agreement — the "Daniel
Thomas arrangement" — is one of the most contentious elements of the farm's
later history, for it is the basis of the family's claim that they acquired
absolute title (freehold) to the farmhouse and garden plot after 1876, while the
surrounding farmland remained on lease.

.. class:: de/ft/wh

   Family testimony records a later agreement with Daniel Thomas & Son under
   which quarrying rights were exchanged for eventual title; the deeds are now
   missing. Post-1876, the family is believed to have gained absolute title /
   freehold to the house and garden plot, with surrounding farmland remaining
   on lease.

The Daniel Thomas arrangement is shrouded in mystery. The key deeds are missing
— removed, lost, or destroyed, according to family testimony — and the only
evidence of the agreement is the family's own recollection, reinforced by a
"tree-planting ceremony" that is said to have marked the transition. The
ceremony is a powerful symbol: the planting of a tree is a traditional act of
possession, a way of marking the land as one's own, and the family's memory of
it suggests that they believed, at the time, that they were acquiring a
permanent right to the property. But belief is not proof, and without the deeds
— or some other documentary evidence of the agreement — the family's claim
remains a working hypothesis rather than an established fact.

The timing of the arrangement is also unclear. The severance took place in 1876,
but the family testimony refers to a "later agreement" with Daniel Thomas & Son.
Was the agreement made immediately after the severance, or did it follow some
years later, after the limeworks had been operating for a time? The phrase
"eventual title" suggests that the transfer was not immediate but conditional —
perhaps dependent on the completion of the quarrying, or on the payment of some
consideration, or on the satisfaction of some other requirement. The ambiguity
of the family's account — "eventual title," "later agreement," "the deeds are
now missing" — is itself a source of frustration and suspicion: if the agreement
was genuine, why are the deeds missing? If the family believed they had acquired
title, why did they continue to pay rent for the surrounding farmland?

The Heneb/Archwilio record (HER GGAT02038s) notes that the 1974 H.J.T. survey
was incomplete due to access problems from the ownership dispute, and that the
plan remained unpublished. This suggests that the physical evidence of the
farm's layout — which might have clarified the relationship between the
farmhouse and the severed land — was never fully recorded.

2.4  The Adamsdown Square Lease: A Williams in Cardiff (July 1877)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   In July 1877, the Glamorgan Record Office records a counterpart lease from
   the Trustees of the late Marquess of Bute to John Williams relating to
   Adamsdown Square, Cardiff (DBDT catalogue).

The Adamsdown Square lease of 1877 is a small but significant clue to the
Williams family's activities in the late nineteenth century. Adamsdown Square
was a residential area in the east of Cardiff, close to the docks and the
growing industrial districts, and a lease there suggests that John Williams —
whoever he was — had interests beyond the farm. He may have been a property
investor, acquiring urban leases as a source of income. He may have been a
relative of the Great House Farm family, using his connection to the estate to
obtain favourable terms. Or he may have been an entirely unrelated John
Williams, one of the many Welshmen of that name who were moving to the towns in
search of work and opportunity.

The lease is recorded in the DBDT catalogue, but its full text has not been
examined. The terms — the rent, the duration, the purpose — are unknown, and
the relationship between the John Williams of Adamsdown Square and the John
Williams of Great House Farm has not been established. Yet the coincidence of
names, combined with the family's later claims of a hereditary connection to the
Bute Estate, makes the possibility of a link worth investigating. If the John
Williams of Adamsdown Square was indeed a member of the Great House Farm
family, then the lease would demonstrate that the family was not merely rural
tenants but urban property-holders as well, with a foot in both the agricultural
and the industrial economies of late Victorian Glamorgan.

2.5  The Administrative Reclassification Complete: From "Great House" to
     "Great House Farm"
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de/wh

   Between the 1840s and the 1880s, estate records show the terminology
   gradually changed from "Great House" (Ty Mawr) to "Great House Farm" (Ty
   Mawr Farm). By the time of the 1881 census and the 1897 Marconi
   experiments, the house was firmly referred to as "Great House Farm" in
   press reports and official documents.

The change in nomenclature, which had begun in the 1840s, was complete by the
1880s. The farm was now "Great House Farm" in the estate records, in the census
returns, in the press reports, and in the everyday speech of the parish. The old
name "Ty Mawr" — the Great House — survived in family memory and in local
tradition, but it was no longer the official designation.

The significance of this change has been debated. Was it a genuine
administrative reclassification, reflecting the estate's modernisation of its
records and its recognition that the property was primarily an agricultural
holding rather than a manorial residence? Or was it a deliberate downgrading, a
way of reducing the property's status and preparing the ground for its eventual
treatment as a mere tenancy? The estate's perspective would presumably be the
former: the name was changed to reflect reality, not to manipulate it. The
family's perspective — informed by their later experience of litigation and
dispossession — would presumably be the latter: the name was changed to erase
the memory of their ancient rights and to reduce them to the status of ordinary
tenants.

The truth probably lies somewhere between these two positions. The estate was
certainly modernising its records in the late nineteenth century, and the
standardisation of farm names was part of this process. But the change also had
practical consequences: a "farm" was a unit of agricultural production, subject
to the Agricultural Holdings Act and the ordinary rules of landlord and tenant;
a "Great House" was a residence of status, potentially subject to different
legal rules and different expectations. The reclassification may not have been
deliberately hostile, but it was not neutral either: it shifted the legal and
perceptual framework within which the family's rights were understood, and it
made it easier for the estate to treat the property as a simple commercial
holding.

The dual listing of the farm in both "Farm rents" and "Cottage rents" sections
of the estate accounts (from 1845 to 1892/93) adds another layer of complexity.
If the farmhouse was listed separately from the agricultural land, then the
estate was already treating the two components of the holding as distinct
entities — a separation that would become the central issue of the twentieth-
century litigation.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The precise date and context of the final change from "Great House"
   to "Great House Farm" in the estate records has not been established. The
   1881 census entry for the property has not been examined.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The estate's internal correspondence and memoranda regarding the
   reclassification have not been located. Was the change discussed at estate
   meetings? Was it approved by the Marquess or his agents? Was it connected
   to any broader policy of estate rationalisation?

2.6  Frederick Buckler: A New Family Line (1888)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   Frederick Buckler was born in 1888. He would later marry Mary Williams,
   the daughter of the Great House Farm family, and their son William
   ("Billy") Buckler would become the last occupier of the farm.

The birth of Frederick Buckler in 1888 is a small but significant event in the
family history, for it marks the beginning of the Buckler line that would
eventually succeed the Williams line as the occupiers and claimants of Great
House Farm. The Buckler family were, according to family tradition, a Glamorgan
farming branch distinct from the "Buckleriana" architectural line of Dorset —
a family of builders and architects whose work can be traced in the churches and
houses of southern England. The connection between the Glamorgan Bucklers and
the Dorset Bucklers, if any, has not been established, and the family's origins
in Wales remain obscure.

Frederick Buckler's early life is not recorded in the current materials. Where
he was born, who his parents were, how he was educated, and how he came to meet
Mary Williams are all unknown. The marriage of Frederick and Mary — which must
have taken place around 1910–1915, given that their son Billy was born during
or shortly after the First World War — united two farming families and brought
the Buckler name to Great House Farm. Mary Williams, although married, retained
her maiden name in daily life — a practice that would later become a source of
confusion and contention in the legal proceedings, as the courts and the estate
referred to her as "Mrs Buckler" while she continued to identify herself as
"Mrs Williams."

2.7  The 1891 Goose Theft: A Williams in the News
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: de

   On 11 November 1891, Robert Williams, a mason's labourer, was charged with
   stealing three geese, the property of Mr John Williams, Great House Farm,
   Llandough. The case was reported in the local press and confirms a John
   Williams in occupation of the farm at that date.

The goose theft of 1891 is one of the few documented events of the late
nineteenth century that places a named Williams at Great House Farm with
independent corroboration. The case was a minor one — a farmyard theft, tried
in the local magistrates' court — but its reporting in the press provides a
snapshot of the farm's operations and its place in the local economy. Geese
were valuable property: they provided meat, feathers, and eggs, and a flock of
geese was a significant asset for a mixed farm. The theft of three geese was
not a trivial matter, and the prosecution suggests that John Williams was
prepared to assert his rights against petty crime.

The identity of the John Williams of 1891 is not established. He may have been
the same John Williams who registered the Baptist meeting house in 1839, or he
may have been his son or grandson. He may have been the John Williams who held
the Adamsdown Square lease in 1877, or he may have been a different man
entirely. The name John Williams was common in Glamorgan, and without further
genealogical research — parish registers, census returns, and probate records —
the connection cannot be proven. But the fact that a John Williams was in
occupation of Great House Farm in 1891, and that he was prosperous enough to
own geese and to prosecute their theft, confirms the family's continued
presence and their continued status as substantial farmers.

The case also provides a glimpse of the farm's social context. Robert Williams,
the accused, was a "mason's labourer" — a building worker, not an agricultural
labourer — which suggests that he was not a regular employee of the farm but an
outsider, perhaps a casual worker or a passer-by. The fact that he was charged
with theft rather than with some lesser offence suggests that the case was taken
seriously, and that John Williams had the standing and the resources to pursue
it through the courts.

   [GAP] The full report of the 1891 goose theft case has not been located.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The identity of John Williams of Great House Farm in 1891 — his
   relationship to the earlier and later Williams generations, his age, his
   family, and his other activities — has not been established.

2.8  The Daniel Thomas Arrangement: A Family Belief in Title (1895–1905)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: ft/wh

   Between 1895 and 1905, family testimony states that Daniel Thomas & Son
   made an agreement with the Williams family whereby quarrying was allowed in
   return for eventual title. A tree-planting ceremony marks this transition.
   The key deeds are now missing.

The Daniel Thomas arrangement, which has already been mentioned in connection
with the 1876 severance, falls within the period 1895–1905 according to family
testimony. This dating is significant, for it suggests that the agreement was
not made immediately after the severance but some twenty years later — a delay
that raises questions about the circumstances of the negotiation and the reasons
for the family's continued occupation of the farmhouse while the limeworks
operated on the severed land.

If the arrangement was made in the 1890s, it coincided with a period of
significant change in the British agricultural economy. The "Great Depression"
of British agriculture, which had begun in the 1870s with the influx of cheap
grain from North America, was at its deepest in the 1890s. Farm incomes were
falling, tenants were defaulting on their rents, and estates were struggling to
maintain their revenues. The Bute Estate, whose wealth was increasingly derived
from coal rather than from agriculture, may have been willing to make
concessions to its agricultural tenants in order to maintain occupancy and to
avoid the costs of eviction and re-letting.

The family's account of the arrangement — quarrying allowed in return for
"eventual title" — suggests a bargain that was favourable to the Williams
family. They would gain ownership of the farmhouse and garden plot, while the
estate would gain the right to exploit the limestone on the severed land. The
tree-planting ceremony, which the family remembers as marking the transition,
would have been a symbolic act of possession, a way of making the transfer
visible and memorable. But without the deeds, the agreement remains a matter of
family belief rather than legal proof, and the estate's records (if they
survive) may tell a different story.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The precise date of the Daniel Thomas arrangement is not known. The

   month and day — are not recorded.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The terms of the arrangement — the consideration, the conditions, the

2.9  The Marconi Experiments: A Moment of Fame (13 May 1897)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. class:: ef

   On 13 May 1897, Guglielmo Marconi conducted wireless telegraphy experiments
   between Lavernock Point and Flat Holm. The first message was "CAN YOU HEAR
   ME," replied to by George Kemp: "YES LOUD AND CLEAR."

.. class:: de

   A contemporary newspaper report of the experiments names "Mr. Williams (of
   the engineering department, Cardiff)" as the person who fixed a 120-foot
   pole with a zinc cylinder on the summit of Lavernock Point.

.. class:: ft/wh

   Family tradition asserts that a Williams family member assisted with the
   mast and that Marconi was a guest at Great House Farm. No direct
   documentary evidence placing Marconi himself at the farm has yet been
   located.

The Marconi experiments of 13 May 1897 were a landmark in the history of
wireless communication, and they brought a moment of international fame to the
quiet coastline of south Wales. Guglielmo Marconi, the Italian inventor who
would later win the Nobel Prize for his work on radio, had come to Lavernock
Point to test his apparatus across the Bristol Channel, to the island of Flat
Holm. The experiment was a success: the first message, "CAN YOU HEAR ME," was
answered with a clear "YES LOUD AND CLEAR," and the age of wireless
telegraphy had begun.

For Great House Farm, the Marconi experiments were a moment of unexpected
connection to the wider world. The contemporary newspaper report — which has
been located in the press archives — names "Mr. Williams (of the engineering
department, Cardiff)" as the person who prepared the experimental site: he
"fixed upon Lavernock Point a pole 120 feet high, with a zinc cylinder at the
summit, 5ft. 6in. by 4ft., insulated from the Flat Holm and Brean..." This Mr.
Williams was an employee of the Post Office engineering department, and his role
was to erect the mast and to ensure that the electrical equipment was properly
installed and insulated.

The family tradition goes further. According to the Williams and Buckler
families, the Mr. Williams who assisted Marconi was a member of the Great House
Farm family, and Marconi himself was a guest at the farm during his stay in the
area. The story is a cherished part of the family's heritage: it connects them
to one of the great moments of modern scientific history, and it reinforces
their sense of the farm's importance as a place of welcome and distinction. But
the story is also unsupported by direct documentary evidence. The newspaper
report names "Mr. Williams" but does not identify him further; it does not say
that he was from Great House Farm, or that he was related to the Williams
family of Llandough. And no record has been found of Marconi staying at the
farm — no hotel register, no letter, no diary entry, no photograph.

The search for primary evidence has been extensive and, so far, unsuccessful.
The Post Office engineering records, the Marconi Company archives, the Cardiff
University Special Collections, and the RCAHMW correspondence files have all
been searched, and none has produced a document that links the "Mr. Williams"
of the 1897 experiments to the Great House Farm family. The Science Museum has
been asked to conduct fuller name-based searches, but the outcome is not yet
known. The possibility remains that the Mr. Williams of Lavernock Point was an
entirely different person — a Post Office engineer from Cardiff, perhaps with
no connection to Llandough at all — and that the family's tradition is a
pious fiction, a way of claiming a share in a glorious moment that was not
theirs.

Yet the possibility also remains that the tradition is true, and that the
evidence has simply not been found. The Post Office engineering department in
Cardiff was a small world, and it is not implausible that a member of the
Williams family — perhaps a son or nephew who had left the farm for an urban
occupation — should have been employed there. The farm was only a few miles from
Lavernock Point, and it would have been a natural place for Marconi to stay if
he needed accommodation in the area. And the family's memory of the event —
passed down through generations, reinforced by local pride — is not easily
dismissed.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The identity of "Mr. Williams (of the engineering department,

2.10  The Physical Farm at Century's End
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

By 1900, Great House Farm had stood for nearly seven centuries, and its
physical form reflected the accumulated adaptations of generations. The
farmhouse, with its sub-medieval core and its later additions, was a substantial
building of stone and timber, with a steep roof, multiple chimneys, and a
complex plan that included the dairy, the hall, the kitchen, and the living
rooms where the family ate, slept, and gathered. The six chimneys — a feature
that would later make the house one of the most distinctive buildings in
Llandough — were already in place, and they would have been a landmark visible
for miles across the coastal plain.

The outbuildings — the barns, stables, and sheds — were equally substantial.
The barns, in particular, were large enough to accommodate community gatherings,
and their association with sport (the Jim Driscoll boxing tradition) and with
social events (the chapel fetes and barn dances) made them an important part of
the parish's social life. The farmyard, with its cobbled or packed-earth
surface, would have been the centre of the farm's daily operations: the
milking of cows, the feeding of pigs and poultry, the storage of carts and
implements, and the comings and goings of workers, visitors, and delivery men.

The surrounding land — the fields, meadows, and pastures — had been reduced by
the 1876 severance but still comprised a significant agricultural holding. The
fields would have been enclosed by hedgerows, ditches, or walls, and they would
have been cultivated in rotation: arable crops one year, pasture the next, fallow
the third. The well or spring that supplied the farm with water would have been
a vital resource, and its location — whether near the house or in one of the
fields — would have determined the layout of the farmyard and the garden.

The farm's equipment — the ploughs, harrows, carts, and tools — would have been
a mixture of traditional hand tools and the new machines that were beginning to
appear in the late nineteenth century: the reaping machine, the steam-powered
thresher, the improved breeds of seed and stock. The Williams family, as
substantial farmers, would have been among the first in the parish to adopt
new techniques, and their farm would have been a model of progressive
agriculture — or so they would have hoped.

   [GAP] No detailed architectural description of the farmhouse as it stood in

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The farm's inventory in 1900 — the animals, tools, machinery, and

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The condition of the farm buildings in 1900 — their state of repair,

2.11  The People of the Farm: A Partial Portrait
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For all the documentary evidence that survives from the period 1850–1900, the
individuals who lived at Great House Farm remain only partially visible. We know
the name John Williams — the occupier of 1891, the possible Baptist of 1839, the
possible lessee of Adamsdown Square in 1877 — but we do not know whether these
names refer to one man or to several. We know that Frederick Buckler was born
in 1888 and that he would later marry Mary Williams, but we do not know his
parents or his early life. We know that a Roman soldier was (reportedly)
discovered under the floor around 1870, but we do not know who discovered him,
or what became of him, or how the family reacted to the find.

The gaps are not merely biographical; they are structural. Without a
reconstructed family tree for the period 1850–1900, it is impossible to trace
the line of inheritance that connects the Williams of 1667 to the Williams of
1916. Without census returns, parish registers, and probate records, it is
impossible to establish the family's size, their occupations, their movements,
and their relationships with the wider community. And without these
foundational facts, the later claims of ownership — claims that rest on the
continuity of family occupation and the transmission of rights from father to
son — cannot be fully evaluated.

What we do know is that the family was substantial enough to prosecute a goose
theft in 1891, to negotiate (or believe they had negotiated) a title agreement
with Daniel Thomas & Son in the 1890s, and to assist (or believe they had
assisted) with the Marconi experiments in 1897. These are not the activities of
a marginal or impoverished family; they are the activities of a family with
standing, with resources, and with a sense of their own importance. Whether that
sense was justified by legal right or by customary belief is the question that
would dominate the family's history in the twentieth century.

.. class:: gap

2.12  The Wider Parish: Llandough at the Fin de Siècle
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To understand Great House Farm in the period 1850–1900, it is necessary to
understand the parish of Llandough at the end of the nineteenth century. The
parish was no longer the isolated rural community it had been in 1800. The
railway from Cardiff to Barry — opened in the 1880s — had brought new residents,
new industries, and new opportunities. The coastal plain was being developed for
housing, and the old agricultural economy was giving way to a suburban economy
of commuters, shopkeepers, and service workers.

The limeworks, which had begun operations in 1876, were a major employer in the
parish, and they brought with them a workforce of quarrymen, kilnmen, and
labourers who lived in the new cottages that were built around the works. The
old farmsteads — Great House Farm, Cogan Pill, Leckwith — were now islands of
agriculture in a sea of industry and housing, and their inhabitants looked
increasingly like relics of a bygone age. The young people of the parish were
leaving the land for the towns, and the farms were becoming dependent on hired
labour from outside.

The religious life of the parish was also changing. The Baptist cause, which
had been registered in 1821 and 1839, may have grown into a chapel congregation
by the end of the century, or it may have declined as the population became more
mobile and more diverse. The established church of St Dochdwy, with its ancient
burial ground and its medieval fabric, remained the parish church, but its
congregation was probably smaller and older than it had been in the early
nineteenth century. The chapel, rather than the church, may have become the
centre of religious life for the Williams family, and their nonconformist
identity may have become more pronounced as the parish became more secular.

For the Williams family, these changes were both opportunities and threats. The
growing market for agricultural produce from the towns offered the possibility
of prosperity; the competition from imported food and the pressure from
development threatened the farm's viability. The family's association with the
Baptist cause, if it continued, would have provided them with a moral community
and a network of support, but it would also have set them apart from the
established order and made them more vulnerable to the legal and administrative
machinery that the estate could deploy against them.

   [GAP] The population, economy, and social structure of Llandough parish in

   awards, poor law records, and trade directories for this period have not
   been examined.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The relationship between the Williams family and the other inhabitants
   of Llandough — their neighbours, their employees, their creditors and
   debtors — is unknown.

.. class:: gap

   [GAP] The impact of the Llandough Limeworks on the parish's economy,

2.13  Summary: The State of Knowledge for 1850–1900
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The period 1850–1900 is the era in which the modern crisis of Great House Farm
began to take shape. The 1876 severance broke the unity of the farm and created
the conditions for the Separate Plot Hypothesis. The Daniel Thomas arrangement
— if it occurred — transformed the family's legal position and set the stage for
the litigation of the twentieth century. The Marconi experiments brought a
moment of fame and reinforced the family's sense of their own importance. And
the administrative reclassification of the property — from "Great House" to
"Great House Farm" — shifted the perceptual framework within which the family's
rights were understood.

**What is established:**

- The Bute Estate severed 33 acres from Great House Farm in 1876 for the
  Llandough Limeworks (D153/4-8).
- The limeworks operated from 1876 to 1912 and produced "widely celebrated"
  Llandough lime for national markets.
- A counterpart lease to John Williams for Adamsdown Square, Cardiff, was
  recorded in July 1877 (DBDT catalogue).
- By the 1880s–1890s, the estate records referred to the property as "Great
  House Farm" rather than "Great House."
- Frederick Buckler was born in 1888 and later married Mary Williams.
- On 11 November 1891, John Williams of Great House Farm prosecuted a goose
  theft in the local press.
- Between 1895 and 1905, family testimony records a Daniel Thomas arrangement
  for quarrying rights in return for eventual title.
- On 13 May 1897, Marconi conducted wireless experiments at Lavernock Point,
  with a "Mr. Williams (of the engineering department, Cardiff)" assisting.

**What is hypothesised:**

- The Williams family may have acquired absolute title / freehold to the
  farmhouse and garden plot after 1876, while the surrounding farmland remained
  on lease.
- The "Mr. Williams" of the 1897 Marconi experiments may have been a member of
  the Great House Farm family.
- The administrative reclassification from "Great House" to "Great House Farm"
  may have been a deliberate downgrading of the property's status.

**What is missing:**

- The full text of the 1876 severance deed (D153/4-8).
- The Daniel Thomas agreement — its date, terms, parties, and legal effect.
- The missing deeds of the Daniel Thomas arrangement.
- The full text of the July 1877 Adamsdown Square lease.
- The identity of John Williams of Great House Farm in 1891.
- The full report of the 1891 goose theft case.
- The identity of "Mr. Williams (of the engineering department, Cardiff)" in
  1897.
- Direct documentary evidence of Marconi's stay at Great House Farm.
- The Williams family tree for 1850–1900.
- Census returns (1861, 1871, 1881, 1891, 1901).
- Wills, inventories, and probate records.
- Tithe maps and awards.
- Enclosure awards.
- Poor law records.
- Trade directories.
- Estate valuations and farm accounts.
- Any description of the farm's daily life, economy, or social relations.
- The whereabouts of the 1870 Roman discovery artefacts.
- The full 1974 architectural survey report.
- The Heneb/Archwilio GGAT02038s full record.

The period 1850–1900 is, in short, the period in which the ancient holding began
to fracture under the pressures of industry, modernisation, and legal
reclassification. It is a period that requires much more research before it can
be written with the fullness and precision that a complete history of Great
House Farm demands.
