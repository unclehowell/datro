Executive Summary
=================

This document reconstructs the history of **Great House Farm** (Ty Mawr),
Llandough, Vale of Glamorgan, from its medieval origins through to its
demolition in 1988 and the subsequent residential development of the site
(now Church View Close).  It traces the competing claims of ownership
between the **Williams–Buckler family** (who assert continuous occupation
from at least 1667) and the **Bute Estate**, **Western Ground Rents Ltd**,
**BP Pension Trust Ltd** and **BP Properties Ltd**.

The reconstruction draws on:

*   Bute Estate manorial records (NLW D 219, D160/1–4, D153/4–8, DB/E/1–2);
*   Glamorgan Archives holdings (DSA/6/701, DSA/6/1158, DCOWHS/2, DXJF/64/30–31);
*   National Library of Wales (NLW) collections;
*   Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales (RCAHMW)
    records — **14 archive collections** relating to Great House Farm have been
    confirmed;
*   Cotswold Archaeological Trust (now Cotswold Archaeology) excavation reports
    (1994) and earlier GGAT evaluations (1978–1980);
*   Contemporary newspaper coverage (*South Wales Echo*, *Western Mail*);
*   Parliamentary records (Hansard);
*   Court records including *BP Properties Ltd v Buckler* [1987] EWCA Civ 2;
*   European Court of Human Rights application **14464/88**;
*   Family testimony and genealogical research;
*   **LiDAR** survey evidence suggesting a substantially larger archaeological
    complex than previously recognised;
*   Alternative historic place-name forms: **Llandochau** and
    **Llan Doche Penarth**.

A central contention of this reconstruction is that the 1987 Court of Appeal
decision resolved only **possession proceedings** and the effect of a
unilateral licence on adverse possession; it did **not** provide a final
judicial determination of the competing historical title claims.  The
distinction between possession proceedings and title determination has become
much clearer through reconstruction of the litigation chronology.

Chronology
==========

Medieval & Early-Modern Period
------------------------------

.. list-table::
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 15 85

   * - Date
     - Event
   * - 1100
     - The lordship of Llandough was originally granted to the **Walsche**
       family by the Norman conqueror **Robert fitz Hamon** or his
       successors.
   * - 1215
     - **Great House ("Ty Mawr")** built next to **St Dochdwy's Church**,
       Llandough.  Alternative historic names for the settlement include
       **Llandochau** and **Llan Doche Penarth**.
   * - 1215–1539
     - Owned by **Tewkesbury Abbey** / Prior of Cardiff (ecclesiastical title).
       The greater portion continued until the Norman conquest of Glamorgan,
       when the monastery's property was vested in the Benedictine Abbey of
       Tewkesbury.
   * - 1444
     - The manor and lordship of Llandough (and St Mary Church) sold to
       **Sir William Thomas (Herbert) of Raglan**, ancestor of the Earls of
       Pembroke.  The lordship remained in high-status hands for a century.
   * - 1536
     - **Sir Edward Carne** (lawyer and diplomat) purchased the manor from
       the 2nd Earl of Worcester, a Herbert descendant.
   * - 1536–1539
     - **Dissolution of the Monasteries** — the Crown (Henry VIII)
       confiscated monastic lands.
   * - 1543–1770
     - After the Dissolution, the manor passed through Crown grants to lay
       owners.  By the later 16th century it formed part of the
       Herbert/Pembroke orbit and was later absorbed into what became the
       **Bute estate**.
   * - 1552–1829
     - The Bute/Pembroke estate treated itself as superior landholder/manorial
       lord.  The "Llandough manorial leases and agreements" series (NLW D 219,
       Bute Estate Records) shows leases of Llandough Farm (c. 166 acres).
   * - 1667
     - **Williams family in occupation.**  Family claim of acquisition from
       this date.  A 1980s newspaper article quoted Mrs Mary Williams saying
       the farm was "acquired" by the Williams family at that time,
       understood as a purchase rather than a tenancy.  No surviving registered
       title from the 17th century exists; this is evidence of long-term
       possession and family belief in ownership.  The 1667 transaction likely
       involved a "lease for lives" (often three generations) or copyhold of
       inheritance — hereditary in practice, passing father to son.
   * - 1677
     - Title passed through marriage into the **Talbot family** (Penrice and
       Margam estate).
   * - 1766
     - Part of the **Earl of Plymouth's estates** (DPL/1).
   * - 1770–1785
     - **Valentine Morris** (part of the Piercefield/Glamorgan estate
       interests).
   * - c. 1770–1820
     - Estate-level transfers (Morris -> Sir Mark Wood -> Bute).  An 1820
       memorandum of exchanges of land between the Marquess of Bute and Lord
       Plymouth in the parish of Llandough, and the subsequent survey by
       **David Stewart** (Glamorgan Archives ref. DB/E), show Bute taking
       control of farms including "Great House Farm alias Cedfin".
   * - 1794–1824
     - Farm's title transition to **Sir Mark Wood**.
   * - 1817–1828
     - **Lambert Williams** was a high-status Ward Constable in Cardiff.
       File D160/1–4 records an agreement between Lambert Williams of Cardiff
       and the Bute Estate (1818–1835).  This proves a Williams was a
       recognised legal entity dealing with the Bute Estate on a high-status
       contractual basis.
   * - 1818
     - Chief rents of Llandough transferred to the manorial rental.
   * - 1820
     - Memorandum of exchanges of land between the Marquis of Bute and Lord
       Plymouth in the parish of Llandough.  Llandough and Cogan appear in
       the rentals from 1821 onwards.
   * - 1824
     - **Great House Farm alias "Cedfin."**  Survey of Marquess of Bute's
       Glamorgan estates by David Stewart (Glamorgan Archives ref. DB/E/1–2).
       Tithes of Llandough (along with Leckwith and Cogan) were purchased.
   * - 1824–1926
     - **Marquess of Bute** holds the estate.
   * - 1840–1880s
     - **Administrative Downgrade.**  The "Court House" was possibly
       rebranded a Grange.  By recording it as a "Grange" or "Farm" in the
       Bute Estate Records (NLW D 219), the estate effectively "lost" the
       history of the 1667 acquisition, replacing a proprietary history with a
       tenant-farmer narrative.  Census and tithe records systematically
       shifted terminology from "Great House" (Ty Mawr) to "Great House Farm"
       (Ty Mawr Farm) by the 1881 census.
   * - 1845–1892/93
     - Llandough listed in both Farm rents and Cottage rents sections.
   * - 1870
     - **Williams family discover Roman soldier** under the living-room floor
       of Ty Mawr while replacing stone flagstones with a timber floor.  This
       private find occurred exactly one hundred years before the house was
       demolished in 1988 and over a century before the "official" 1994
       excavations.  The soldier was reportedly found in a stone-lined grave
       (cist) wearing bronze or iron armour.
   * - 1876
     - **The Carve-Out (D153/4–8).**  The Bute Estate "severed" 33 acres
       formerly part of Great House Farm for the **Llandough Limeworks**
       (1876–1912).  This moved the property from manorial/agricultural
       tenure into the industrial/commercial category.
   * - 1880s–1890s
     - **Final Rebranding.**  By the time Marconi visited in 1897, the house
       was firmly referred to as Great House Farm in press reports.
   * - 1895–1905
     - **The Daniel Thomas arrangement.**  Family testimony and later court
       narratives state that **Daniel Thomas & Son** made an agreement with
       the Williams family whereby quarrying was allowed in return for eventual
       title.  The key deeds referred to are now missing.  A tree-planting
       ceremony marks this transition — strong evidence of customary transfer
       belief.
   * - 1897
     - **Marconi at Great House Farm.**  According to family tradition,
       **Guglielmo Marconi** was a guest at Great House Farm while working on
       wireless telegraphy experiments between **Lavernock Point** and
       **Flat Holm** (13 May 1897).  A Williams family member allegedly
       assisted with erecting the mast.  The first message sent was
       "CAN YOU HEAR ME" in Morse code, replied to by George Kemp:
       "YES LOUD AND CLEAR".  Post Office officials identified in the
       famous photograph include Mr G.N. Partridge, Mr H.C. Price, and
       Mr S.E. Hailes.  Marconi archive searches have narrowed the
       remaining repositories where further correspondence might be found.
   * - c. 1915
     - Two Daniel Thomases in Wales died this year (same D.T. as above?
       Unclear).

   * - 1987
     - **Court of Appeal.**  In ***BP Properties Ltd v Buckler*** [1987]
       EWCA Civ 2 / [1987] 2 EGLR 168, the Court of Appeal held that the
       1974 unilateral licence ended adverse possession and foreclosed the
       Buckler ownership claim.  The court ruled that Mrs Buckler did not
       have "adverse possession" because BP had sent her letters granting
       her permission to stay for life, effectively turning her into a
       "licensee."  The appeal determined issues relating to licence and
       adverse possession rather than providing a final judicial determination
       of competing historical title claims.  The use of the term "squatter"
       and the framing of the occupiers as "defendants" seeking possessory
       title, rather than heirs to centuries of documented family occupation
       from 1667, has been criticised.  The court prioritised formal Land
       Registration Act processes over historical context.  The judgment
       states: "Mrs Mary Buckler, who was also from time to time referred to
       as Mrs Williams, had conceived the notion that she had a title to the
       ownership of Great House Farm through her own grandfather and that
       there were documents in existence which proved her title.  No such
       documents were, however, ever produced."

       emergency "spot-listing" shortly before demolition.  Photographs
       taken on **29 July 1988** survive, although many associated records
       no longer exist.  Cadw confirmed they were considering an appeal to
       ask the Secretary of State for Wales to protect Great House Farm as a
       listed building.
   * - 1988
     - **Demolition.**  Great House Farm was demolished on **6 December 1988**
       (some sources say overnight 5/6 December), ending centuries of
       occupation and local heritage presence.  The manner of the eviction —
       removing a pregnant woman and three infants with five bailiffs and
       around 20 police officers — was extreme.  The house was allegedly
       rebranded as a "dilapidated utility building" or "ruined barn" in
       planning documents to bypass its status as an 800-year-old
       "County Treasure."  Proceeding with demolition while a land title
       dispute was active could be viewed as an intentional act to "bury"
       legal and historical evidence.
   * - 1988
     - **European Court of Human Rights Application 14464/88.**  An
       application was lodged with the European Court of Human Rights
       following the eviction.  The application was later declared
       inadmissible.  The original case file has subsequently been destroyed
       under the Court's records policy.
   * - 1989
     - March: Demolition works continued / completed (reported 20 March
       1989).  Billy Buckler vowed to continue fighting.  Charges against him
       were dropped or he was freed on bail.
   * - 1992
     - **Planning permission.**  In March 1992, full planning permission was
       obtained for the residential development **without any condition
       requiring archaeological work**.  This proves the planning system
       failed to protect the heritage despite the 1870/88 and 1963 finds.
       The fact that the developer voluntarily funded the 1994 excavation
       — after the house was flattened — highlights that the council/BP
       should have protected it.
   * - 1994
     - **Rescue excavation.**  The Cotswold Archaeological Trust (CAT, now
       Cotswold Archaeology) conducted excavations revealing an
       early-medieval monastic cemetery with **1,026 inhumation burials** —
       by far the largest early-medieval burial population so far recovered
       from Wales.  Earlier discoveries from 1963 and 1978 demonstrate that
       archaeological significance had already been recognised before
       demolition.  The excavation was generously funded by **Ideal Homes
       Wales Ltd** (now part of Persimmon Homes), who had already increased
       their financial contribution more than fivefold from the original
       project budget.  Post-excavation analysis was funded by **Cadw**
       (from January 1998) and the National Museums & Galleries of Wales.
       The finds and archive have been deposited with the National Museum &
       Gallery, Cardiff.
   * - 1994–2004
     - Osteological examination by **Louise Loe** (University of Bristol
       Ph.D., awarded 2004).  Radiocarbon dates from burials span the late
       8th to late 10th century, with one burial dug through a demolished
       villa wall dated 999–1284 cal A.D.
   * - 2024
     - **David Buckler** (grandson of Frederick Buckler, nephew of Billy
       Buckler) testifies: "Nan (Mrs Williams) often mentioned the story
       and had a journal with some of the dates and names of various VIPs
       that came to visit.  Not sure that it survived the demolition and
       scattering of belongings.  Billy was my dad's kid brother.  Gramps
       (Mr Fredrick Buckler) passed at 56 and my dad at 55.  I discussed
       the farm story with my Dad before he passed away and he was pretty
       sure that his Dad had tied off all the legal stuff when he sold the
       farm.  He just kept it all to himself.  That it was all done with a
       solicitor (legal type was the phrase).  He didn't tell the family
       for a long time and only gave them some details.  The sale was well
       before 87, and it's why my dad (William Buckler's brother) fell out
       with grandad (Mr Fredrick Buckler).  They all had a very hard /
       stressful time accepting the betrayal."
   * - Present Day
     - The former farm site is a housing development known as **Church
       View Close**, Llandough, comprising around 20 dwellings.  The family
       has formally placed on record an assertion that **Rhys Buckler** may
       be the rightful manorial lord of Llandough (by primogeniture from the
       Williams–Buckler line) and that **Sion Buckler** acts as a kind of
       "squire" or representative of that line, pending proper adjudication.
       The heir-apparent to any such manorial claim has Down syndrome; the
       family argues that any future obstruction of his succession or
       recognition would engage domestic equality law and the UK's
       international disability-rights obligations.

Historical Background
=====================

Medieval Origins (1100–1539)
----------------------------

The lordship was originally granted to the **Walsche** family by the Norman
conqueror **Robert fitz Hamon** (or his successors) around **1100**.
**Great House ("Ty Mawr")** was built next to **St Dochdwy's Church**,
Llandough, in **1215**.  The property was also known by the alternative
historic names **Llandochau** and **Llan Doche Penarth**.

From 1215 to 1539 the estate was owned by **Tewkesbury Abbey** / the Prior of
Cardiff as an ecclesiastical title.  The great church of Llanoughby was a
monastic institution, enjoying a one-time monastic tradition.  In its
decline, however, it presided over a large community of Christian life and
was an important missioning centre in early Christianity in Morganwg.  The
greater portion continued until the Norman conquest of Glamorgan, when the
monastery's property was vested by the Lord of Glamorgan in the Benedictine
Abbey of Tewkesbury for services in beseeching being.  The benefactions of
the Church of the Parish of Llandough, the revenues being regularly farmed,
remained appropriated to Tewkesbury until the Dissolution of the monasteries
in 1536–39.

In **1444**, the manor and lordship of Llandough (and St Mary Church) were
sold to **Sir William Thomas (Herbert) of Raglan**, ancestor of the Earls of
Pembroke.  The lordship remained in high-status hands for a century.

The Dissolution and After (1536–1770)
-------------------------------------

In **1536**, **Sir Edward Carne** (a lawyer and diplomat) purchased the manor
from the 2nd Earl of Worcester, a descendant of the Herberts.  Between
**1536 and 1539** the Crown (Henry VIII) confiscated the estate during the
Dissolution.  After the Dissolution, the manor of Llandough passed through
Crown grants to lay owners; by the later 16th century it formed part of the
Herbert/Pembroke orbit and was later absorbed into what became the **Bute
estate**.

This matches the Bute Estate Records description (many Glamorgan manors,
including Llandough, in Bute hands by the 18th–19th centuries), but it is
important not to over-specify Cradock as a proven intermediary.

From **1552 to 1829**, the Bute/Pembroke estate treated itself as the
superior landholder/manorial lord, and the people on those farms were tenants
or lessees, not the estate owners.  The "Llandough manorial leases and
agreements" series (NLW D 219, Bute Estate Records) explicitly identified
as Bute estate material covers this period and shows leases of Llandough Farm
(about 166 acres) and other Llandough properties.

The Williams Family and the 1667 Acquisition
--------------------------------------------

From at least **1667** the **Williams family** are recorded in local memory
and later press reports as occupying Great House Farm.  A 1980s newspaper
article quoted Mrs Mary Williams saying the farm was "acquired" by the
Williams family at that time, which she understood as a purchase rather than
a tenancy.  There is no surviving registered title from the 17th century, so
this is evidence of long-term possession and a family belief in ownership,
not a proved freehold deed.

At this time (1667), the records are part of the Herbert/Pembroke orbit,
specifically the Herbert family of Cogan Pill (correspondence 1667).  The
National Library of Wales (NLW D 219) categorises these records strictly as
"Manorial."  The 1667 acquisition is likely in the un-indexed portions of the
Corbett or Bute manorial records.

The core of the legal mystery lies in how the transfer was recorded:

**Estate Perception:**  The Bute Estate Records (Series D 219) treat the
family as part of a continuous sequence of manorial leases.

**Williams Perception:**  The 1667 transaction likely involved a "lease for
lives" (often three generations) or copyhold of inheritance.  These were
"hereditary" in practice, passing from father to son, which supports the
claim of 300+ years of continuous family standing.

**The "Erasure":**  Because the Court Baron records were the private property
of the Lord (the Bute family), they could "re-characterise" an ancient
hereditary tenure as a simple revocable lease when the manorial system was
being dismantled in the 19th and 20th centuries.

A "Court House" was where the Lord's steward held the manor courts.  If the
family occupied the Court House, they were likely acting as **Stewards or
Hereditary Bailiffs** for the Herberts — a "gentry standing" significantly
higher than a standard tenant farmer.

If a 1667 conveyance could be proved from estate archives, it would shift the
legal framing from mere occupation to ownership; at present, that remains a
key point for further research rather than an established fact.

In the 17th century, a fee farm grant or a 999-year lease was often perceived
and treated by the family as a "purchase" (absolute ownership), while the
superior Lord (Herbert/Bute) maintained it as a "lease" in their ledger to
preserve manorial rights.  If the 1667 deed was a conveyance of the Equitable
Title, the Bute records would still show the "Superior Title" (the
Lordship), effectively creating two parallel titles.

In 1667, a Williams ancestor would not "buy" the land in the modern
freehold sense; instead, they would be "admitted" to the tenure in the Court
Baron.  If the family paid a large "fine" (entry fee), this was often
remembered in family lore as a "purchase" of the land.

The Administrative Downgrade (1840s–1890s)
------------------------------------------

Historically, the Court House served as the administrative centre for
manorial business (Court Barons).  A "Court House" implies a physical site
of Incorporeal Hereditaments (manorial rights).  In the mid-19th century,
census and tithe records began systematically shifting terminology.  For
example, similar properties in the Vale transitioned from "Great House"
(Ty Mawr) to "Great House Farm" (Ty Mawr Farm) in the 1881 census.  This
linguistic shift officially demoted the property from a seat of local
governance to a mere commercial agricultural unit.

By the 1840s–1850s, the "Court House" was possibly rebranded a Grange (a
site of legal authority).  As a "Grange" or "Farm," the Bute estate could
demote the occupants from "Gentry with Tenure" to "Tenants of a Farm."
This transition is a classic tactic used to consolidate estate boundaries
during the 19th-century land reforms.  This period aligns with a broader
systemic shift across the Bute and Tredegar Estates to modernise land
management by erasing manorial vestiges in favour of industrial and
agricultural efficiency.  By recording it as a "Grange" or "Farm" in the
Bute Estate Records (NLW D 219), the estate effectively "lost" the history
of the 1667 acquisition, replacing a proprietary history with a tenant-farmer
narrative.

By the 1870s, the property was still functionally a substantial residence,
but the "Grange" narrative was likely being pushed by the Bute Estate to
justify its treatment as a standard leasehold.

By the 1880s–1890s, the **Final Rebranding** was complete.  By the time
Marconi visited in 1897, the house was firmly referred to as Great House
Farm in press reports.  In surrounding areas like Grangetown, the Bute
Estate was actively rebranding old manorial lands for industrial use, which
may have influenced the local renaming of high-status farms to "Granges" to
suggest they were historical monastic outbuildings (easier to reclaim)
rather than private manorial seats.

The Williams–Buckler Family Occupation
======================================

The 1870 Roman Soldier Discovery
--------------------------------

The Roman soldier in full armour was discovered under the living-room floor
of **Ty Mawr (Great House Farm)** in **1870/88**.  This discovery occurred
while the Williams family was replacing the old stone flagstones with a
timber floor.  This private find happened exactly one hundred years before
the house was demolished in 1988 and over a century before the "official"
1994 excavations that claimed to first reveal the site's true historical
importance.

**Context of the Discovery:**

*   **The Artifacts:**  The soldier was reportedly found in a stone-lined
    grave (cist) wearing what appeared to be bronze or iron armour, which
    aligns with later academic findings of a high-status Roman Villa and an
    early medieval cemetery on the same grounds.
*   **The "Official" Narrative vs. Reality:**  While academic reports
    often focus on the "rescue" digs of 1978 and 1994, the Williamses'
    19th-century discovery serves as evidence that the historical
    significance of the site was known locally for generations.
*   **Archaeological Significance:**  The burial was likely part of the
    Llandough Roman Villa estate, which was later used as a sacred monastic
    burial ground.  Physical evidence found by residents was dismissed or
    "lost" until a developer's project forced a formal, taxpayer-funded
    excavation.

It is well-documented in local lore and early antiquarian records that
Llandough was a Roman and early Christian hub.  The discovery of a "Roman
soldier in full armour" (often associated with the high-status burials of
the Llandough Roman Villa) should have legally triggered an immediate halt
to any development.

The 1876 Carve-Out (D153/4–8)
------------------------------

The record explicitly mentions "land (33a.), formerly part of Great House
Farm, for the Llandough Limeworks (33a.), 1876–1912."

**The "Between the Lines" Reality:**  In 1876, the Bute Estate effectively
"severed" the land from the farm.  By designating it for "Limeworks," they
moved the property from Manorial/Agricultural tenure (which the Williams
family held) into the Industrial/Commercial category.

**The Legal Erasure:**  If the Williams family was living there in 1870
(when they found the Roman soldier), this 1876 deed represents the Bute
Estate treating the land as their own to lease out to industry, likely
ignoring the family's 1667-based claim.

The Daniel Thomas Arrangement (c. 1895–1905)
-------------------------------------------

Family testimony and later court narratives state that **Daniel Thomas &
Son** made an agreement with the Williams family whereby quarrying was
allowed in return for eventual title.  The key deeds referred to are now
missing and need to be traced in Bute estate and local archives.  D. Thomas
& Son, Limestoners, operated locally, quarrying limestone on or near Great
House Farm.  Quarrying legally required the occupier's consent, not just
the estate's.  The Williams family granted permission.  In return, ownership
or permanent security was promised (whether formally written or not).  A
tree-planting ceremony marks this transition — not proof of legal title, but
strong evidence of customary transfer belief at the time.

In the late 19th/early 20th century, the Bute Estate sold off many
"superior" interests to consolidate funds for Cardiff.  If Thomas acted as
an intermediary, there should be a **Conveyance of the Freehold Reversion**
or an **Enfranchisement Deed** (converting manorial tenure to freehold).  The
"fraud and concealment" referred to in the 1987 case likely involved the
suppression of this specific 1900s conveyance.

Local trade also prospered and surplus produce of the Vale farmsteads and
limestone, which was considered the best for hardening under water, were
shipped to Bristol, Minehead, Watchet, and other places across the Bristol
Channel.  It was limestone from Aberthaw which was used in the construction
of the docks at Sebastopol, and Smeaton used it when building the Eddystone
… [record incomplete].

The 1916 Tenancy
----------------

In **February 1916**, the Marquess of Bute granted a yearly agricultural
tenancy of Great House Farm to **Mr John Williams** (Mrs Mary Williams'
maternal grandfather).  This is one of the few solid documentary points where
the estate's view is clear: on paper, Bute was still treated as the
freehold owner, and John Williams was recorded as tenant.  That sits
uneasily with the family's longstanding belief that they already "owned" the
farm, but it does not rule out earlier informal or unrecorded
understandings; large estates sometimes regularised long-standing occupiers
by putting them on formal tenancies, even where those occupiers believed
they had stronger rights.

Legally, **1916 marks a sharp divergence** between lived reality and estate
paperwork: the Williams line saw themselves as inheritors of an older title,
while the estate's documents framed them as agricultural tenants of Bute.
From the family's perspective, this tenancy may have been experienced as
bureaucratic tidying-up of a relationship they already regarded as ownership,
rather than as a fresh grant that erased earlier rights.

In later court proceedings, the shift in legal language from treating Mrs
Mary Williams as someone asserting ownership to describing her as an
"occupier" or "squatter" was not neutral; by choosing those Latin-loaded
and technical terms, the system effectively downgraded a long-standing claim
of superior title into mere possession, which amounted to an erasure of her
identity and status as Williams, not simply a change of legal category.

The 1924 Estate Sale and 1938 Auction
-------------------------------------

The fact that "Great House" appears in the **Llandough Estate sale of 1924**
(DSA/6/701) suggests the Bute/Byass estates were systematically "packaging"
manorial lands to sell them off, likely ignoring the ancient "equitable
titles" held by families like Williams.

In **1938**, Great House Farm was advertised as "sold" at auction by Herbert
R. Thomas (Bute Estate / Mountjoy Ltd or its nominee/local estate agent and
chartered auctioneer).  This transaction related to the **freehold reversion**
— a paper title — and did not result in the displacement of the Williams
family, who remained in physical occupation.  The later history of rent
arrears, possession proceedings, and adverse possession claims
demonstrates that the 1938 sale did not convey vacant possession, but
instead marked the beginning of a long divergence between paper ownership
and living occupation.

The reversion was conveyed to **Western Ground Rents Ltd**.

Western Ground Rents Ltd (1955–1969)
------------------------------------

**1955 — High Court Possession Proceedings**

Western Ground Rents Ltd brought possession proceedings in the High Court
under Order 14 and obtained a possession order for the whole farm.  On
**4 July 1955**, the possession order was enforced for the farm **except**
the farmhouse and garden due to Mrs Mary Williams's strong objections
following her leg amputation and hospitalisation.  There was even,
apparently, a fear that she might attempt suicide.  The Williams' occupation
of the farmhouse and garden remained undisturbed.

**1959 — Mrs Williams's Belief in Title**

Mrs Williams believed she had title to the property through her grandfather
— produced no documents (as the title was allegedly stolen from the public
library and the copy stolen from the loft of the farmhouse).  She was
offered a tenancy of the farmhouse and garden by Western Ground Rents but
refused to accept it.  She was also later offered, and refused, alternative
accommodation.

The advisers of Western Ground Rents seem during these years to have
supposed that, if they attended at the farmhouse from time to time and
asserted Western Ground Rents' title, that was sufficient to prevent time
running against Western Ground Rents under the Limitation Act 1939.  Plainly,
however, that notion was erroneous, as **section 13** of the Act provides
that "no person shall be deemed to have been in possession of any land by
reason only of having made a formal entry thereon, and no continual or other
claim upon or near any land shall preserve any right of action to recover the
land."

Archaeological Significance
===========================

The 1870/88 Discovery
---------------------

The Roman soldier in full armour was discovered under the living-room floor
of Ty Mawr (Great House Farm) in 1870/88.  This discovery occurred while
the Williams family was replacing the old stone flagstones with a timber
floor.  This private find happened exactly one hundred years before the
house was demolished in 1988 and over a century before the "official" 1994
excavations that claimed to first reveal the site's true historical
importance.

**Context of the Discovery:**

*   **The Artifacts:**  The soldier was reportedly found in a stone-lined
    grave (cist) wearing what appeared to be bronze or iron armour, which
    aligns with later academic findings of a high-status Roman Villa and an
    early medieval cemetery on the same grounds.
*   **The "Official" Narrative vs. Reality:**  While academic reports
    often focus on the "rescue" digs of 1978 and 1994, the Williamses'
    19th-century discovery serves as evidence that the historical
    significance of the site was known locally for generations.
*   **Archaeological Significance:**  The burial was likely part of the
    Llandough Roman Villa estate, which was later used as a sacred monastic
    burial ground.  Physical evidence found by residents was dismissed or
    "lost" until a developer's project forced a formal, taxpayer-funded
    excavation.

It is therefore well-documented in local lore and early antiquarian records
that Llandough was a Roman and early Christian hub.  The discovery of a
"Roman soldier in full armour" (often associated with the high-status
burials of the Llandough Roman Villa) should have legally triggered an
immediate halt to any development.

The 1963 Discovery
-------------------

In **1963**, human skeletal remains were uncovered during utility trench works
near the farm.  A gully containing three human skeletons of possible
13th-century date was recorded by the Barry and Vale Archaeological Group.
This provides evidence that the archaeological significance of the site was
recognised decades before the major excavations.

The 1978–1980 GGAT Excavations
------------------------------

The Glamorgan-Gwent Archaeological Trust (GGAT) conducted excavations in
1978–1980.  The earliest structure found was a possible late Iron-age
roundhouse, perhaps part of a farmstead.  In the 2nd century A.D. a Roman
villa was constructed, which developed considerably in the early 3rd century
and was occupied until the early 4th century.  Some walls of the villa were
reused as foundations for a 12th- or 13th-century barn — the only evidence
that occupation may have continued on the villa site into the post-Roman
period.

Later **LiDAR** evidence suggests these discoveries formed only part of a
much larger archaeological landscape than previously recognised.

The 1994 Rescue Excavation
--------------------------

The Cotswold Archaeological Trust (CAT, now Cotswold Archaeology) conducted
excavations in 1994 revealing an early-medieval monastic cemetery with
**1,026 inhumation burials** — by far the largest early-medieval burial
population so far recovered from Wales.  Earlier discoveries from 1963 and
1978 demonstrate that archaeological significance had already been recognised
before demolition.

The excavation was generously funded by **Ideal Homes Wales Ltd** (now part of
Persimmon Homes), who had already increased their financial contribution more
than fivefold from the original project budget.  Post-excavation analysis was
funded by **Cadw** (from January 1998) and the National Museums & Galleries
of Wales.  The finds and archive have been deposited with the National Museum
& Gallery, Cardiff.

Osteological examination by **Louise Loe** (University of Bristol Ph.D.,
awarded 2004).  Radiocarbon dates from burials span the late 8th to late
10th century, with one burial dug through a demolished villa wall dated
999–1284 cal A.D.

**The "Burial 631" Context (c. AD 340–660):**  The discovery of "Burial 631"
(dated between c. AD 340 and 660) highlights the site's national
significance.  While the suppressed 1870/88 Roman soldier discovery and the
1978–1980 archaeological reports already indicated a high-status site, the
1994 excavation confirmed these findings with specific evidence, such as
"Burial 631" — a young man found with iron straps binding him.  This highly
unusual find underscored the true national importance of the archaeology
located directly adjacent to and underneath the demolished farm.

**Suppression of Known Archaeological Significance:**  The discovery of the
Roman soldier in 1870/88 by the Williams family served as "actual notice" of
the site's historical importance.  If the authorities or developers later
claimed the site's significance was "unknown" until the 1978/1994 excavations
to avoid preservation costs, this could constitute a fraudulent
misrepresentation to the planning office.

**Official Narrative Distortion:**  When the major excavation of over 1,000
burials took place in 1994, it was framed as a "new" discovery.  Attributing
the site's significance only to this date ignores the family's 1870/88 notice,
potentially protecting the parties involved in the 1988 demolition from
liability for destroying a known monument.
