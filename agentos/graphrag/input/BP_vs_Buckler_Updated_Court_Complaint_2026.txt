PART A: INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF CLAIM

1.  The Claimant, [full name], brings this application for declaratory relief and/or review of the proceedings in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2, [1987] 2 EGLR 168, on the grounds that those proceedings were fundamentally procedurally mischaracterised, placed in an inappropriate judicial forum, and conducted under an inadequate legal framework, resulting in the deprivation of the Williams/Buckler family's home and ancestral land without proper determination of the underlying proprietary, equitable, and historical rights at stake.

2.  This is not an application to re-open the 1987 decision on its merits per se. It is an application for recognition that the court in 1987 committed a procedural error of such magnitude — the failure to classify the dispute according to its true substance and to place it in the appropriate judicial forum — that the proceedings ought never to have been permitted to proceed as a conventional possession claim in a lower-tier court, and that the consequences — the permanent loss of a family home, the destruction of an historic building, and the extinguishment of generations of connection with the land — were the direct and foreseeable result of that procedural failure.

3.  The Claimant asserts that the 1987 proceedings were, in substance, a dispute over competing land rights of the highest order — involving alleged ancient occupation, proprietary expectations, equitable interests, and competing claims arising from long possession — yet were treated procedurally as a simple possession claim between parties with no competing historic claim, and were placed in a court framework designed for immediate entitlement determinations rather than for the adjudication of deeper questions of land rights. This procedural mischaracterisation and forum misplacement determined the scope of inquiry, the safeguards available, and the ultimate outcome.

_______________________________________________________________

PART B: THE FACTUAL BACKGROUND

4.  The property in question is Great House Farm (Ty Mawr), Llandough, in the Vale of Glamorgan, Wales (hereinafter "the Property"). The Williams/Buckler family had been in occupation of the Property for generations, with roots extending back to the nineteenth century and beyond.

5.  The family's connection to the land was not merely one of recent tenancy or casual occupation. It was a connection founded upon:
    (a) continuous generational occupation from at least the late 19th century;
    (b) a historic arrangement with Daniel Thomas in 1871 concerning lime and land rights, which the family asserts gave rise to proprietary and equitable interests;
    (c) long-standing possession as a source of title under the Limitation Act 1980;
    (d) the family's status as occupying beneficiaries with equitable interests in the Property;
    (e) the family's actual occupation of the Property at all material times.

6.  In or about 1974, BP Properties Ltd (the Defendant) acquired the freehold title to the Property. The family remained in occupation. The Defendant subsequently brought possession proceedings.

7.  The 1987 proceedings were presented to the court as a straightforward claim for possession of land. The remedy sought was possession. The practical and legal consequence, however, was the removal of a family from its longstanding home and the extinguishment of a historic connection with the land.

8.  The Court of Appeal, in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] 2 EGLR 168, determined the matter solely on the basis of adverse possession and the effect of a purported licence granted in 1974. The court held that from the moment the mother received the offer to remain on the land, she occupied it under a licence and her possession was no longer adverse. It did not matter that she never accepted the offer.

9.  The Court of Appeal did not, however, examine whether the proceedings had been properly characterised in the first place, whether they had been placed in the appropriate judicial forum, or whether the procedural framework adopted was adequate to the nature of the rights at stake. The court did not consider whether the family's competing proprietary, equitable, and historical claims required a different procedural approach or a higher-tier court.

_______________________________________________________________

PART C: THE PROCEDURAL ERROR ALLEGED — MISCHARACTERISATION AND FORUM MISPLACEMENT

10. The Claimant alleges that the court in 1987 committed two fundamental procedural errors:
    (a) it permitted the dispute to proceed under a procedural framework defined by the Defendant's requested remedy (possession) rather than by the full nature of the rights affected; and
    (b) it placed the dispute in a judicial forum — County Court possession proceedings — that was inadequate to the determination of the competing land rights at stake.

11. The court thereby considered the question:
    "Who has the immediate right to possession?"
    when it should first have considered:
    "What competing rights exist in the land, and can those rights lawfully be extinguished through a possession procedure in this forum?"

12. The procedural classification of the case and its placement in an inappropriate forum determined the scope of inquiry and the protections available. A party should not obtain the practical equivalent of extinguishing historic land rights merely by selecting possession as the form of remedy sought and by placing the case in a lower-tier court.

13. The Claimant does not allege that the court reached the wrong conclusion on the facts as presented. The Claimant alleges that the court asked the wrong question, in the wrong forum, with the wrong procedural safeguards, and thereby reached a conclusion that did not reflect the true nature of the dispute.

PART D: THE APPROPRIATE JUDICIAL FORUM

14. The dispute in 1987 involved matters of the highest importance:
    (a) competing claims to land rights extending back to the 19th century;
    (b) equitable interests and beneficial ownership;
    (c) overriding interests under the Land Registration Act 1925;
    (d) the deprivation of a family home;
    (e) the destruction of an historic building;
    (f) the extinguishment of generations of connection with the land.

15. Such a dispute required adjudication in a forum capable of examining the full depth of the competing rights. The appropriate hierarchy of courts and frameworks, ranked by suitability for this dispute, was as follows:

    1. HIGH COURT — CHANCERY DIVISION
       The most appropriate forum. The Chancery Division has exclusive jurisdiction over matters of equity, trusts, land registration, and competing proprietary interests. It is the proper forum for disputes involving:
       — equitable interests and beneficial ownership;
       — overriding interests under the Land Registration Act 1925;
       — competing claims to land title;
       — the determination of rights arising from long possession;
       — the protection of occupying beneficiaries.

    2. HIGH COURT — QUEEN'S BENCH DIVISION
       The second most appropriate forum. The Queen's Bench Division has jurisdiction over matters of land law, possession, and the determination of competing rights, and is capable of examining the full substance of the dispute.

    3. COUNTY COURT — FULL LAND/TITLE PROCEEDINGS
       A lower-tier forum, but capable of examining questions of title and competing rights if properly constituted.

    4. COUNTY COURT — POSSESSION PROCEEDINGS
       The forum actually used. A narrow framework designed to determine immediate entitlement to possession, not to adjudicate deeper questions of land rights, equity, historic occupation, and competing claims.

    5. MAGISTRATES' COURT
       The least suitable forum for a dispute of this nature.

16. The dispute in 1987 plainly fell within categories 1 or 2. It involved deeper questions of land rights, equity, historic occupation, and competing claims. It required the scrutiny and safeguards of the High Court.

17. Instead, the case was placed in category 4 — County Court possession proceedings — a narrower possession framework designed to determine immediate entitlement rather than the wider rights affected. This was a case involving the loss of a longstanding family home and historic land connection, yet it was allowed to proceed through a lower-scrutiny route rather than the higher-rights framework the circumstances arguably required.

18. The alleged procedural error is therefore not merely that the wrong procedure was used, but that the case was placed in the wrong tier of court — a tier inadequate to the determination of the rights at stake.

PART E: THE LEGAL FRAMEWORK THAT SHOULD HAVE APPLIED

19. At the relevant time, civil procedure was governed principally by:
    (a) the County Courts Act 1959 (as amended);
    (b) the Rules of the Supreme Court 1965 / County Court Rules.

20. Those rules required courts to determine the real issues between parties and provided mechanisms for wider examination where possession proceedings disclosed substantial questions of title or equitable interest.

21. The court possessed inherent powers and procedural duties to prevent a party from obtaining a remedy without determination of the underlying rights upon which that remedy depended.

22. The true issue before the court was not merely possession. The underlying issue was:
    Whether BP possessed a superior right to extinguish the Williams/Buckler family's longstanding occupation and connection with the land.

23. The court was therefore required to distinguish between:
    (a) a conventional possession dispute between parties with no competing historic claim; and
    (b) a dispute involving alleged ancient occupation, proprietary expectations, equitable interests, and competing claims arising from long possession.

24. The court failed to make this distinction. It treated the case as falling within category (a) when it plainly fell within category (b), and it placed the case in a forum suited to category (a) when it required a forum suited to category (b).

_______________________________________________________________

PART F: THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN LEGAL TITLE AND EQUITABLE RIGHTS

25. The courts of England and Wales historically recognised that strict legal title did not always represent the complete extent of rights and obligations concerning land.

26. The principles of equity required consideration of matters including:
    (a) unconscionable conduct;
    (b) reliance;
    (c) equitable interests;
    (d) beneficial ownership;
    (e) circumstances surrounding acquisition of title.

27. Where a party sought possession but the opposing party asserted a substantial competing interest in the land, the court was required to determine the true nature of that interest rather than merely accept the claimant's chosen procedural description.

28. The Williams/Buckler family's claim was not founded upon recent occupation but upon alleged generations of continuous occupation and connection with the land. Such a claim engaged historical legal concepts including:
    (a) possession as a source of title;
    (b) adverse possession;
    (c) prescriptive rights;
    (d) customary interests;
    (e) the protection of longstanding occupation.

29. The court should therefore have first determined whether the matter was properly characterised as a simple possession claim or as a dispute requiring fuller adjudication of competing land rights, and whether the forum selected was adequate to that determination.

_______________________________________________________________

PART G: REGISTRATION OF TITLE WAS NOT THE END OF THE INQUIRY

30. The proceedings relied upon BP's registered title following registration under the Land Registration Act 1925.

31. However, registration under the Land Registration Act 1925 was not intended to eliminate the court's duty to examine overriding interests, equitable interests, or competing rights where properly raised.

32. Section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 expressly protected "the rights of every person in actual occupation of the land or in receipt of the rents and profits thereof, save where enquiry is made of such person and the rights are not disclosed."

33. This provision was specifically designed to protect occupying beneficiaries and others with equitable interests in registered land. As Russell L.J. observed in Hodgson v Marks [1971] Ch 892, CA: "It must depend on the circumstances, and a wise purchaser or lender will take no risks. Indeed, however wise he may be he may have no ready opportunity of finding out; but, nevertheless, the law will protect the occupier."

34. A registered proprietor's claim to possession remained subject to the law governing the creation, protection, and enforcement of competing interests in land.

35. The court should have examined whether the family's actual occupation gave rise to overriding interests or equitable rights that could not be extinguished by a simple possession order. This examination required the scrutiny of the Chancery Division, not the abbreviated framework of County Court possession proceedings.

PART H: THE CONSEQUENCES REQUIRED A HIGHER LEVEL OF JUDICIAL PROTECTION

36. The court was aware, or should have been aware, that the result would affect:
    (a) a family home;
    (b) children residing at the property;
    (c) an historic building (Great House Farm, Ty Mawr);
    (d) archaeological and heritage interests;
    (e) a location with evidence of ancient occupation.

37. The destruction of the property meant that any later remedy would be incapable of fully restoring the previous position.

38. A procedure resulting in irreversible consequences required careful examination before final orders were made.

39. The court failed to apply the enhanced scrutiny that the nature of the case demanded. The forum selected — County Court possession proceedings — was inherently incapable of providing that scrutiny.

PART I: THE ALLEGED PROCEDURAL MISCHARACTERISATION AND FORUM MISPLACEMENT IN DETAIL

40. The alleged error was not simply that the court reached an unfavourable conclusion on the facts or the law as presented.

41. The alleged error was that the court:
    (a) permitted the dispute to proceed under a procedural framework defined by the Defendant's requested remedy rather than by the full nature of the rights affected; and
    (b) placed the dispute in a judicial forum — County Court possession proceedings — that was inadequate to the determination of the competing land rights at stake.

42. By characterising the case as a simple possession claim and placing it in a lower-tier forum, the court:
    (a) limited the scope of inquiry to the immediate right to possession;
    (b) excluded from consideration the family's competing proprietary and equitable claims;
    (c) failed to apply the procedural safeguards appropriate to a case involving the deprivation of a home and ancestral land;
    (d) permitted the Defendant to obtain the practical equivalent of a declaration of superior title without having to prove that superior title in a forum suited to that determination;
    (e) denied the family the protections of the Chancery Division, the proper forum for disputes involving equity, trusts, and competing proprietary interests.

43. The procedural classification of the case and its placement in an inappropriate forum therefore determined the scope of inquiry and the protections available.

PART J: THE PRINCIPLE OF LAWFUL DEPRIVATION

44. The constitutional principle expressed in Magna Carta 1215, Clause 39 — that no free person shall be deprived of land except by lawful judgment or law of the land — reflects a longstanding principle against arbitrary dispossession.

45. While Clause 39 was not itself a direct procedural rule governing the 1987 proceedings, its underlying principle remains relevant: deprivation of land requires proper lawful determination.

46. The issue was therefore not merely possession, but whether the process used was proportionate and appropriate to the seriousness of the rights affected.

47. The family's deprivation of their home and land was effected through a procedure that did not adequately determine the competing rights at stake, in a forum that was not adequate to that determination. This was not lawful judgment in the substantive sense required by the principle.

_______________________________________________________________

PART K: THE FAMILY'S COMPETING RIGHTS

48. The Claimant particularises the family's competing rights as follows:

    (1) RIGHTS ARISING FROM LONG POSSESSION
    The family had been in occupation of the Property for generations, from at least the late 19th century. This long possession gave rise to rights under the Limitation Act 1980, including the potential for title by adverse possession.

    (2) RIGHTS ARISING FROM THE 1871 ARRANGEMENT
    The family asserts that an arrangement with Daniel Thomas in 1871 concerning lime and land rights gave rise to proprietary and equitable interests in the Property. This arrangement was never properly examined in the 1987 proceedings.

    (3) EQUITABLE INTERESTS AS OCCUPYING BENEFICIARIES
    The family occupied the Property as beneficiaries of a trust or similar arrangement, giving rise to equitable interests that could not be extinguished by a simple possession order.

    (4) OVERRIDING INTERESTS UNDER SECTION 70(1)(g) LRA 1925
    The family's actual occupation of the Property gave rise to overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925, which the court failed to examine.

    (5) CUSTOMARY AND PRESCRIPTIVE RIGHTS
    The family's long use of the Property gave rise to customary and prescriptive rights that required separate determination.

PART L: RELIEF SOUGHT

49. The Claimant respectfully requests that the Court:

    (1) DECLARE that the proceedings in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 were procedurally mischaracterised and placed in an inappropriate judicial forum, and that the dispute should have received treatment proportionate to the nature of the rights affected;

    (2) DECLARE that the court should have considered whether the proceedings were properly classified as a possession claim and whether the forum selected was adequate to the determination of the competing rights before determining possession;

    (3) DECLARE that the principles of due process, natural justice, and lawful protection of property require that procedure must reflect substance, and that the forum must be adequate to the rights at stake, not merely the remedy requested by the claimant;

    (4) DECLARE that future cases involving the loss of homes and longstanding land interests should require examination of underlying proprietary and equitable rights in an appropriate forum before irreversible remedies are granted;

    (5) MAKE SUCH OTHER ORDER as the Court thinks fit, including:
        (a) an order for compensation;
        (b) an order for inquiry;
        (c) an order for restitution;
        (d) such other relief as may be appropriate in all the circumstances.

50. The Claimant relies upon the following grounds:

    GROUND 1: PROCEDURAL MISCHARACTERISATION
    The court failed to recognise that the dispute was not a simple possession claim but a dispute involving competing proprietary, equitable, and historical land rights.

    GROUND 2: FORUM MISPLACEMENT
    The court placed the dispute in County Court possession proceedings — a lower-tier forum — when it required the scrutiny of the High Court, Chancery Division.

    GROUND 3: FAILURE TO APPLY APPROPRIATE SAFEGUARDS
    The court failed to apply the procedural safeguards appropriate to a case involving the deprivation of a family home and ancestral land.

    GROUND 4: FAILURE TO DETERMINE COMPETING RIGHTS
    The court failed to determine the family's competing proprietary and equitable interests before granting a possession order.

    GROUND 5: FAILURE TO PROTECT OVERRIDING INTERESTS
    The court failed to examine whether the family's actual occupation gave rise to overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925.

    GROUND 6: FAILURE TO APPLY ENHANCED SCRUTINY
    The court failed to apply the enhanced scrutiny required by the irreversible nature of the consequences.

    GROUND 7: BREACH OF PRINCIPLES OF NATURAL JUSTICE
    The procedural framework adopted did not permit the full and fair determination of the real issues between the parties.

    GROUND 8: BREACH OF DUE PROCESS
    The family's rights were determined by a procedure that did not reflect the substance of the dispute, in a forum that was not adequate to that determination.

    GROUND 9: FAILURE TO CONSIDER EQUITABLE PRINCIPLES
    The court failed to consider the principles of equity, including unconscionable conduct, reliance, and beneficial ownership.

    GROUND 10: FAILURE TO CONSIDER THE 1871 ARRANGEMENT
    The court failed to examine the family's rights arising from the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas.

    GROUND 11: FAILURE TO CONSIDER CUSTOMARY AND PRESCRIPTIVE RIGHTS
    The court failed to examine the family's customary and prescriptive rights arising from long use of the Property.

    GROUND 12: DENIAL OF ACCESS TO THE APPROPRIATE FORUM
    The family was denied access to the Chancery Division, the proper forum for disputes involving equity, trusts, and competing proprietary interests, by the procedural mischaracterisation of the case.

PART N: PARTICULARS OF THE PROCEDURAL ERROR

51. The Claimant particularises the procedural error as follows:

    (1) The Defendant presented the claim as a simple possession claim, when in fact the claim involved the extinguishment of longstanding family rights in land.

    (2) The court accepted the Defendant's procedural characterisation without independent examination of whether that characterisation reflected the true nature of the dispute.

    (3) The court failed to consider whether the family's claim to longstanding occupation, equitable interests, and proprietary expectations required a different procedural framework and a higher-tier forum.

    (4) The court failed to consider whether the family's rights under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 (rights of persons in actual occupation) required separate determination in the Chancery Division.

    (5) The court failed to consider whether the consequences of the possession order — the permanent loss of a family home and the destruction of an historic building — required enhanced procedural safeguards and a forum capable of examining the full depth of the competing rights.

    (6) The court permitted the Defendant to obtain the practical equivalent of a declaration of superior title without having to prove that superior title in a forum suited to that determination.

    (7) The court failed to examine the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas and its effect on the family's rights.

    (8) The court failed to examine whether the family's long possession gave rise to adverse possession or other proprietary rights.

    (9) The court failed to examine whether the family's occupation gave rise to equitable interests as occupying beneficiaries.

    (10) The court failed to examine whether the family's use of the Property gave rise to customary or prescriptive rights.

    (11) The court denied the family access to the Chancery Division, the proper forum for disputes involving equity, trusts, and competing proprietary interests, by the procedural mischaracterisation of the case.

    (12) The court placed the dispute in County Court possession proceedings — a forum designed for immediate entitlement determinations — when the dispute required the scrutiny of the High Court, Chancery Division.
