FORENSIC REPORT: THE EXTRAORDINARY BEQUEST OF WILLIAM EVANS WILLIAMS TO THE 3RD MARQUESS OF BUTE (1870)

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

In February 1870, William Evans Williams, a 62-year-old bachelor magistrate of Pwllypant House, Caerphilly, Glamorgan, died leaving a will dated 1867 that bequeathed his entire estate—valued at approximately £200,000—to John Patrick Crichton-Stuart, 3rd Marquess of Bute, a man with whom he had no blood relation, no personal acquaintance, and only limited business dealings. The will made virtually no provision for his own family, save a small legacy to his manservant and directions for his dog and pony. The bequest caused a sensation in the South Wales press, was publicly discussed in terms of its injustice to needy relatives, and yet was ultimately accepted by the Marquess, who kept the real property and distributed the personal estate among Williams's relations. This report examines every known detail of this anomaly.

Full Name: William Evans Williams

Residence: Pwll-y-pant House (also spelled Pwllypant), near Caerphilly, Glamorgan

Date of Birth: 1806

Date of Death: February 1870 (aged 62)

Occupation: Magistrate; Landowner

Marital Status: Bachelor (never married)

Williams was the son of William Williams Esq. of Caerphilly and Mary, daughter of Thomas Evans of Bultloyd, Co. Glamorgan. His mother died at Clevedon in 1840, aged 54, and had been previously married to William Williams, with whom she had two children: William Evans Williams and Mary Ann.

1.2 Character and Mode of Life

The newspaper account describes Williams as having lived "a very retired life, never receiving company." He was not a social figure. His time was occupied by "a few literary pursuits" and scientific observation. He was a man of intellectual habits who took "solar observations, by which he regulated a tower clock over his house."

This clock was described as "massive" and was probably purchased at the Paris Exhibition of 1867. The fact that he regulated it by solar observations indicates a serious interest in astronomy, horology, and precision measurement—suggesting a methodical, perhaps obsessive, personality.

1.3 Wealth and Property

Williams was a man of very considerable means. His estate was estimated at £200,000—a sum that in 1870 placed him among the wealthiest private individuals in Glamorgan.

• Extensive lands in Newport and Breconshire
• Royalties from the Energlyn colliery
• Royalties from the Rhymney Railway
• Annual royalties of "several hundred pounds yearly" from the Bute estate for stone taken from his quarry

The house itself is thought to date in part from the 17th century. The Williams family had been established there from the late 18th century or earlier.

1.4 Public Role

Williams served as a magistrate, indicating he was a figure of some standing in local administration and justice, despite his reclusive habits.

The will was dated 1867—three years before Williams's death in February 1870. This is significant: the will was not a deathbed document but a deliberate, premeditated disposition made while Williams was in apparently good health.

The will contained the following provisions:

That is all. No provision for his sister. No provision for his half-brother. No provision for any other relative.

The specificity of the dog and pony provision, combined with the total exclusion of family, suggests either a profound estrangement or a psychological state in which animals and servants were valued above blood relations.

SECTION III: THE RECIPIENT — JOHN PATRICK CRICHTON-STUART, 3RD MARQUESS OF BUTE

3.1 Identity and Status

John Patrick Crichton-Stuart (1847–1900) succeeded to the marquisate in March 1848 at the age of six months, following the sudden death of his father, the 2nd Marquess. He was widely reported to be the richest infant in Britain, and ultimately one of the wealthiest men in the world.

By 1900, the Glamorgan estate alone amounted to 22,000 acres.

3.2 The Catholic Conversion Scandal

The most important contextual fact is Bute's conversion to Roman Catholicism in December 1868. This was one of the great religious scandals of Victorian Britain. The young Marquess had been attracted to Catholicism since childhood. While at Oxford in 1866, he abandoned Presbyterianism. On 8 December 1868, he was received into the Church by Monsignor Capel at a convent in Southwark, and later confirmed by Pope Pius IX in Rome.

This conversion inspired Benjamin Disraeli to use Bute as the basis for the hero of his novel Lothair (1870). It scandalized Protestant Britain, and was particularly shocking in Wales, where Nonconformity was dominant and anti-Catholic sentiment was strong.

3.3 Williams's Reaction to the Conversion

The newspaper reports a critical contradiction in Williams's attitude:

"He has been several times heard to express his high appreciation of the efforts of the late and present Lord Bute in promoting the commercial interests of the town and district, but on the other hand it is only a few weeks since the deceased publicly expressed his strong disapproval of the conversion of his Lordship to the Roman Catholic Church."

This is extraordinary. Williams publicly condemned Bute's religion only weeks before his death, yet had already made a will three years earlier leaving everything to that same man. This suggests:
• The bequest was not motivated by religious sympathy
• Williams was capable of separating his admiration for Bute's commercial activities from his disapproval of his religion
• There was no last-minute reconciliation or deathbed conversion of sentiment

3.4 Bute's Architectural and Commercial Activities

At the time of the bequest, Bute was engaged in the massive transformation of Cardiff Castle under architect William Burges. Work on the Clock Tower began in 1868/1869 and was completed in 1873/1874. The tower was built in Burges's signature Forest of Dean ashlar stone, but the newspaper specifically mentions that "Lord Bute also selected the stone in preference to others for building the new tower at Cardiff Castle."

The 2nd Marquess (Bute's father) had been "the creator of modern Cardiff" through the development of the Bute Docks, which opened in 1839 and transformed Cardiff into the world's greatest coal-exporting port.

SECTION IX: FORENSIC ANALYSIS OF THE ANOMALY

9.1 The Central Puzzle

The bequest is anomalous on multiple levels:

1. No personal relationship: "His lordship was in no way related to Mr. Williams, and probably had not even a casual acquaintanceship with the donor."

1. No religious sympathy: Williams publicly disapproved of Bute's Catholic conversion weeks before his death.

1. No financial need on Bute's part: Bute was already one of the richest men in Britain.

1. Total exclusion of family: Despite having a sister, a half-brother, and needy relatives including an 80-year-old woman on parish relief.

1. The will predated the most specific connection: The selection of stone for Cardiff Castle tower came after the will was written.

9.2 Possible Explanations

A. Gratitude for Commercial Development
Williams had "several times heard to express his high appreciation of the efforts of the late and present Lord Bute in promoting the commercial interests of the town and district." The Bute family's development of the docks and railways had massively increased the value of Williams's own property and royalties. He may have viewed the bequest as a kind of patriotic or commercial tribute.

B. Anti-Familial Sentiment
The total exclusion of family, combined with the provision for a dog, pony, and manservant, suggests deep estrangement or misanthropy. His reclusive lifestyle, never receiving company, points to a personality that had withdrawn from human society.

C. The Clock and Scientific Connection
Williams's "solar observations" and his regulation of a tower clock suggest a mind drawn to precision, order, and perhaps symbolic gestures. The Clock Tower at Cardiff Castle—Bute's great architectural project—was itself a monument to time, astronomy, and planetary observation. The tower contains statues of the planets and zodiac signs, and its rooms are themed around time, the seasons, and the universe. It is conceivable that Williams, as an amateur astronomer and horologist, felt a kind of intellectual kinship with the project, even if he never met its patron.

D. The Quarry as Symbolic Connection
The quarry lease created a physical, economic bond between Williams's land and Bute's great projects. The stone from Pwllypant literally built the infrastructure of modern Cardiff. Williams may have seen his legacy as inseparable from Bute's vision.

E. Psychological Factors
At 62, a bachelor, living in isolation, with no children, Williams may have simply lacked the normal familial attachments that would have motivated a conventional will. The dog, pony, and manservant may have been his closest companions.

9.3 What the Newspaper Got Wrong

The newspaper states that the stone selection for Cardiff Castle "was made many months subsequent to the date of the will." This is true. But it also implies that the quarry lease was "some three years ago" (i.e., 1867), while other sources confirm the lease was in 1866. The discrepancy is minor but worth noting.

The newspaper also accepts the "daily contemporary's" claim that Williams had no needy relatives, only to correct it with its own correspondent's information. This suggests that the initial coverage was incomplete or biased.

SECTION XI: CONCLUSIONS

The bequest of William Evans Williams to the 3rd Marquess of Bute remains one of the most extraordinary acts of testamentary disposition in 19th-century Wales. It was not the result of personal friendship, religious sympathy, or financial need on the part of the beneficiary. It appears to have been driven by a combination of:

1. Gratitude for commercial development — the Bute family's docks and railways had enriched Williams through land values and royalties
2. Intellectual or symbolic affinity — Williams's interest in astronomy, time, and clocks may have created a psychological connection to Bute's great architectural projects
3. Social isolation and familial estrangement — Williams's reclusive life, bachelor status, and preference for animals and servants over family suggest a personality detached from conventional kinship obligations
4. The physical connection of the quarry — the stone from his land literally built modern Cardiff, making the bequest a kind of symbolic fusion of his property with Bute's vision

The Marquess of Bute acted with notable decency in dividing the personal estate among Williams's relations, while retaining the real property that had strategic value to his estate. The case was a public sensation because it violated every expectation of Victorian testamentary behavior: it excluded family, favored a stranger, ignored religious difference, and transferred wealth from a local gentleman to an already-immensely-rich aristocrat.

The anomaly was not a crime, nor was it apparently the result of fraud or undue influence. It was simply inexplicable—a "singular bequest" whose reason was, and remains, "purely conjectural."

To understand the Williamses at their absolute social and financial peak—primarily spanning the mid-18th through the late 19th century across Glamorgan—it is necessary to look past standard published genealogies and look into the unsaid dynamics, architectural signaling, and unrecorded leverage that defined the family's zenith.
At their height, the family occupied a rare dual space: they were landed gentry holding ancient manorial weight in rural parishes (such as Great House Farm / Tŷ Mawr in Llandough, with roots traced in manorial records back to 1667), while simultaneously positioning themselves as crucial powerbrokers in the massive industrialization of South Wales.
1. The Shadow Power of Boundary and Mineral Control
While public history remembers high-profile aristocrats like the Marquesses of Bute as the "creators of modern Cardiff," the reality on the ground was that the Bute estate could not expand without dealing directly with established local families like the Williamses.
• The Royalty Leverage: At their peak, the family’s wealth was not merely derived from conventional agricultural rents, but from strategic mineral holdings and key transport rights-of-way. Holding freeholds with quarrying rights (such as Pwllypant) and land sitting directly on railway lines (like the Rhymney Railway) meant they held direct economic leverage over imperial-scale developers.
• The Unsaid Imperial Friction: Archives reveal that while public declarations often praised industrial progress, the day-to-day legal reality was defined by hard-nosed negotiations over ground rents, wayleaves, and stone royalties. Lord Bute’s empire paid significant annual sums back to local freeholders—a stark inversion of the standard feudal dynamic where the local gentry paid tribute upward.
2. Architectural Pretentions: The Paris Exhibition Clock & Astronomical Obsession
At the height of their influence in the 1860s, the physical presentation of Williams family estates moved far beyond standard farming seats. The architectural and scientific choices made at sites like Pwllypant and Tŷ Mawr revealed a high-status obsession with precision, permanence, and modern technological display.
• The Paris Exhibition Clock: Following the 1867 Paris Universal Exposition, William Evans Williams acquired a massive, state-of-the-art tower clock and mounted it above his estate.
• Solar Regulation as Power: Rather than relying on railway time or local church bells, Williams regulated this massive clock personally using a transit instrument/theodolite via astronomical solar noon. In Victorian high society, controlling time at a local level was the ultimate expression of autarchy and intellect—it signaled that the estate ran on celestial alignment, entirely independent of municipal or external authority.
• Stone of Immortality: The family's local quarries provided the heavy Caerphilly/Pwllypant stone used for structural foundations across the Cardiff Docks expansion and key elements of William Burges's Gothic Revival redesign of Cardiff Castle (including the Clock Tower). The family was literally embedding its land into the permanent monumentality of the region.
3. Social Isolation as Elite Posturing
The popular press often categorized the reclusive habits of the late-period Williamses as mere eccentricities or misanthropy. However, within 19th-century elite culture, extreme social withdrawal was frequently a calculated rejection of nouveau-riche industrial society.
• Selective Kinship: At their peak, family marital alliances were executed with precision. Alliances linked the line directly into parliamentary power structures (such as Mary Ann Williams’s 1865 marriage to William Henry Gore-Langton, MP for Bristol and Mayor of Bristol, tying the family to the elite Somerset gentry of Newton Park).
• The Feudal Landlord Display: Despite keeping the regional bourgeois "company" at arm's length, the patriarchal bond with the local working class was maintained through traditional feudal displays. At key family milestones and funerals (such as Eglwysilan), tenant farmers were fully outfitted in bespoke Cardiff drapery (silk scarves, top hats, gloves) and hosted at lavish banquets—a public performance designed to show that while the family disdained the rising middle class, they fully owned their role as benevolent feudal overlords.
4. The Hidden Record: Deeds, Demolitions, and Disinheritance
The true climax of the family's social peak lies in what was intentionally kept out of official legal records or buried in private archives:
• The £200,000 Unconditional Bequest: When William Evans Williams drafted his 1867 will leaving an estate valued at £200,000 (roughly £28+ million today) to the 3rd Marquess of Bute—bypassing immediate relatives—it represented a deliberate act of legacy engineering. By gifting the real estate directly to the richest peer in the realm, he ensured the landholdings would never be broken up by lesser heirs, effectively cementing the family's land holdings into the immortal Bute estate portfolio.
• Unrecorded Manorial Title Lines: Private archive research into the family's ancestral holdings at Great House Farm / Tŷ Mawr reveals a long history of unrecorded manorial transfers, custom-of-the-manor tenures, and undocumented boundary line adjustments dating from the 17th century through the late 20th century. The legal fights over these specific parcels in modern times—including missing 19th-century deed packages and mid-20th-century municipal demolitions—highlight how much of the family's peak power was held through unwritten local precedence, indigenous manorial status, and physical possession that defied modern registration systems.

Expanded sources of Williams’s wealth and landholdings
Industrialisation added royalties from the Energlyn colliery and the Rhymney Railway, on top of the Pwll-y-pant quarry lease (several hundred pounds annually from 1866). He also held substantial land in the neighbourhood of Caerphilly plus properties in Newport and Breconshire. These income streams help explain the ~£200,000 estate valuation.

Earlier history of the house and family
Pwll-y-pant (Pwllypant) House’s oldest parts may date to the 17th century. It was home to the Williams family from the late 18th century or earlier. In the 18th century it had been occupied by the Reverend David Williams (1709–1785) , a figure in the mid-18th-century Methodist Revival. William Evans Williams (the bachelor magistrate) was the mid-19th-century owner who installed the massive tower clock (almost certainly the one from the 1867 Paris Exhibition).

Post-bequest occupation and later history of the property (more granular)
After accepting the real estate, the Marquess of Bute leased the house to John Stuart Corbett (son of the Archdeacon of York and great-grandson of the 3rd Earl of Bute). Corbett extended the building for his family and library; he was a keen historian and genealogist.
• His son of the same name inherited, lived there, and daily-commuted by train from Llanbradach to Cardiff as solicitor to the Bute estate; he died at the house in 1921.
• The last family member in residence was Sybil Corbett-Williams, who died in 1957.
• Early 1960s: home for disabled children.
• Later: nightclub called Corbetts.
• Then the Cedar Tree pub until 2015, when it became a Toby Carvery (still operating under that name in recent references; postcode CF83 3HX).

Associated structures include a coach house (shown on the 1841 tithe map; front wing probably 18th-century, with later 19th-century clock-tower and dormer additions linked to Williams’s era) and garden.

Archival and legal footprint
Estate papers (deeds, rentals, mining papers, 18th–19th centuries) relating to William Evans Williams of Pwllypant survive within the Bute Estate Records at the National Library of Wales (references D242–59). This confirms the property and mineral interests passed into the Bute archive after the bequest.

John Stuart Corbett (lessee) – fuller biographical context
Born 16 May 1845 (eldest son of the earlier Bute agent John Stuart Corbett). Educated at Cheltenham, admitted solicitor 1867, practised in Cardiff, and served as clerk to the Llandaff magistrates. Married 1872 to Blanche Evans. Appointed Bute estate solicitor in 1890 (succeeding his brother James Andrew Corbett) and held the post until retirement in 1917—covering the peak of the South Wales coalfield and the “war of the railways.” After 1890 he focused on historical research on the lordship of Glamorgan; his papers and notes were published posthumously as Glamorgan (1925) with a memoir. He died 9 March 1921. His brother James Andrew had earlier edited Rice Merrick’s Booke of Glamorganshire’s Antiquities (1887).
