PARTICULARS OF CLAIM

APPLICATION FOR DECLARATORY RELIEF

Concerning the procedural consequences of the proceedings in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2

________________________________________

PART A – INTRODUCTION

1. The Claimant, Sion Buckler, is a descendant of the Williams/Buckler family who occupied Great House Farm, Llandough, Vale of Glamorgan, for generations. He brings this claim in a representative capacity on behalf of the family estate.

2. This claim seeks declaratory relief concerning the procedural consequences of the proceedings in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2. It does not seek to reopen or appeal the judgment on its merits. Rather, it seeks declarations that the procedure adopted in those proceedings failed to determine the competing proprietary, equitable and historical rights affected by the possession order, with the result that those rights were effectively extinguished without ever having been adjudicated.

3. The Claimant contends that the 1987 proceedings were conducted as a conventional possession action concerned solely with immediate entitlement to possession. However, the dispute engaged longstanding occupation, alleged equitable interests, proprietary expectations, overriding interests arising from actual occupation, and other historical claims concerning Great House Farm. Those matters were neither pleaded nor determined before the irreversible consequences of the possession order occurred.

4. The relief sought is directed to the adequacy of the procedure employed, rather than the correctness of the Court of Appeal's reasoning on the issues placed before it.

________________________________________

PART B – FACTUAL BACKGROUND AND CHRONOLOGY

The Property

5. The property the subject of these proceedings is Great House Farm, also known as Ty Mawr, situated at Llandough, Vale of Glamorgan ("the Property"). The Property comprised a farmhouse, outbuildings, and agricultural land.

6. The Claimant believes, based upon family tradition and the documentary records referred to at paragraph 7 below, that the Williams family have occupied the Property since at least 1667.

7. The Williams family name appears in Llandough parish records (Exhibit SB1) and land tax assessments (Exhibit SB2) from the 18th century onwards. The Claimant infers from this documentary evidence that the family's occupation was continuous from the late 17th century, but this is a matter of inference from an incomplete record requiring further investigation.

8. Archaeological evidence confirms that the site has been occupied continuously since at least the Roman period, with a monastic grange established by Tewkesbury Abbey by the 12th century (Exhibit SB3). The farmhouse itself was essentially 17th-century in construction, though it may have incorporated earlier medieval fabric (Exhibit SB4).

The 1871 Arrangement with Daniel Thomas

9. The Claimant believes, based upon family records (Exhibit SB5) and oral tradition, that in or about 1871 an arrangement was entered into between members of the Williams family and one Daniel Thomas concerning lime quarrying rights and land associated with the Property. The Claimant understands that under this arrangement, Daniel Thomas was permitted to quarry lime on the family's land on condition that, upon completion of the quarrying, the Williams family would acquire equitable title to the land so quarried.

10. The precise terms of the 1871 arrangement require investigation. The Claimant has not yet located the original agreement, if one exists in documentary form.

11. The Claimant contends that, if the 1871 arrangement is established, it gave rise to the following interests:

    (a) an equitable interest in the land subject to the quarrying arrangement, arising from the agreement that title would pass upon completion;

    (b) a constructive trust over the proceeds or benefit of the quarrying operations; and

    (c) proprietary estoppel, in that the Williams family permitted the quarrying in reliance upon the representation that they would acquire title, and acted to their detriment by permitting the extraction of minerals from their land.

12. The Claimant infers from the absence of any reference to the 1871 arrangement in the 1987 proceedings that the legal effect of that arrangement was never investigated within the possession proceedings. The arrangement was not pleaded, no evidence was adduced concerning it, and the Court made no determination as to whether it gave rise to proprietary or equitable interests binding upon subsequent owners.

The 1938 Sale and the Missing Deeds

13. The Claimant believes that in or about 1938 the Property was the subject of a sale (Exhibit SB6). The Claimant has been unable to locate the deeds of title and conveyance records for this transaction (Exhibit SB7). The absence of these deeds prevents a complete understanding of:

    (a) the precise nature of the interest conveyed in 1938;

    (b) whether the Williams family's interests were expressly or impliedly reserved;

    (c) whether the purchaser took subject to any existing equitable interests; and

    (d) the chain of title between 1938 and the registration of BP Properties Ltd in 1982.

The 1962 Possession Proceedings

14. The Claimant believes that in December 1962 possession proceedings were commenced against the Williams/Buckler family (Exhibit SB8). A possession order was obtained but was not enforced. The Claimant contends that these proceedings were deficient in that:

    (a) they did not determine any of the family's alleged equitable or proprietary interests;

    (b) the possession order was obtained without any inquiry into the family's historical claims or the 1871 arrangement; and

    (c) the non-enforcement of the order for approximately twelve years (until 1974) allowed the family to continue in occupation, during which time their adverse possession claim continued to mature.

The 1969 Transfer

15. The Claimant believes that in or about 1969 there was a transfer of the freehold interest in the Property (Exhibit SB9). The Claimant contends that this transfer:

    (a) was effected without notice to the Williams/Buckler family;

    (b) was not accompanied by any inquiry into the family's asserted interests; and

    (c) may have been registered without disclosure of the family's actual occupation or the 1871 arrangement.

The 1974 Licence

16. In October 1974, BP Pension Trust Limited and BP Properties Limited wrote to Mrs Williams (also referred to as Mrs Buckler) offering her a licence to occupy the farmhouse and garden rent-free for the remainder of her life (Exhibit SB10). Mrs Williams never expressly accepted or rejected the offer. She remained in occupation until her death in 1983.

17. The Claimant does not dispute that the Court of Appeal determined the legal effect of the 1974 letters upon the running of limitation. The Claimant's case is that the Court's determination was confined to that narrow issue and did not extend to:

    (a) whether the 1974 letters were capable of overriding or extinguishing the family's pre-existing equitable interests;

    (b) whether Mrs Williams had capacity to bind the family estate by her conduct (or inaction) in relation to the letters;

    (c) whether the letters operated as a licence in circumstances where the family disputed the offeror's title; and

    (d) whether the procedural framework of a possession action was adequate to determine these questions.

The 1982 Registration

18. BP Properties Limited was registered as proprietor of the Property in November 1982 (Exhibit SB11). The Claimant contends that:

    (a) the registration did not eliminate any equitable interests or overriding interests arising from actual occupation;

    (b) the Williams/Buckler family remained in actual occupation of the farmhouse and garden at the date of registration; and

    (c) section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 required that the family's interests, if subsisting, be protected as overriding interests.

The 1987 Proceedings

19. In BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2, the Court of Appeal held that the 1974 letters constituted a licence which terminated the family's adverse possession, with the consequence that time ceased to run in their favour and BP Properties Ltd was entitled to possession.

20. The Court confined its determination to the legal effect of the alleged licence and the law governing adverse possession. It did not determine:

    (a) whether the family's alleged equitable interests under the 1871 arrangement existed or were binding;

    (b) whether actual occupation created overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925;

    (c) whether proprietary rights arising from the 1871 arrangement required independent determination;

    (d) whether any trust, proprietary estoppel or constructive trust arose from the family's longstanding occupation;

    (e) whether customary or prescriptive rights existed; or

    (f) whether BP Properties Ltd's title was subject to any equitable obligations arising from the family's longstanding occupation.

The Demolition

21. Following the possession order, the historic farmhouse at Great House Farm was demolished (Exhibit SB12). The demolition rendered restoration of the previous position impossible and extinguished any practical possibility of the family asserting their historical rights in relation to the Property.

22. The Claimant contends that the demolition was a direct consequence of the possession proceedings and that the irreversible nature of that consequence distinguishes this case from an ordinary possession dispute. Where proceedings will permanently deprive an occupier of land, extinguish longstanding occupation and render any future remedy ineffective, the Court should be satisfied that every material competing interest has either been determined or appropriately reserved.

PART C – THE PROCEDURAL FAILURE ALLEGED

23. The Claimant alleges that the fundamental defect in the 1987 proceedings was procedural, not substantive. The Court of Appeal applied the law of adverse possession correctly to the issue presented to it. The defect lay in the procedural framework within which those issues were defined and the scope of the inquiry permitted.

24. The Defendant's claim was advanced as an ordinary possession action under the County Court procedure then applicable. By adopting that procedural characterisation, the Court confined its inquiry to the question of immediate entitlement to possession. The pleadings did not raise, and the Court was not invited to determine, the full extent of the competing legal and equitable rights asserted in respect of the Property.

25. The practical consequence was that a procedure designed to determine possession also brought about the irreversible extinction of alleged proprietary, equitable and historical interests without those interests themselves being determined. The possession order became the mechanism by which:

    (a) the family's physical occupation was terminated;

    (b) the historic farmhouse was demolished;

    (c) any proprietary, equitable or historical rights capable of being asserted in relation to the Property lost practical effect; and

    (d) the family's ability to seek independent adjudication of those rights was rendered futile.

26. The Claimant therefore contends that the proceedings determined the consequence of the dispute (deprivation of possession and demolition of the farmhouse) without first determining every material right affected by that consequence.

27. The present claim does not assert that the Court of Appeal misapplied the law of adverse possession to the issue presented. Rather, it asserts that the issue presented to the Court was procedurally incomplete and therefore incapable of finally determining all rights affected by the resulting possession order.

________________________________________

PART D – THE RIGHTS NOT DETERMINED

28. The Court of Appeal determined the legal consequences of the 1974 letters and their effect upon adverse possession. It did not determine the following matters, each of which the Claimant contends required adjudication before possession could lawfully be granted:

Equitable Interests under the 1871 Arrangement

29. The Court did not determine whether the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas gave rise to:

    (a) an equitable interest in the land subject to quarrying;

    (b) a constructive trust over the proceeds of quarrying; or

    (c) proprietary estoppel based upon detrimental reliance.

Overriding Interests under Section 70(1)(g) LRA 1925

30. The Court did not determine whether the family's actual occupation of the farmhouse and garden at the date of registration in 1982 created overriding interests binding upon the registered proprietor under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925.

Trust, Proprietary Estoppel and Constructive Trust

31. The Court did not determine whether the family's longstanding occupation, expenditure upon the Property, and reliance upon their asserted rights gave rise to:

    (a) a resulting trust;

    (b) a constructive trust;

    (c) proprietary estoppel; or

    (d) any other equitable interest recognised by the general law.

Customary and Prescriptive Rights

32. The Court did not determine whether the family's longstanding occupation and use of the Property gave rise to customary rights or rights by prescription, including rights of way, grazing rights, or other incidents of agricultural tenure.

The Procedural Vehicle

33. Nor did the Court determine whether possession proceedings constituted the appropriate procedural vehicle through which such issues could lawfully be extinguished, or whether the family's rights required determination in separate proceedings.

34. The Claimant therefore distinguishes between:

    (a) the issue actually decided by the Court (the effect of the 1974 letters upon adverse possession); and

    (b) the wider body of rights which were affected by the resulting order but were never themselves adjudicated.

________________________________________

PART E – WHY THE PROCEDURE WAS INADEQUATE

35. Possession proceedings are primarily directed towards determining immediate entitlement to possession between the parties before the Court. Such proceedings are not ordinarily intended to constitute a comprehensive inquiry into every equitable, historical or proprietary interest which may exist in relation to the land unless those matters are properly pleaded and determined.

36. In the present case, the procedural framework failed in the following respects:

Pleadings

37. The Defendant's claim was pleaded as a straightforward possession action. The family's alleged equitable interests, overriding interests, and historical claims were not pleaded by either party. The Court was therefore not invited to determine those matters.

Evidential Omissions

38. No evidence was adduced concerning:

    (a) the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas;

    (b) the family's continuous occupation from the late 17th century;

    (c) the circumstances of the 1938 sale and the missing deeds;

    (d) the 1962 proceedings and the non-enforcement of the possession order;

    (e) the 1969 transfer and the chain of title; or

    (f) the family's actual occupation at the date of the 1982 registration.

Procedural Scope

39. The County Court procedure adopted did not permit the comprehensive determination of the family's competing rights. The Court was confined to the issues raised by the pleadings, which did not extend to the equitable and proprietary interests now asserted.

Consequences of the Order

40. Where proceedings will permanently deprive an occupier of land, extinguish longstanding occupation and render any future remedy ineffective, the Court should be satisfied that every material competing interest has either been determined or appropriately reserved. The Claimant alleges that this safeguard was absent in the present case.

41. Consequently, the possession order became the practical mechanism by which alleged competing rights ceased to have any effective value despite never having received independent judicial determination.

________________________________________

PART F – EQUITY AND ACTUAL OCCUPATION

42. The Claimant relies upon the longstanding distinction recognised by English law between strict legal title and equitable interests affecting land.

43. Registration under the Land Registration Act 1925 did not eliminate equitable rights or overriding interests arising from actual occupation. Section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 expressly recognised that persons in actual occupation could possess interests binding upon a registered proprietor notwithstanding registration.

44. The Williams/Buckler family remained in actual occupation of the farmhouse and garden at all material times, including at the date of registration in 1982. Their continuous occupation was capable of giving rise to overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) and required judicial examination beyond the limited question of adverse possession.

45. Whether those interests ultimately succeeded is distinct from whether they required independent procedural determination before possession was ordered.

________________________________________

PART G – THE FAMILY'S COMPETING RIGHTS

Long Possession

46. The Williams/Buckler family occupied Great House Farm continuously from at least the late 17th century, and the Claimant believes from as early as 1667. The Claimant contends that such occupation was capable of giving rise to legal and equitable rights recognised by the common law and the law relating to adverse possession.

The 1871 Arrangement

47. The Claimant relies upon the arrangement believed to have been entered into with Daniel Thomas in or about 1871 concerning lime and land rights associated with the Property, as pleaded at paragraphs 9–12 above. The Claimant believes that the legal effect of that arrangement was never investigated within the possession proceedings and remains capable of giving rise to proprietary or equitable consequences requiring judicial determination.

Equitable Interests

48. The Claimant further alleges that the family's occupation was capable of giving rise to equitable interests arising through trust, proprietary estoppel, reliance, acquiescence or other recognised equitable doctrines. Whether such interests ultimately existed was never determined because those questions were outside the scope of the possession proceedings.

Actual Occupation

49. Throughout the material period the family remained in actual occupation of the Property. The Claimant contends that this occupation required consideration under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 before any final order affecting the land was made.

Customary and Prescriptive Rights

50. The Claimant also contends that longstanding occupation and use of the Property were capable of giving rise to customary or prescriptive rights which likewise remained undetermined.

PART H – CONSEQUENCES OF THE PROCEDURE

51. The consequences of the possession proceedings extended far beyond the determination of immediate possession. The proceedings ultimately resulted in the permanent removal of the Williams/Buckler family from Great House Farm.

52. Following possession, the historic farmhouse was demolished, rendering restoration of the previous position impossible. Any proprietary, equitable or historical rights capable of being asserted in relation to the Property thereby lost practical effect without independent adjudication.

53. The irreversible nature of those consequences distinguishes the present proceedings from an ordinary possession dispute and underscores the necessity of procedural adequacy where deprivation of land is at stake.

PART I – ISSUES REMAINING UNDETERMINED

54. For the assistance of the Court, the Claimant sets out below the specific questions that were never adjudicated in the 1987 proceedings but which the Claimant contends required determination before possession could lawfully be granted:

    1. Whether the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas gave rise to an equitable interest in the land subject to quarrying, a constructive trust over the proceeds, or proprietary estoppel by detrimental reliance.

    2. Whether the Williams/Buckler family's continuous occupation of Great House Farm from the late 17th century (and the Claimant believes from 1667) gave rise to proprietary rights by adverse possession or otherwise.

    3. Whether the circumstances of the 1938 sale reserved any interest in the Williams/Buckler family, and whether the missing deeds affect the validity of the chain of title.

    4. Whether the 1962 possession proceedings, having been obtained without inquiry into the family's historical claims, were capable of affecting their equitable interests.

    5. Whether the 1969 transfer of the freehold was effected with notice of the family's asserted interests, and whether the purchaser took subject to those interests.

    6. Whether the family's actual occupation of the farmhouse and garden at the date of registration in 1982 created overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925.

    7. Whether the 1974 letters were capable of overriding or extinguishing pre-existing equitable interests, and whether Mrs Williams had authority to bind the family estate by her conduct.

    8. Whether the family's longstanding occupation, expenditure upon the Property, and reliance upon their asserted rights gave rise to a resulting trust, constructive trust, proprietary estoppel, or other equitable interest.

    9. Whether the family possessed customary or prescriptive rights in relation to the Property, including rights of way, grazing rights, or other incidents of agricultural tenure.

    10. Whether possession proceedings were the appropriate procedural vehicle to extinguish the rights referred to in paragraphs 1–9 above, or whether those rights required independent adjudication.

    11. Whether the irreversible consequences of the possession order (deprivation of the family home and demolition of the farmhouse) were proportionate to the scope of the inquiry conducted.

55. The Claimant contends that each of these questions required adjudication before the possession order was made, and that the failure to determine them renders the procedure inadequate.

PART J – GROUNDS FOR DECLARATORY RELIEF

Ground 1

56. The procedure adopted in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 did not determine every competing proprietary, equitable or historical interest affected by the possession order.

Ground 2

57. The Court was not invited to determine whether possession proceedings were procedurally sufficient to extinguish the family's asserted interests, or whether those interests required independent adjudication.

Ground 3

58. The proceedings focused upon adverse possession and the legal effect of the 1974 letters rather than the wider body of competing rights asserted by the family.

Ground 4

59. The irreversible consequences of the proceedings (deprivation of the family home and demolition of the farmhouse) required a level of procedural scrutiny proportionate to the rights affected.

Ground 5

60. The possession order produced consequences extending beyond the issues actually determined within the proceedings, including the practical extinguishment of rights that were never adjudicated.

________________________________________

PART K – RELIEF SOUGHT

61. The Claimant respectfully seeks the following declarations:

    (a) that the proceedings in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 determined the question of entitlement to possession but did not determine the family's alleged equitable interests under the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas;

    (b) that the proceedings did not determine whether the family's actual occupation created overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925;

    (c) that the proceedings did not determine whether the family's longstanding occupation gave rise to trust, proprietary estoppel, constructive trust or other equitable interests;

    (d) that the proceedings did not determine whether the family possessed customary or prescriptive rights in relation to the Property;

    (e) that the procedure employed was not directed to determining those wider rights and was not adequate to do so;

    (f) that the possession order produced irreversible consequences (deprivation of the family home and demolition of the farmhouse) in respect of rights that were never adjudicated; and

    (g) that where proceedings produce irreversible deprivation of land, the procedure adopted must be adequate to determine every material right affected by the resulting order.

62. The Claimant further seeks such consequential orders as the Court considers appropriate, including any inquiry into matters left undetermined by the 1987 proceedings and any further relief the Court considers necessary in the interests of justice.

PART L – STANDING AND JURISDICTION

63. The Claimant is a descendant and representative of the Williams/Buckler family whose interests were directly affected by the 1987 proceedings.

64. The Claimant accordingly possesses a sufficient interest to seek declaratory relief concerning the procedural consequences of those proceedings.

65. Jurisdiction is relied upon under the Senior Courts Act 1981, the Civil Procedure Rules, and the inherent jurisdiction of the High Court to grant declaratory relief where appropriate. Declaratory relief is sought because the claim does not seek to reopen the Court of Appeal's decision on its merits, but rather to obtain declarations regarding the procedural scope and legal consequences of those proceedings, which consequences continue to affect rights asserted by the Claimant.
