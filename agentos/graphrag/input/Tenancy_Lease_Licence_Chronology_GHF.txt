PURPOSE AND METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

This document reconstructs every known lease, tenancy, licence, agreement, offer,
acceptance, rejection and payment relating to the occupation of Great House Farm
(Ty Mawr), Llandough, by the Williams and Buckler families. It is organised
strictly chronologically and groups all tenancy-related events into a single
narrative thread.

The reconstruction does not accept the estate's tenancy narrative at face value.
Instead, it subjects every estate assertion to scrutiny, highlights documentary
gaps, and records the family's consistent position that they held ownership
through hereditary title — not tenancy.

Every major claim is tagged with its evidential basis:
  [ESTABLISHED FACT]      — Securely documented and independently verifiable
  [DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE]  — Contemporaneous record, generally reliable
  [FAMILY TESTIMONY]      — Oral tradition, personal recollection, notebook
  [WORKING HYPOTHESIS]    — Interpretive inference pending further evidence
  [EVIDENCE GAP]          — Document known or expected to exist but not located

PART I — BEFORE THE TWENTIETH CENTURY
================================================================================

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1667 — THE ORIGINAL TRANSACTION
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] The Williams family have occupied Great House Farm from at
least 1667. The property is documented in estate records as having been held
under manorial arrangement from this date.

[FAMILY TESTIMONY / WORKING HYPOTHESIS] Family evidence maintains that the
Williams family acquired Great House through a 999-year lease — a "virtual
freehold" — in 1667. Such leases were commonly regarded as providing hereditary
possession with only nominal obligations, passing father to son in practice.

[EVIDENCE GAP] No surviving original deed of the 1667 transaction has yet been
located. The National Library of Wales holds the Bute Estate Collection (NLW
D 219, "Llandough manorial leases and agreements"), which is the most likely
archive series for tracing the historic Williams tenancy and any surviving
evidence relating to the 1667 transaction. The precise legal nature of the 1667
transaction remains unproven.

[SCRUTINY] The reconstruction repeatedly distinguishes between two competing
legal understandings:

  Estate understanding:    The family remained leaseholders under the manorial
                           estate, part of a continuous sequence of manorial
                           tenancies recorded in Bute Estate ledgers.

  Family understanding:    The transaction effectively created hereditary
                           ownership — a virtual freehold — which the family
                           understood as a purchase rather than a tenancy.

[CHALLENGE] If the family interpretation is correct, every later tenancy
document may represent an attempt to redefine an existing proprietary interest
rather than create a fresh landlord-tenant relationship. The absence of the
original 1667 deed means the estate's interpretation rests on its own
self-interested records rather than on independently verified evidence.

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] The 1824 David Stewart survey (Glamorgan Archives ref.
DB/E/1–2) records "Great House Farm alias Cedfin" within the Marquess of Bute's
Glamorgan estates. The recurring appearance of "Great House alias Cedfin" within
estate surveys indicates continuity of identity despite later administrative
changes in naming.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1818–1835 — LAMBERT WILLIAMS AGREEMENTS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] Archive references indicate agreements involving Lambert
Williams of Cardiff and the Bute Estate during the period 1818–1835 (Glamorgan
Archives ref. D160/1–4). Lambert Williams was a Ward Constable in Cardiff,
demonstrating high-status standing.

[SCRUTINY] These agreements establish that formal legal dealings continued
between the Williams family and the estate. However, the surviving catalogue
entries do not presently establish whether these agreements:
  • renewed an existing lease;
  • altered tenancy conditions;
  • conveyed land;
  • surrendered land; or
  • recognised ownership.

[CHALLENGE] The original documents remain important outstanding evidence. The
estate's summary catalogue descriptions do not disclose the legal character of
these agreements. Without inspection of the originals, it is impossible to
determine whether they support the estate's tenancy narrative or the family's
ownership claim.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1876 — THE INDUSTRIAL CARVE-OUT AND THE DANIEL THOMAS ARRANGEMENT
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] In 1876, the Bute Estate recorded the severance of 33 acres
formerly part of Great House Farm for the Llandough Limeworks (Glamorgan
Archives ref. D153/4–8). This is an established documentary event.

[FAMILY TESTIMONY] Family testimony records that an agreement with Daniel
Thomas & Son followed the severance. According to family evidence:
  • quarrying rights were exchanged;
  • title to the farmhouse and garden plot would ultimately pass to the family;
  • deeds once existed;
  • those deeds later disappeared.

[EVIDENCE GAP] No surviving documentary copy of the Daniel Thomas agreement has
yet been located. The Bute Estate → Daniel Thomas deed and index card are
reported missing from Cardiff Library (Family Testimony). This is one of the
most significant outstanding evidential gaps in the entire chronology.

[SCRUTINY] The reconstruction identifies 1876 as the greatest documentary turning
point. The following occurred simultaneously:
  • Thirty-three acres removed for industrial purposes;
  • Daniel Thomas arrangements allegedly negotiated;
  • Family evidence says quarry rights exchanged for eventual ownership;
  • Missing deeds become central to later ownership arguments.

[CHALLENGE — THE SEPARATE PLOT HYPOTHESIS] Post-1876, the family is believed to
have gained absolute title / freehold to the house and garden plot, with
surrounding farmland remaining on lease. Any mention of rent and tenure
thereafter referred to the surrounding farmland still on the lease agreement.

This hypothesis would explain a series of otherwise difficult-to-reconcile events
in the litigation chronology:
  • The 1955 partial enforcement (farmhouse excluded);
  • The 1962 fresh action specifically for farmhouse and garden;
  • Mrs Williams's consistent refusal to accept tenancy offers for the farmhouse;
  • The 1974 unilateral licence (offered for the farmhouse only, not the whole
    farm).

[EVIDENCE GAP] No explicit deed of severance conveying the farmhouse plot to the
Williams family has yet been located. The 1916 yearly agricultural tenancy
granted by the Marquess of Bute to John Williams appears, on its face, to treat
the whole farm (including the farmhouse) as subject to a single tenancy. This
remains unresolved.

PART II — THE TWENTIETH CENTURY: THE TENANCY NARRATIVE EMERGES
================================================================================

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1916 — THE YEARLY AGRICULTURAL TENANCY TO JOHN WILLIAMS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] In February 1916, the Marquess of Bute granted a yearly
agricultural tenancy of Great House Farm to Mr John Williams (Mrs Mary
Williams's maternal grandfather). On paper, Bute was still treated as freehold
owner, and John Williams as tenant.

[SCRUTINY] This document forms the foundation of almost every later court
argument. It is described in the reconstruction as:

    "the clearest documentary divergence between estate paperwork (tenant)
     and family belief (owner)."

[CHALLENGE] The reconstruction identifies what is presently unknown about this
critical document:
  [EVIDENCE GAP] • signed copy — not identified
                 • attached plans — not identified
                 • exact acreage — not verified
                 • boundaries — not verified
                 • whether farmhouse included — not verified
                 • whether curtilage included — not verified
                 • whether previous rights surrendered — not verified

[CHALLENGE] If the Williams family held a hereditary interest from 1667, the
1916 tenancy may have been experienced as bureaucratic tidying-up of a
relationship they already regarded as ownership, rather than as a fresh grant
that erased earlier rights. Large estates sometimes regularised long-standing
occupiers by putting them on formal tenancies even where those occupiers
believed they had stronger title.

[CHALLENGE] No document has been identified in which John Williams expressly
abandons any proprietary claim or acknowledges that all ownership rights
belonged to the estate. The estate narrative depends heavily upon the existence
of this 1916 tenancy, yet the evidential basis for the family's acceptance of
that tenancy status remains unclear.

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] By the 1880s–1890s, estate and census terminology had
systematically shifted from "Great House" (Ty Mawr) to "Great House Farm" (Ty
Mawr Farm) by the 1881 census. This administrative reclassification may have
reflected a deliberate demotion from a manorial residence (with potential
hereditary rights) to a mere commercial agricultural holding.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1924 — LLANDOUGH ESTATE SALE
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] "Great House" appears in the Llandough Estate sale of
1924 (Glamorgan Archives ref. DSA/6/701), suggesting the Bute/Byass estates
were packaging manorial lands.

[SCRUTINY] The sale related to the freehold reversion — a paper title — and did
not result in displacement of the Williams family, who remained in physical
occupation. This marks the beginning of a divergence between paper ownership and
living occupation.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1938 — WESTERN GROUND RENTS ACQUISITION AND SALE PARTICULARS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] On 16 November 1938, Western Ground Rents Ltd bought the
urban leaseholds of the Bute Glamorgan Estate from Mountjoy Ltd (a private
family company for the Bute Estate). The reversion was conveyed to Western
Ground Rents Ltd, a Cardiff-based ground-rent company.

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] Estate sale particulars described Great House Farm as a
single holding. No distinction appears between farmhouse and farmland.

[SCRUTINY] The Western Mail (Page 12, 16 November 1938) reported Great House
Farm advertised as "sold" at auction by Herbert R. Thomas (Bute Estate /
Mountjoy Ltd nominee). The Williams family nevertheless remained in
uninterrupted physical occupation.

[CHALLENGE] The 1938 auction description does not distinguish the farmhouse plot
from the surrounding farmland. However, the family's continued occupation
demonstrates the difference between documentary ownership and physical
possession. The sale did not convey vacant possession.

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] Hansard records from July 1961 reveal Western Ground
Rents was accused by MPs of "callous behaviour," "machinations," and
"arrogantly ignoring communications" from long-term residents. In 1966, Western
Ground Rents was described in Parliament as "one of the most inhuman and
unjust" landlords, with MPs stating they were "draining the lifeblood out of
Wales." In 1969, the company was explicitly labelled "The Worst in Wales" in
the House of Commons.

[SCRUTINY] This parliamentary record demonstrates that the Williams family was
not engaged in a fair legal fight against a neutral landlord. They were
confronting an entity with a documented history of systemic cruelty toward Welsh
residents — a context that BP later inherited and continued.

PART III — THE FREDERICK BUCKLER TENANCY AND ITS TERMINATION
================================================================================

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
2 February 1949 — NEW TENANCY TO FREDERICK BUCKLER
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT / DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] Following the alleged surrender of
John Williams' tenancy, a new yearly agricultural tenancy arose for Frederick
Buckler (who had married Mary Williams). Mrs Mary Williams, although married,
retained her maiden name in daily life and in dealings with the estate.

[CRITICAL EVIDENCE GAP] No executed written tenancy agreement for the 1949
tenancy has been identified within the presently available material. The Court
of Appeal accepted that a tenancy existed, but it inferred that tenancy from
conduct rather than from a surviving executed document.

[SCRUTINY] The Court of Appeal proceeded on the basis that John Williams
surrendered his tenancy, allowing a new yearly agricultural tenancy to arise in
favour of Frederick Buckler. That surrender is a critical event because it is
what the estate relied upon to argue that the legal chain of tenancy continued
rather than ownership passing.

[EVIDENCE GAP] No executed deed of surrender has been identified. If a surrender
occurred, questions naturally arise:
  • Was it written?
  • Was it oral?
  • Was it by operation of law?
  • Was it inferred from conduct?

The judgment accepts that a surrender occurred, but the surviving documents do
not include the instrument itself.

[CHALLENGE] English property law recognises that tenancy can arise without a
formally executed written agreement — through occupation, payment of rent,
acceptance of rent, and conduct consistent with tenancy. That explains why the
Court of Appeal was willing to recognise the 1949 agricultural tenancy despite
the apparent absence of an executed written agreement. It does not necessarily
mean a written tenancy never existed — but it does mean the court's finding rests
on inference rather than documentary proof.

[SCRUTINY — THE AGE ISSUE] By the time later tenancy arrangements are alleged,
John Williams was no longer a young man establishing a holding. He was an elderly
occupant whose family had occupied the property for generations. Long-established
occupiers frequently regard themselves as owners regardless of technical legal
descriptions. The reconstruction contains no evidence that independent legal
advice was obtained before any alleged surrender or acceptance.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1949–1953 — PERFORMANCE OF TENANCY AND RENT PAYMENTS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] The Court of Appeal records that rent was paid during this
period. However, the judgment does not reproduce rent books, receipts or
ledgers. Instead it refers to evidence heard at trial.

[EVIDENCE GAP] The uploaded reconstruction repeatedly identifies major
evidential gaps. Although the Court accepted rent was paid, the reconstruction
presently identifies no:
  • rent book
  • receipt
  • ledger
  • estate account
  • rent demand
  • annual rental schedule
  • payment register

within the available evidence.

[SCRUTINY] The only payment specifically discussed is the final payment under a
judgment for rent arrears, made sometime during 1953. The judgment does not
identify:
  • the court that entered the judgment;
  • the amount;
  • the land concerned;
  • the surviving documentary exhibits.

[CHALLENGE] The reconstruction therefore distinguishes between:
  Judicial finding (that rent was paid) and
  Primary documentary evidence (which has not been located).

The absence of identified rent documentation after the nineteenth century
weakens the documentary picture of continuous tenancy. If tenancy continued for
decades, the missing rent ledgers, rent books, annual accounts or demands would
ordinarily be valuable corroborative evidence.

[SCRUTINY — THE TENANCY APPEARS TO BECOME PROGRESSIVELY SMALLER] Looking across
the chronology:
  • Earlier arrangements concern the whole farm;
  • Later proceedings increasingly concern only the farmhouse and garden;
  • Later still there is merely a personal licence.

That progressive narrowing of the legal relationship is an important
chronological observation that suggests the estate itself may have recognised
that its claim did not extend uniformly across the entire property.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
2 February 1955 — TERMINATION OF TENANCY
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] The yearly agricultural tenancy was formally terminated. A
notice to quit expired on this date. Both parents were found to have the
required animus possidendi (intention to possess).

[SCRUTINY] The 1955 proceedings depended upon service of a notice to quit.
Without terminating a yearly tenancy first, possession proceedings would
generally have been premature. The validity of that notice therefore formed one
of the legal foundations for subsequent proceedings. The uploaded documents
summarise this step but do not reproduce the notice itself.

[EVIDENCE GAP] The notice to quit has not been located in the available
material. Its validity — including whether it correctly described the property,
whether it was properly served, and whether it covered the farmhouse and garden
as well as the agricultural land — cannot presently be tested.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1955 — HIGH COURT POSSESSION PROCEEDINGS AND PARTIAL ENFORCEMENT
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] Western Ground Rents Ltd brought possession proceedings in
the High Court under Order 14 and obtained a possession order for the whole
farm.

[ESTABLISHED FACT] On 4 July 1955, the possession order was enforced for the
farm EXCEPT the farmhouse and garden. The enforcement was limited because of
Mrs Mary Williams's strong objections following her leg amputation and
hospitalisation. The Williams' occupation of the farmhouse and garden remained
undisturbed.

[SCRUTINY] This is one of the principal anomalies in the entire chronology. If
the 1955 possession order covered the "whole farm," and if the tenancy had truly
ended over the whole holding, continued occupation of the farmhouse requires
explanation.

Possible explanations include:
  • humanitarian discretion (due to Mrs Williams's medical condition);
  • practical enforcement difficulties;
  • legal distinction between farmhouse and farmland (the Separate Plot
    Hypothesis);
  • recognition that the farmhouse occupied a different legal position.

[CHALLENGE] The documentary record does not resolve which explanation is correct.
Humanitarian considerations alone do not fully explain why the farmhouse and
garden were left undisturbed for a further seven years (until the 1962
proceedings) and indeed for decades thereafter. Courts and bailiffs frequently
exercise discretion in cases involving elderly or ill occupiers, but this
discretion does not typically extend to leaving a substantial farmhouse in the
occupation of persons against whom a possession order has been granted for the
"whole farm."

[SCRUTINY] If the farmhouse was a separate legal parcel — as the family
maintained — the partial enforcement was not humanitarian discretion at all.
It was legally necessary because the 1955 order could not sweep the farmhouse
into the same possession order as the leased farmland.

PART IV — MRS MARY WILLIAMS'S CONSISTENT ASSERTION OF OWNERSHIP
================================================================================

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1959 — TENANCY OFFER REFUSED
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE / FAMILY TESTIMONY] Western Ground Rents subsequently
offered another tenancy relating to the farmhouse and garden. According to the
reconstruction, Mary Williams refused.

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] Mrs Williams believed she had title to the property
through her grandfather. She produced no documents in court, stating that the
title was "stolen from the public library and the copy stolen from the loft of
the farmhouse."

[SCRUTINY] This refusal is significant because it demonstrates that she did not
accept the landlord's asserted title. Instead she continued occupying the
property while asserting ownership. She was also later offered, and refused,
alternative accommodation.

[CHALLENGE] The advisers of Western Ground Rents seem during these years to have
supposed that, if they attended at the farmhouse from time to time and asserted
Western Ground Rents' title, that was sufficient to prevent time running against
Western Ground Rents under the Limitation Act 1939. Plainly, however, that
notion was erroneous, as section 13 of the Act provides that "no person shall be
deemed to have been in possession of any land by reason only of having made a
formal entry thereon, and no continual or other claim upon or near any land
shall preserve any right of action to recover the land."

[NEWSPAPER EVIDENCE] A 1974 newspaper article ("Open day to save ancient Welsh
house," by Godfrey Francis) records Mrs Mary Williams, then 60, stating:

    "Planning permission to develop the land and build about 13 houses was
     given to B.P. Petroleum Pension Trust by the former Glamorgan County
     Council. But I will refuse to sell because not only is my home it is
     part of the nation's heritage."

And regarding ownership:

    "The ownership of the house has been in dispute for 25 years but my
     family has lived here for more than 400 years and my solicitors tell
     me I have a valid claim to it."

[SCRUTINY] This contemporary newspaper report — published years before the 1987
Court of Appeal hearing — demonstrates that Mrs Williams was not silent about
her ownership claim. She was publicly and explicitly asserting it. This directly
contradicts any later suggestion that she accepted tenancy status or that she
acquiesced in the estate's ownership claims.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1962 — COUNTY COURT PROCEEDINGS (FRESH ACTION FOR FARMHOUSE AND GARDEN)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] Western Ground Rents brought a fresh action in Cardiff
County Court against Mr Frederick Buckler and Mrs Buckler (sued as Mrs
Williams) for possession of the farmhouse and garden and mesne profits since
1955.

[ESTABLISHED FACT] On 11 December 1962, Judge Temple Morris QC made a
possession order together with an order for payment of mesne profits. The claim
did not result in eviction.

[SCRUTINY] This is a critical anomaly. If the 1955 High Court possession order
had already covered the "whole farm," why was a second possession action
required specifically for the farmhouse and garden?

Possible explanations include:
  • The 1955 order had not in fact covered the farmhouse plot;
  • The 1955 order required separate enforcement proceedings for the remaining
    portion;
  • The farmhouse occupied a different legal status that necessitated separate
    proceedings.

[CHALLENGE] The existence of the 1962 proceedings strongly suggests that the
legal position was not fully resolved in 1955. Whether this reflected
enforcement difficulties or a different legal issue (the Separate Plot
Hypothesis) requires examination of the complete court files — which have not
been located.

[SCRUTINY — MESNE PROFITS DISTINCTION] The 1962 proceedings claimed mesne
profits. Mesne profits are NOT rent. They are compensation claimed for
occupation after lawful entitlement to remain has allegedly ended. That
distinction is important. By claiming mesne profits instead of continuing rent,
the landlord was effectively asserting that tenancy had already ceased — yet the
family remained in occupation, suggesting the landlord recognised that mere
assertion of title was insufficient to remove them.

[EVIDENCE GAP] The complete 1962 County Court file has not been located.
Cardiff County Court (Enforcement Section) has confirmed that no records are
held on its current electronic case management system due to the age of the
proceedings. Historical retention, destruction, transfer schedules, paper
indexes, cause books, order books and any transfer to The National Archives or
Glamorgan Archives remain unresolved.

[SCRUTINY — WESTERN GROUND RENTS' LITIGATION STRATEGY] Western Ground Rents
kept the matter in the County Court (a lower court) rather than seeking a
declaration of title in the High Court. County courts are designed for
possession orders (evictions), not for complex, centuries-old title disputes
involving ancestral rights and Bute Estate covenants. By keeping it "small,"
they avoided a deep dive into the 300-year history of the Williams occupation.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1963 — SUSPENDED COMMITTAL ORDER
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] In June 1963, a suspended committal order was made against
Mr Frederick Buckler, referring to the December 11 1962 order for payment of
mesne profits.

[SCRUTINY] The use of committal proceedings — which can result in imprisonment —
against an elderly farmer who had occupied the land for decades, and whose wife
had recently suffered a leg amputation, demonstrates the asymmetry of power
between the institutional landlord and the occupying family.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1964–1965 — END OF AGRICULTURAL TENANCY AND FAILED NEGOTIATIONS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] In October 1964, the agricultural tenancy of Great House
Farm (held by the Williams family) officially came to an end. Mr Frederick
Buckler and his wife remained in the farmhouse.

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] In January/February 1965, negotiations took place between
Western Ground Rents and the Bucklers. Mr Buckler expressed frustration that the
company would not let him the agricultural land, as he wished to continue
farming.

[SCRUTINY] This is significant. Mr Buckler was not seeking to assert ownership
of the agricultural land — he was seeking to continue farming it. His
frustration was that the company would not grant him a tenancy of the
agricultural land. This is consistent with the Separate Plot Hypothesis: the
family believed they owned the farmhouse and garden, and were prepared to
acknowledge a tenancy relationship regarding the surrounding farmland.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
2 March 1965 — WEEKLY TENANCY PROPOSAL
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] Western Ground Rents' agents sent a formal offer to Mrs
Williams for a weekly tenancy of the farmhouse and garden at a rent of £2 per
week.

[ESTABLISHED FACT] Late March 1965: Mrs Williams and Mr Frederick Buckler did
not sign or return the tenancy agreement. They remained in the house, and
because they did not pay rent, their occupation began to be treated as
"adverse" by the owners.

[SCRUTINY] The proposed agreement was forwarded. Mary Williams never signed it.
It was never returned. No rent was paid. No contractual relationship therefore
appears to have arisen from this proposal. Instead, occupation simply continued.

[CHALLENGE] The family's position remains broadly constant across decades:
  • They did not sign the 1916 tenancy (no signed copy identified);
  • They did not sign the 1949 tenancy (no executed written agreement);
  • They did not sign the 1965 weekly tenancy;
  • They did not accept the 1974 licence.

This pattern of refusal is not consistent with a family that misunderstood its
legal position. It is consistent with a family that genuinely believed it owned
the farmhouse and garden and therefore had no need to enter into tenancy
agreements for property it already owned.

[SCRUTINY — THE FAMILY BELIEF SYSTEM] One consistent feature appears across
generations. The family acted as though they possessed ownership. Examples
include:
  • continuous occupation;
  • maintenance of the property;
  • resistance to tenancy offers;
  • rejection of landlord assertions;
  • continuing ownership claims.

Whether those beliefs were legally correct is a separate question. The point is
that they appear consistently held.

[SCRUTINY — TREE PLANTING EVIDENCE] The reconstruction records tree planting
between approximately 1895 and 1905. Tree planting is not proof of ownership.
However, historically it is a behaviour frequently associated with long-term
proprietary expectation. People expecting removal within a short tenancy
commonly plant annual crops. People expecting multi-generational occupation
commonly plant trees. This evidence is consistent with a belief in permanent
ownership.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
December 1965 — DEATH OF FREDERICK BUCKLER
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] Mr Frederick Buckler died of heart-related issues (coronary
thrombosis), only a few months after the failed 1965 tenancy negotiations.

[SCRUTINY] After the death of Mr Frederick Buckler, Mrs Williams remained in
possession of the farmhouse and garden, with various of her children living
there with her, including Mr Buckler junior. No payment was made.

[CHALLENGE] The interest in a licence, expressed by Mr Frederick Buckler before
his death in late 1965, died with him. Any later attempt to bind Mrs Williams
(or her estate) to a licence based on her deceased husband's earlier expressed
interest would be legally questionable.

PART V — THE BP ERA AND THE 1974 UNILATERAL LICENCE
================================================================================

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
December 1969 — WESTERN GROUND RENTS SELLS TO BP PENSION TRUST
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] Western Ground Rents sells to BP Pension Trust Ltd. BP
Pension Trust Ltd acquires the interest in Great House Farm, including the
farmhouse and garden.

[SCRUTINY] BP Pension Trust inherited the existing legal position established by
Western Ground Rents, continuing litigation that had originated before BP
acquired the land. The chain of landlord succession is:
  Bute Estate → Western Ground Rents → BP Pension Trust / BP Properties.

[CHALLENGE] Each transfer potentially required evidence of the successor's
title. The reconstruction does not identify any document in which the Williams
or Buckler family sold the freehold, executed a conveyance away, executed a
release of ownership, or expressly disclaimed ownership. That absence is not
proof that no such document exists, but it is relevant to the question of how
BP's predecessors acquired title free of the family's claimed interest.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1974 — BP PENSION TRUST FRESH ACTION AND THE UNILATERAL LICENCE
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] BP Pension Trust started a fresh action in Cardiff County
Court for possession and mesne profits against Mrs Williams and various members
of her family, including Mr Buckler junior.

[ESTABLISHED FACT] 3 July 1974: Court hearing. Mary Williams pleads claim of
ownership. This was part-heard and a formal defence was filed asserting that BP
Pension Trust's claim was barred by adverse possession under the Limitation Act
1939. So far as Mr Buckler junior was concerned, his plea was that he occupied
as licensee of Mrs Williams. That action has never been finally heard. The
court adjourned without ruling on title, focusing only on possession.

[SCRUTINY] The court adjourned without ruling on title. This is critical. At no
point during the 1960–1988 window did the developers seek a High Court ruling
to settle the land title dispute. By relying on "possession" (physical control)
rather than "title" (legal right), they avoided a discovery process that would
have revealed the Williams family's legal standing and the historical artifacts
tied to the land.

[ESTABLISHED FACT] 17 September 1974: BP Pension Trust Ltd gave notice of an
application for leave to enforce the possession order of 11 December 1962 (the
order of Judge Temple Morris).

[ESTABLISHED FACT] 19 September 1974: Judge Watkin Powell gave BP Pension Trust
Ltd leave to execute the order for possession from 11 December 1962. He ordered
that the possession warrant do issue forthwith, but be not enforced until
31 October 1974.

[ESTABLISHED FACT] 30 October 1974: Mrs Williams's solicitors issued a notice
of appeal against Judge Watkin Powell's order (on the ground that Mrs Williams
had insufficient notice of the application and therefore had had no opportunity
of presenting her case).

[ESTABLISHED FACT] 31 October 1974: A scheduled possession enforcement warrant
for this date is withdrawn. Withdrawal prevented immediate eviction, allowing
Mary to remain until her death in 1983.

[ESTABLISHED FACT] 4 November 1974: Letters received by Mrs Williams's
solicitor from BP Pension Trust Ltd and BP Properties Ltd, allegedly sent
31st October, were a unilateral offer of a lifetime licence to "Mrs Buckler" to
occupy the farm until her death.

[SCRUTINY — THE UNILATERAL LICENCE] This became the most important
tenancy-related event in later litigation. Important features include:
  • UNILATERAL — decided without consulting the other party;
  • PERSONAL — applied only to Mary Williams;
  • NOT OWNERSHIP — explicitly did not confer title;
  • TERMINATED ON DEATH — ended when Mary Williams died;
  • NON-HERITABLE — created no continuing estate capable of passing to heirs.

[SCRUTINY — WHY ISSUE A LICENCE AT ALL?] If BP believed they already possessed
absolute title to the farmhouse and garden, why issue a personal licence? Why
negotiate at all? Why not simply enforce the existing possession order? Does
the act of offering a licence suggest that BP recognised the occupation was not
straightforwardly terminable?

Possible explanations:
  1. Pragmatic humanitarian compromise;
  2. Recognition that occupation was legally more complicated than the tenancy
     narrative suggested;
  3. Avoidance of a full hearing on ownership that might have exposed gaps in
     BP's title chain.

[CHALLENGE] The surviving record cannot presently determine which explanation is
correct. However, the timing is significant: the licence was offered immediately
after the appeal application and immediately before the scheduled enforcement
date. It appears designed to interrupt the adverse possession clock and to avoid
a full judicial examination of title.

[SCRUTINY — THE NAMING CONVENTION ("MRS BUCKLER" VS "MRS WILLIAMS")]

Mary Doreen Williams (1913–1983) was the daughter of John Williams. She married
Frederick Buckler yet retained her maiden name in daily life and in dealings
with the estate. From 1962 onwards, the courts and estate consistently referred
to her as "Mrs Buckler." The 1974 licence was addressed to "Mrs Buckler."

[CHALLENGE] Did the consistent use of "Mrs Buckler" in legal documents
disconnect her from the Williams lineage that underpinned her asserted
grandfather rights? If the Separate Plot Hypothesis depends on a title derived
through the Williams line, did the naming convention obscure the lineage through
which that title was claimed?

The 1987 judgment explicitly notes that she "was also from time to time referred
to as Mrs Williams." The court's characterisation of this as an occasional
alternative inverts the reality: her title was Williams, and the courts' use of
"Mrs Buckler" was the occasional imposition.

[NEWSPAPER EVIDENCE] A 1978 newspaper article reports Mrs Williams publicly
claiming ownership and rejecting BP's asserted claim. This demonstrates that she
was not silent — she expressly rejected the offer. Evidence of this rejection
was later suppressed and/or excluded from proceeding events and cases.

[SCRUTINY — ACCEPTANCE BY SILENCE?] The licence was alleged to be accepted by
her silence, as no expressed rejection or acceptance occurred. However:
  • She had already pleaded claim of ownership in court (3 July 1974);
  • She had instructed solicitors who issued a notice of appeal (30 October 1974);
  • She had publicly rejected BP's claim in the press (1978);
  • She consistently maintained ownership across decades.

[CHALLENGE] The proposition that she accepted the licence by silence is
contradicted by overwhelming evidence of her express and repeated rejection of
BP's ownership claims. The licence was unilateral — decided without consulting
her — and she never voluntarily accepted it.

[SCRUTINY — THE TWO BP ENTITIES] Two separate entities — BP Pension Trust Ltd
and BP Properties Ltd — issued letters simultaneously or in sequence to
"corroborate" a narrative. The judiciary treated them as separate entities to
facilitate the 1987 ruling, but then treated them as the "same" to dismiss
William Buckler Jnr's appeal. This is a violation of the principle of Equity,
where the law should be applied consistently.

[CHALLENGE] In 1974, BP Properties likely did not yet have the paper title from
the Pension Trust. They issued "licences" for land they didn't technically own
on paper yet, while the Williams Family actually possessed it.

[SCRUTINY — MARY WILLIAMS AS WIDOW AND PENSIONER] By the time of later disputes,
Mary Williams was no longer dealing with the estate as part of a married couple.
She was a widow and a pensioner. On one side stood major landowning
institutions, solicitors and corporate successors. On the other stood a widowed
occupant asserting inherited rights. This does not establish wrongdoing, but it
does establish asymmetry.

The reconstruction further records her serious health difficulties, including
thrombosis and amputation. Such circumstances inevitably affect a person's
ability to contest complex property disputes.

[SCRUTINY — NO EXPRESS RENUNCIATION] The reconstruction identifies no document
in which John Williams, Mary Williams, or William Beverly Buckler expressly
renounce ownership claims. The absence of such a document does not prove
ownership, but it does demonstrate persistence of claim across generations.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1974 — COMMUNITY PETITION
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] A petition signed by 1,700 local residents circulated
for preservation of Great House Farm from demolition and housing development,
after BP Pension Trust Ltd obtained planning permission from the former
Glamorgan County Council.

[SCRUTINY] The community recognised the family's connection to the land and the
property's heritage value. This is not legal evidence of title, but it is
contextual evidence that the family was regarded as the established household
associated with the property — not as transient squatters.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1975 — THE BUCKLERS AS ESTABLISHED FARMERS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE] The 1975 papers (DCOWHS) record the Bucklers as
established members of the farming community.

[SCRUTINY] The family was not a group of squatters who had recently occupied
the land. They were established members of the farming community with a
"moral" expectation of ownership rooted in centuries of occupation.

PART VI — DEATH OF MARY WILLIAMS AND THE 1987 COURT OF APPEAL
================================================================================

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
14 March 1983 — DEATH OF MARY DOREEN WILLIAMS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] Mary Doreen Williams (born 10 August 1913) died on 14 March
1983 in South Glamorgan. She died at the farmhouse, having remained in
possession until her death, before the Court of Appeal proceedings and before
the demolition.

[CRITICAL SIGNIFICANCE] The "licence" was personal and terminated on death.
Mary Williams died four years before the 1987 Court of Appeal ruling. The
licence had therefore legally expired before the Court of Appeal ruled that it
prevented adverse possession.

[SCRUTINY] The 1987 Court of Appeal decision in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler
[1987] EWCA Civ 2 / [1987] 2 EGLR 168 held that the 1974 unilateral licence
ended adverse possession. But the licence holder was dead. The eviction of a
pregnant woman and three infants in 1988 was not an act of "permitting a
relative to stay" under a valid licence — it was a disproportionate use of
force against a new generation that had no legal agreement with BP.

[CHALLENGE] The most significant opportunity for justice lies in the gap between
the 1983 death of Mary Williams and the 1988 eviction. BP used a "Ghost
Licence" (a permit for a deceased person) as a legal shield to evict a living
family. This highlights the absurdity of the law at the time: it prioritised a
piece of mail sent to a dead grandmother over the human rights of her living
successors.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1982–1983 — FIRST LAND REGISTRATION
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] Land registration was introduced in Wales; the farm was
registered under BP's title. BP Pension Trust Ltd / BP Properties first
registered title at HM Land Registry in 1982 (title numbers WA231076 and
WA240304).

[ESTABLISHED FACT] On 23 February 1983, application WA240304 was submitted. Mary
Williams died on 14 March 1983, before the Court of Appeal proceedings.

[EVIDENCE GAP] The original registration bundle survives but remains
inaccessible following an FOI refusal. HM Land Registry has confirmed that
substantial first-registration documentation survives (letter 30 July 2026, ref
N610CGV) and has identified the documents held. Examination remains outstanding.

[CHALLENGE] The first registration occurred while Mary Williams was still alive
and in possession. If the Williams family held a separate freehold to the
farmhouse plot, the 1982–1983 registration process may have conflated that
freehold with the surrounding leasehold or reversionary interest. The 1983
merger of two titles at the Land Registry has been suggested as corroborating
evidence for the Separate Plot Hypothesis.

[SCRUTINY] The registration is an established fact. The legal validity of that
registration (whether it correctly reflected the underlying title position) was
never adjudicated. First registration under the Land Registration Act 1925 could
be based on any of several qualifying interests. The fact of registration does
not prove the nature of the interest registered.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1987 — COURT OF APPEAL DECISION
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[ESTABLISHED FACT] In BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 / [1987] 2
EGLR 168, the Court of Appeal held that the 1974 unilateral licence ended
adverse possession and foreclosed the Buckler ownership claim.

[CRITICAL LEGAL DISTINCTION] The Court of Appeal did NOT determine absolute
ownership. The litigation culminating in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler was
concerned with possession and adverse possession, not with a comprehensive trial
of the root of title stretching back centuries.

The Court of Appeal accepted the title put before it for the purposes of
deciding the issues in the appeal. It did not reconstruct the entire chain of
title from 1667 onwards. Consequently, the judgment should not be read as
positively determining every historical ownership issue.

[SCRUTINY — THE COURT'S LANGUAGE] The judgment states:

    "Mrs Mary Buckler, who was also from time to time referred to as Mrs
     Williams, had conceived the notion that she had a title to the
     ownership of Great House Farm through her own grandfather and that
     there were documents in existence which proved her title. No such
     documents were, however, ever produced."

[CHALLENGE] The court's characterization of Mrs Buckler's belief as a "notion"
is the court's language. It does not establish whether the underlying belief was
legally correct or incorrect. The absence of produced documents in court could
mean:
  • the documents never existed;
  • they had been lost;
  • they had been destroyed;
  • they had been removed (as alleged from Cardiff Library and the farmhouse
    loft).

The court was not asked to, and did not, determine whether the documents had
existed and been lost — only that they were not produced at trial.

[SCRUTINY — THE COURT RELIED HEAVILY UPON INFERENCE] The Court of Appeal
accepted several matters which appear to have been inferred from evidence rather
than proved by surviving executed documents:
  • existence of the 1949 tenancy;
  • surrender of the 1916 tenancy;
  • payment history;
  • continuation of tenancy.

That distinction between documentary proof and judicial inference belongs in the
record.

[SCRUTINY — THE ADVERSE POSSESSION TIMELINE DEPENDED ENTIRELY ON TENANCY]
The court's reasoning is essentially:
  tenancy → notice to quit → tenancy ends → adverse possession can begin →
  1962 interruption → 1974 licence.

If the tenancy chronology changes, the adverse possession chronology changes.
The court treated the Williams/Buckler occupation as arising from successive
landlord-tenant relationships rather than hereditary ownership. But that
treatment depended upon accepting the estate's tenancy narrative — a narrative
that rests on missing documents, unsigned agreements, and inference from conduct.

[SCRUTINY — THE ESTATE'S BURDEN WAS DIFFERENT] The estate was not required to
prove every historical conveyance from the seventeenth century. Instead, it
relied upon documentary title and subsequent tenancy arrangements which the
courts accepted as sufficient. That is procedurally different from proving that
no earlier proprietary rights ever existed.

PART VII — CROSS-CUTTING SCRUTINY AND CHALLENGES
================================================================================

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THE DOCUMENTARY GAP AFTER 1876
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[EVIDENCE GAP] The chronology presently contains no continuous chain of rent
records after 1876. This does not prove rent ceased. Equally, it does not prove
rent continued. If a landlord alleges a continuing tenancy across many decades,
surviving rent ledgers, rent books, annual accounts or demands would ordinarily
be valuable corroborative evidence. Their apparent absence in the present record
is an evidential gap, not proof of either position.

[EVIDENCE GAP] There is no identified continuous rent account covering the
family's occupation across the decades. The reconstruction has not seen evidence
of:
  • annual rental schedules;
  • tenant ledger;
  • running account;
  • balancing statements.

If such records survive, they would be among the strongest tenancy evidence
either way.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THE REPEATED ATTEMPTS TO REGULARISE OCCUPATION
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Looking across the chronology reveals a repeated pattern. After the tenancy
allegedly ended, there were repeated attempts to place occupation back onto an
express legal footing:
  • 1959 tenancy proposal (refused);
  • 1965 weekly tenancy (unsigned);
  • 1974 lifetime licence (unilateral, to dead name).

Viewed together these suggest repeated efforts to redefine the legal basis upon
which occupation continued. The documents themselves do not explain why those
repeated attempts became necessary. If BP's title was secure, a single
enforcement action would have sufficed. The need for repeated attempts suggests
that the legal relationship remained disputed or uncertain.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THE NAMING CONVENTION AS LEGAL MECHANISM
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[WORKING HYPOTHESIS] The consistent use of "Mrs Buckler" in legal documents may
have carried legal significance by disconnecting Mary Williams from the Williams
lineage through which her asserted grandfather rights derived. This was not
merely a clerical error; it was a tactical erasure of her identity as a
Williams, the holder of the 320-year-old possessory title.

Courts and estates typically use married names as a matter of convention. There
is no documentary evidence that the naming was chosen deliberately to obscure
lineage. However, the effect — whether intended or not — was to frame the
dispute as involving a Buckler family member with no hereditary connection to
the 1667 transaction, rather than a Williams descendant asserting ancestral
rights.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THE PROGRESSIVE NARROWING OF THE ESTATE'S CLAIM
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The estate's legal strategy evolved from claiming the whole farm to claiming
increasingly less:
  • 1916: yearly agricultural tenancy of the whole farm;
  • 1955: possession order for the "whole farm" (but only partially enforced);
  • 1962: possession of farmhouse and garden only;
  • 1965: weekly tenancy of farmhouse and garden only;
  • 1974: personal lifetime licence for Mary Williams only.

That progressive narrowing is historically significant. It suggests that the
estate itself recognised that its strongest claim lay in the agricultural land,
and that its claim to the farmhouse and garden was progressively weaker,
requiring more limited and personal legal instruments.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
POSSESSION IS NOT THE SAME AS TITLE
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[CRITICAL LEGAL PRINCIPLE] The proceedings principally concerned possession. A
possession order determines who is entitled to possession as between the parties
before the court. It does not necessarily amount to a comprehensive adjudication
of every possible historical title issue dating back centuries.

Whether broader ownership issues were pleaded and determined depends upon the
complete court record — which has not been located.

[CHALLENGE] At no point during the 1960–1988 window did the developers seek a
High Court ruling to settle the land title dispute. By relying on possession
rather than title, they avoided a discovery process that would have revealed the
Williams family's legal standing.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THE CENTRAL HISTORICAL TENSION
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The entire chronology can be reduced to one unresolved question:

Were the Williams and Buckler family:
  (a) tenants who mistakenly believed they were owners, OR
  (b) owners whose occupation was progressively recharacterised as tenancy by
      successive institutional landowners?

The uploaded material does not presently prove either proposition. What it does
show is that the family's assertion of ownership remained remarkably consistent
across generations, while the legal descriptions applied by external parties
changed repeatedly over time.

PART IX — CONCLUSION
================================================================================

The tenancy, lease and licence history of Great House Farm is not a simple
sequence of one uninterrupted lease. Instead, it is a series of changing legal
characterisations — yearly tenancy, alleged surrender, agricultural tenancy,
notice to quit, possession proceedings, proposed replacement tenancies, and
finally a personal lifetime licence — superimposed upon decades of uninterrupted
physical occupation by the Williams and Buckler family.

That distinction between continuous occupation and changing legal descriptions
is one of the defining features of the surviving record.

The estate's documents consistently describe the family as tenants under
successive agricultural arrangements. The family's evidence consistently
describes themselves as owners who rejected later attempts to redefine that
ownership as tenancy.

Several features remain unresolved:
  • no original 1667 deed;
  • uncertain legal effect of the 1876 arrangements;
  • missing Daniel Thomas agreement;
  • no identified signed 1916 tenancy presently produced;
  • no identified surviving rent book after the early twentieth century;
  • unidentified documentary basis for the 1953 rent payment;
  • partial enforcement in 1955;
  • second possession proceedings in 1962;
  • repeated tenancy offers apparently refused;
  • unsigned weekly tenancy in 1965;
  • unilateral lifetime licence in 1974 addressed to a name the recipient did
    not use;
  • licence holder dead four years before the Court of Appeal ruled on its
    effect.

Taken together, these events create a continuous documentary history in which
the estate repeatedly asserted a tenancy relationship while the Williams and
Buckler family increasingly disputed that characterisation. The surviving
evidence is sufficient to reconstruct that chronology but is not sufficient, on
its own, to resolve the underlying question of title.

The strongest objective criticism of the surviving record is not that it proves
one side or the other. Rather, it is that several important legal conclusions
depend upon documentary foundations that are not presently available for
independent scrutiny. Until those foundations are examined, questions concerning
the precise evolution of the legal relationship between the estate and the
Williams/Buckler family remain open to further historical investigation.

The Separate Plot Hypothesis — that the farmhouse, garden and curtilage became a
separate freehold after the 1876 severance — remains a Working Hypothesis. It is
consistent with a significant body of evidence, but it is contradicted by other
documented facts. It cannot be tested conclusively without access to the
original HM Land Registry first-registration bundle, examination of the 1971
Ordnance Survey records, and location of any surviving copy of the 1667
transaction deed or the Bute Estate → Daniel Thomas agreement.

At present, the evidence is insufficient to distinguish conclusively between
administrative error, the confusion of two legal interests, and deliberate
concealment. The question remains open and testable.
