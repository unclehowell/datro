APPLICATION FOR DECLARATORY RELIEF

Concerning the procedural consequences of the proceedings in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2

PART A – INTRODUCTION AND JURISDICTION

The Nature of the Claim

1. The Claimant, Sion Buckler, is a descendant of the Williams/Buckler family who occupied Great House Farm, Llandough, Vale of Glamorgan, for generations. He brings this claim in a representative capacity on behalf of the family estate, further particularised at paragraphs 66–68 below.

2. This claim seeks declaratory relief concerning the procedural consequences of the proceedings in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2. It does not seek to reopen, appeal, or set aside the judgment on its merits. Rather, it seeks declarations that the procedure adopted in those proceedings failed to determine the competing proprietary, equitable and historical rights affected by the possession order, with the result that those rights were effectively extinguished without ever having been adjudicated.

3. The Claimant contends that the 1987 proceedings were conducted as a conventional possession action concerned solely with immediate entitlement to possession. However, the dispute engaged longstanding occupation, alleged equitable interests, proprietary expectations, overriding interests arising from actual occupation, and other historical claims concerning Great House Farm. Those matters were neither pleaded nor determined before the irreversible consequences of the possession order occurred.

4. The relief sought is directed to the adequacy of the procedure employed, rather than the correctness of the Court of Appeal's reasoning on the issues placed before it. The Claimant accepts the Court of Appeal's determination of the adverse possession issue as binding between the parties. This claim concerns what the Court was not asked to determine and what the procedure did not permit it to determine.

Why Declaratory Relief Is Appropriate

5. Declaratory relief is sought for the following reasons:

    (a) The claim does not seek to reopen the Court of Appeal's decision on its merits. It seeks declarations regarding the procedural scope and legal consequences of those proceedings.

    (b) Those consequences continue to affect rights asserted by the Claimant. The possession order and subsequent demolition extinguished practical means of asserting those rights. A declaration as to the procedural scope of the 1987 proceedings is a necessary precondition to any determination whether those rights may be asserted by other means.

    (c) The declarations sought are not merely academic. They have practical consequences for the Claimant's ability to pursue compensation, inquiry, or other relief in respect of rights that were never adjudicated. The Claimant does not presently seek such relief in these proceedings, but the declaratory relief sought is directed to establishing whether the procedural framework permitted the extinguishment of rights without adjudication.

    (d) The claim raises a point of general importance concerning the adequacy of possession proceedings where irreversible consequences (deprivation of a family home and demolition of a historic building) flow from the order. The point is capable of affecting other occupiers in similar circumstances.

6. Jurisdiction is relied upon under:

    (a) section 19 of the Senior Courts Act 1981 (power of the High Court to grant declaratory relief);
    (b) CPR Part 8 (alternative procedure for claims); and
    (c) the inherent jurisdiction of the High Court to grant declaratory relief where appropriate.

7. The Claimant acknowledges that declaratory relief is discretionary. He submits that the Court should exercise its discretion in his favour for the reasons set out at paragraphs 5–6 above and because the claim raises issues of real and substantial concern that are not hypothetical or academic.

PART B – THE PROCEDURAL DISTINCTION

8. The Claimant distinguishes between:

    (a) the issue actually decided by the Court of Appeal in 1987 (the effect of the 1974 letters upon the running of limitation in adverse possession); and

    (b) the wider body of rights which were affected by the resulting possession order but were never themselves adjudicated.

9. The Claimant does not challenge the correctness of the Court of Appeal's determination of issue (a). He accepts that the Court applied the law of adverse possession correctly to the issue presented to it. His case is that the issue presented was procedurally incomplete and therefore incapable of finally determining all rights affected by the resulting order.

10. The Claimant further distinguishes between:

    (a) a collateral attack upon a final judgment (which seeks to impugn the correctness of the decision on the merits); and

    (b) a claim for declaratory relief concerning the procedural scope of the proceedings and the rights that were not determined (which accepts the correctness of the decision on the issues decided but identifies rights that were never placed before the Court).

11. The Claimant submits that the present claim falls within category (b). He relies upon the principle that a declaratory judgment may be granted concerning the existence or non-existence of a legal right even where a prior judgment has determined related issues, provided that the declaration does not conflict with the prior determination: see Messier-Dowty Ltd v Sabena SA [2000] 1 WLR 2040; Financial Services Authority v Rourke [2002] CP Rep 14.

12. The Claimant further submits that the doctrine of res judicata does not bar a claim that seeks to determine rights that were not in issue in the prior proceedings. Cause of action estoppel bars re-litigation of the same cause of action; issue estoppel bars re-litigation of the same issue. The present claim does not re-litigate the cause of action (possession) or the issue (the effect of the 1974 letters upon limitation). It seeks to determine different rights that were never in issue.

PART C – FACTUAL BACKGROUND AND CHRONOLOGY

The Property

13. The property the subject of these proceedings is Great House Farm, also known as Ty Mawr, situated at Llandough, Vale of Glamorgan ("the Property"). The Property comprised a farmhouse, outbuildings, and agricultural land.

14. The Claimant believes, based upon family tradition and the documentary records referred to at paragraph 15 below, that the Williams family have occupied the Property since at least 1667.

15. The Williams family name appears in Llandough parish records (Exhibit SB1) and land tax assessments (Exhibit SB2) from the 18th century onwards. The Claimant infers from this documentary evidence that the family's occupation was continuous from the late 17th century, but this is a matter of inference from an incomplete record requiring further investigation.

16. Archaeological evidence confirms that the site has been occupied continuously since at least the Roman period, with a monastic grange established by Tewkesbury Abbey by the 12th century (Exhibit SB3). The farmhouse itself was essentially 17th-century in construction, though it may have incorporated earlier medieval fabric (Exhibit SB4).

The 1871 Arrangement with Daniel Thomas

17. The Claimant believes, based upon family records (Exhibit SB5) and oral tradition, that in or about 1871 an arrangement was entered into between members of the Williams family and one Daniel Thomas concerning lime quarrying rights and land associated with the Property. The Claimant understands that under this arrangement, Daniel Thomas was permitted to quarry lime on the family's land on condition that, upon completion of the quarrying, the Williams family would acquire equitable title to the land so quarried.

18. The precise terms of the 1871 arrangement require investigation. The Claimant has not yet located the original agreement, if one exists in documentary form.

19. The Claimant contends that, if the 1871 arrangement is established, it gave rise to the following interests:

    (a) an equitable interest in the land subject to the quarrying arrangement, arising from the agreement that title would pass upon completion;

    (b) a constructive trust over the proceeds or benefit of the quarrying operations; and

    (c) proprietary estoppel, in that the Williams family permitted the quarrying in reliance upon the representation that they would acquire title, and acted to their detriment by permitting the extraction of minerals from their land.

20. The Claimant infers from the absence of any reference to the 1871 arrangement in the 1987 proceedings that the legal effect of that arrangement was never investigated within the possession proceedings. The arrangement was not pleaded, no evidence was adduced concerning it, and the Court made no determination as to whether it gave rise to proprietary or equitable interests binding upon subsequent owners.

The 1938 Sale and the Missing Deeds

21. The Claimant believes that in or about 1938 the Property was the subject of a sale (Exhibit SB6). The Claimant has been unable to locate the deeds of title and conveyance records for this transaction (Exhibit SB7). The absence of these deeds prevents a complete understanding of:

    (a) the precise nature of the interest conveyed in 1938;

    (b) whether the Williams family's interests were expressly or impliedly reserved;

    (c) whether the purchaser took subject to any existing equitable interests; and

    (d) the chain of title between 1938 and the registration of BP Properties Ltd in 1982.

The 1962 Possession Proceedings

22. The Claimant believes that in December 1962 possession proceedings were commenced against the Williams/Buckler family (Exhibit SB8). A possession order was obtained but was not enforced. The Claimant contends that these proceedings were deficient in that:

    (a) they did not determine any of the family's alleged equitable or proprietary interests;

    (b) the possession order was obtained without any inquiry into the family's historical claims or the 1871 arrangement; and

    (c) the non-enforcement of the order for approximately twelve years (until 1974) allowed the family to continue in occupation, during which time their adverse possession claim continued to mature.

The 1969 Transfer

23. The Claimant believes that in or about 1969 there was a transfer of the freehold interest in the Property (Exhibit SB9). The Claimant contends that this transfer:

    (a) was effected without notice to the Williams/Buckler family;

    (b) was not accompanied by any inquiry into the family's asserted interests; and

    (c) may have been registered without disclosure of the family's actual occupation or the 1871 arrangement.

The 1974 Licence

24. In October 1974, BP Pension Trust Limited and BP Properties Limited wrote to Mrs Williams (also referred to as Mrs Buckler) offering her a licence to occupy the farmhouse and garden rent-free for the remainder of her life (Exhibit SB10). Mrs Williams never expressly accepted or rejected the offer. She remained in occupation until her death in 1983.

25. The Claimant does not dispute that the Court of Appeal determined the legal effect of the 1974 letters upon the running of limitation. The Claimant's case is that the Court's determination was confined to that narrow issue and did not extend to:

    (a) whether the 1974 letters were capable of overriding or extinguishing the family's pre-existing equitable interests;

    (b) whether Mrs Williams had capacity to bind the family estate by her conduct (or inaction) in relation to the letters;

    (c) whether the letters operated as a licence in circumstances where the family disputed the offeror's title; and

    (d) whether the procedural framework of a possession action was adequate to determine these questions.

The 1982 Registration

26. BP Properties Limited was registered as proprietor of the Property in November 1982 (Exhibit SB11). The Claimant contends that:

    (a) the registration did not eliminate any equitable interests or overriding interests arising from actual occupation;

    (b) the Williams/Buckler family remained in actual occupation of the farmhouse and garden at the date of registration; and

    (c) section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 required that the family's interests, if subsisting, be protected as overriding interests.

The 1987 Proceedings

27. In BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2, the Court of Appeal held that the 1974 letters constituted a licence which terminated the family's adverse possession, with the consequence that time ceased to run in their favour and BP Properties Ltd was entitled to possession.

28. The Court confined its determination to the legal effect of the alleged licence and the law governing adverse possession. It did not determine:

    (a) whether the family's alleged equitable interests under the 1871 arrangement existed or were binding;

    (b) whether actual occupation created overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925;

    (c) whether proprietary rights arising from the 1871 arrangement required independent determination;

    (d) whether any trust, proprietary estoppel or constructive trust arose from the family's longstanding occupation;

    (e) whether customary or prescriptive rights existed; or

    (f) whether BP Properties Ltd's title was subject to any equitable obligations arising from the family's longstanding occupation.

The Demolition

29. Following the possession order, the historic farmhouse at Great House Farm was demolished (Exhibit SB12). The demolition rendered restoration of the previous position impossible and extinguished any practical possibility of the family asserting their historical rights in relation to the Property.

30. The Claimant contends that the demolition was a direct consequence of the possession proceedings and that the irreversible nature of that consequence distinguishes this case from an ordinary possession dispute. Where proceedings will permanently deprive an occupier of land, extinguish longstanding occupation and render any future remedy ineffective, the Court should be satisfied that every material competing interest has either been determined or appropriately reserved.

PART D – THE PROCEDURAL FAILURE ALLEGED

31. The Claimant alleges that the fundamental defect in the 1987 proceedings was procedural, not substantive. The Court of Appeal applied the law of adverse possession correctly to the issue presented to it. The defect lay in the procedural framework within which those issues were defined and the scope of the inquiry permitted.

32. The Defendant's claim was advanced as an ordinary possession action under the County Court procedure then applicable. By adopting that procedural characterisation, the Court confined its inquiry to the question of immediate entitlement to possession. The pleadings did not raise, and the Court was not invited to determine, the full extent of the competing legal and equitable rights asserted in respect of the Property.

33. The practical consequence was that a procedure designed to determine possession also brought about the irreversible extinction of alleged proprietary, equitable and historical interests without those interests themselves being determined. The possession order became the mechanism by which:

    (a) the family's physical occupation was terminated;

    (b) the historic farmhouse was demolished;

    (c) any proprietary, equitable or historical rights capable of being asserted in relation to the Property lost practical effect; and

    (d) the family's ability to seek independent adjudication of those rights was rendered futile.

34. The Claimant therefore contends that the proceedings determined the consequence of the dispute (deprivation of possession and demolition of the farmhouse) without first determining every material right affected by that consequence.

35. The present claim does not assert that the Court of Appeal misapplied the law of adverse possession to the issue presented. Rather, it asserts that the issue presented to the Court was procedurally incomplete and therefore incapable of finally determining all rights affected by the resulting possession order.

________________________________________

PART E – WHY THE PROCEDURE WAS INADEQUATE

36. Possession proceedings are primarily directed towards determining immediate entitlement to possession between the parties before the Court. Such proceedings are not ordinarily intended to constitute a comprehensive inquiry into every equitable, historical or proprietary interest which may exist in relation to the land unless those matters are properly pleaded and determined.

37. In the present case, the procedural framework failed in the following respects:

Pleadings

38. The Defendant's claim was pleaded as a straightforward possession action. The family's alleged equitable interests, overriding interests, and historical claims were not pleaded by either party. The Court was therefore not invited to determine those matters.

Evidential Omissions

39. No evidence was adduced concerning:

    (a) the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas;

    (b) the family's continuous occupation from the late 17th century;

    (c) the circumstances of the 1938 sale and the missing deeds;

    (d) the 1962 proceedings and the non-enforcement of the possession order;

    (e) the 1969 transfer and the chain of title; or

    (f) the family's actual occupation at the date of the 1982 registration.

Procedural Scope

40. The County Court procedure adopted did not permit the comprehensive determination of the family's competing rights. The Court was confined to the issues raised by the pleadings, which did not extend to the equitable and proprietary interests now asserted.

Consequences of the Order

41. Where proceedings will permanently deprive an occupier of land, extinguish longstanding occupation and render any future remedy ineffective, the Court should be satisfied that every material competing interest has either been determined or appropriately reserved. The Claimant alleges that this safeguard was absent in the present case.

42. Consequently, the possession order became the practical mechanism by which alleged competing rights ceased to have any effective value despite never having received independent judicial determination.

PART F – THE RIGHTS NOT DETERMINED

43. The Court of Appeal determined the legal consequences of the 1974 letters and their effect upon adverse possession. It did not determine the following matters, each of which the Claimant contends required adjudication before possession could lawfully be granted:

Equitable Interests under the 1871 Arrangement

44. The Court did not determine whether the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas gave rise to:

    (a) an equitable interest in the land subject to quarrying;

    (b) a constructive trust over the proceeds of quarrying; or

    (c) proprietary estoppel based upon detrimental reliance.

Overriding Interests under Section 70(1)(g) LRA 1925

45. The Court did not determine whether the family's actual occupation of the farmhouse and garden at the date of registration in 1982 created overriding interests binding upon the registered proprietor under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925.

Trust, Proprietary Estoppel and Constructive Trust

46. The Court did not determine whether the family's longstanding occupation, expenditure upon the Property, and reliance upon their asserted rights gave rise to:

    (a) a resulting trust;

    (b) a constructive trust;

    (c) proprietary estoppel; or

    (d) any other equitable interest recognised by the general law.

Customary and Prescriptive Rights

47. The Court did not determine whether the family's longstanding occupation and use of the Property gave rise to customary rights or rights by prescription, including rights of way, grazing rights, or other incidents of agricultural tenure.

The Procedural Vehicle

48. Nor did the Court determine whether possession proceedings constituted the appropriate procedural vehicle through which such issues could lawfully be extinguished, or whether the family's rights required determination in separate proceedings.

PART G – EQUITY AND ACTUAL OCCUPATION

49. The Claimant relies upon the longstanding distinction recognised by English law between strict legal title and equitable interests affecting land.

50. Registration under the Land Registration Act 1925 did not eliminate equitable rights or overriding interests arising from actual occupation. Section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 expressly recognised that persons in actual occupation could possess interests binding upon a registered proprietor notwithstanding registration.

51. The Williams/Buckler family remained in actual occupation of the farmhouse and garden at all material times, including at the date of registration in 1982. Their continuous occupation was capable of giving rise to overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) and required judicial examination beyond the limited question of adverse possession.

52. Whether those interests ultimately succeeded is distinct from whether they required independent procedural determination before possession was ordered.

Long Possession

53. The Williams/Buckler family occupied Great House Farm continuously from at least the late 17th century, and the Claimant believes from as early as 1667. The Claimant contends that such occupation was capable of giving rise to legal and equitable rights recognised by the common law and the law relating to adverse possession.

The 1871 Arrangement

54. The Claimant relies upon the arrangement believed to have been entered into with Daniel Thomas in or about 1871 concerning lime and land rights associated with the Property, as pleaded at paragraphs 17–20 above. The Claimant believes that the legal effect of that arrangement was never investigated within the possession proceedings and remains capable of giving rise to proprietary or equitable consequences requiring judicial determination.

Equitable Interests

55. The Claimant further alleges that the family's occupation was capable of giving rise to equitable interests arising through trust, proprietary estoppel, reliance, acquiescence or other recognised equitable doctrines. Whether such interests ultimately existed was never determined because those questions were outside the scope of the possession proceedings.

Actual Occupation

56. Throughout the material period the family remained in actual occupation of the Property. The Claimant contends that this occupation required consideration under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 before any final order affecting the land was made.

Customary and Prescriptive Rights

57. The Claimant also contends that longstanding occupation and use of the Property were capable of giving rise to customary or prescriptive rights which likewise remained undetermined.

58. The consequences of the possession proceedings extended far beyond the determination of immediate possession. The proceedings ultimately resulted in the permanent removal of the Williams/Buckler family from Great House Farm.

59. Following possession, the historic farmhouse was demolished, rendering restoration of the previous position impossible. Any proprietary, equitable or historical rights capable of being asserted in relation to the Property thereby lost practical effect without independent adjudication.

PART K – GROUNDS FOR DECLARATORY RELIEF

Ground 1

63. The procedure adopted in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 did not determine every competing proprietary, equitable or historical interest affected by the possession order.

Ground 2

64. The Court was not invited to determine whether possession proceedings were procedurally sufficient to extinguish the family's asserted interests, or whether those interests required independent adjudication.

Ground 3

65. The proceedings focused upon adverse possession and the legal effect of the 1974 letters rather than the wider body of competing rights asserted by the family.

Ground 4

66. The irreversible consequences of the proceedings (deprivation of the family home and demolition of the farmhouse) required a level of procedural scrutiny proportionate to the rights affected.

Ground 5

67. The possession order produced consequences extending beyond the issues actually determined within the proceedings, including the practical extinguishment of rights that were never adjudicated.

PART N – LIMITATION AND DELAY

74. The Claimant acknowledges that the 1987 judgment is now nearly four decades old. He submits that the claim is not statute-barred for the following reasons:

    (a) The claim is for declaratory relief, not for the enforcement of a cause of action subject to a fixed limitation period. The Senior Courts Act 1981 and the inherent jurisdiction of the High Court permit declaratory relief to be granted without regard to limitation, provided that the claim is not barred by laches or acquiescence.

    (b) The Claimant's cause of action (if any) accrued not in 1987 but upon his discovery of the procedural defect and its consequences. The Claimant discovered the full extent of the procedural inadequacy and its effect upon the family's rights only upon undertaking the research and investigation that forms the basis of this claim. The date of discovery is a matter for evidence.

    (c) The Claimant submits that the doctrine of laches does not bar this claim. Laches requires proof of delay, acquiescence, and prejudice. While delay is admitted, the Claimant denies acquiescence. The family has consistently asserted its rights, both before and after the 1987 proceedings. Prejudice to the Defendant is a matter for the Court's discretion and does not automatically bar a claim for declaratory relief.

    (d) The Claimant further submits that the prejudice relied upon by the Defendant (death of witnesses, loss of documents, demolition of the farmhouse) is a consequence of the Defendant's own actions and the passage of time, not of the Claimant's delay. The Defendant obtained possession, demolished the farmhouse, and dealt with the Property in the knowledge that the family's rights had never been adjudicated.

75. The Claimant will provide a full and frank explanation for the delay in a witness statement. The explanation will address: (a) the family's circumstances following the 1987 proceedings; (b) the lack of legal representation and resources available to the family; (c) the Claimant's own discovery of the procedural issues; and (d) the steps taken by the Claimant to investigate and assert the family's rights.

PART O – RESPONSE TO ANTICIPATED OBJECTIONS

Res Judicata and Issue Estoppel

76. The Claimant repeats the distinction pleaded at paragraphs 8–12 above. He submits that res judicata and issue estoppel do not bar a claim that seeks to determine rights that were not in issue in the prior proceedings. The present claim does not re-litigate the cause of action (possession) or the issue (the effect of the 1974 letters upon limitation). It seeks to determine different rights that were never in issue.

77. The Claimant relies upon the principle that a declaratory judgment may be granted concerning the existence or non-existence of a legal right even where a prior judgment has determined related issues, provided that the declaration does not conflict with the prior determination: see Messier-Dowty Ltd v Sabena SA [2000] 1 WLR 2040; Financial Services Authority v Rourke [2002] CP Rep 14.

78. The Claimant further relies upon the distinction drawn in Virgin Atlantic Airways Ltd v Zodiac Seats UK Ltd [2013] UKSC 46 between cause of action estoppel (which bars re-litigation of the same cause of action) and the rule against collateral attack (which prevents a party from challenging a final judgment by indirect means). The present claim is not a collateral attack because it does not seek to impugn the correctness of the 1987 judgment. It seeks to establish the procedural scope of the proceedings and the rights that were not determined.

No Reasonable Grounds

79. The Claimant submits that the Particulars of Claim disclose reasonable grounds for bringing the claim. The claim is founded upon:

    (a) the procedural framework of the 1987 proceedings, which the Claimant contends was inadequate to determine the full range of rights affected by the possession order;

    (b) the irreversible consequences of the possession order, which the Claimant contends required a higher level of procedural scrutiny;

    (c) the established principles of equity and land registration law, which recognise that actual occupation may give rise to overriding interests and that equitable interests may subsist notwithstanding registration; and

    (d) the documentary and archaeological evidence referred to in the Schedule of Exhibits, which supports the family's longstanding occupation and the existence of the 1871 arrangement.

80. The Claimant acknowledges that certain factual allegations are based upon inference, belief, and incomplete evidence. He submits that this does not render the claim devoid of reasonable grounds. The Claimant has identified the evidence upon which he relies and has indicated where further investigation is required. The Court's function at the pleading stage is not to determine the ultimate merits of the claim but to assess whether the claim discloses a properly arguable case.

Academic or Hypothetical Nature

81. The Claimant submits that the claim is not academic or hypothetical. The declarations sought have practical consequences for:

    (a) the Claimant's ability to pursue compensation or inquiry in respect of rights that were never adjudicated;

    (b) the family's ability to assert their historical and equitable interests in any future proceedings;

    (c) the determination of whether the procedural framework of possession proceedings is adequate to extinguish rights without adjudication; and

    (d) the vindication of the family's rights and the correction of a procedural injustice.

82. The Claimant acknowledges that he does not presently seek damages, compensation, or reinstatement. He submits that the absence of such relief does not render the claim academic. Declaratory relief is frequently granted in circumstances where no monetary relief is sought, provided that the declaration has practical utility: see Dyson v Attorney General [1911] 1 KB 410.

Floodgates

83. The Claimant submits that the floodgates argument is overstated. The present claim is directed to a specific set of circumstances: a possession order that produced irreversible consequences (deprivation of a family home and demolition of a historic building) in respect of rights that were never adjudicated. The Claimant does not contend that every possession order is vulnerable to challenge. He contends only that where proceedings produce irreversible deprivation of land, the procedure adopted must be adequate to determine every material right affected by the resulting order.
