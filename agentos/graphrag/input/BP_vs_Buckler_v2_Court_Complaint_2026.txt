PART A: INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF CLAIM

1.  The Claimant, [full name], brings this application for declaratory relief concerning the procedural consequences of the proceedings in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2, [1987] 2 EGLR 168, on the grounds that the procedure adopted in those proceedings failed to determine the competing proprietary, equitable, and historical rights that were extinguished by the possession order, and thereby denied the Williams/Buckler family effective access to a determination of those rights.

2.  This is not an application to re-open the 1987 decision on its merits. It is an application for recognition that the procedure used in 1987 — a possession procedure designed to determine immediate entitlement — did not lawfully determine the rights that were in fact extinguished by its outcome, and that the consequences — the permanent loss of a family home, the destruction of an historic building, and the extinguishment of generations of connection with the land — were the direct and foreseeable result of that procedural failure.

3.  The Claimant asserts that the 1987 proceedings were, in substance, a dispute over competing land rights — involving alleged historic occupation, proprietary expectations, equitable interests, and competing claims arising from long possession — yet were treated procedurally as a simple possession claim between parties with no competing claim. This procedural mischaracterisation determined the scope of inquiry, the safeguards available, and the ultimate outcome.

4.  The Claimant further asserts that the court had information before it indicating a substantial dispute as to proprietary rights, yet proceeded without determining whether possession proceedings were the appropriate vehicle for the determination of those rights.

_______________________________________________________________

PART B: THE FACTUAL BACKGROUND

5.  The property in question is Great House Farm (Ty Mawr), Llandough, in the Vale of Glamorgan, Wales (hereinafter "the Property"). The Williams/Buckler family had been in occupation of the Property for generations, with roots extending back to the nineteenth century and beyond.

6.  The family's connection to the land was not merely one of recent tenancy or casual occupation. It was a connection founded upon:
    (a) continuous generational occupation from at least the late 19th century;
    (b) a historic arrangement with Daniel Thomas in 1871 concerning lime and land rights, which the family asserts gave rise to proprietary and equitable interests;
    (c) long-standing possession as a source of title under the Limitation Act 1980;
    (d) the family's status as occupying beneficiaries with equitable interests in the Property;
    (e) the family's actual occupation of the Property at all material times.

7.  In or about 1974, BP Properties Ltd (the Defendant) acquired the freehold title to the Property. The family remained in occupation. The Defendant subsequently brought possession proceedings.

8.  The 1987 proceedings were presented to the court as a straightforward claim for possession of land. The remedy sought was possession. The practical and legal consequence, however, was the removal of a family from its longstanding home and the extinguishment of a historic connection with the land.

9.  The Court of Appeal, in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] 2 EGLR 168, determined the matter on the basis of adverse possession and the effect of a purported licence granted in 1974. The court held that from the moment the mother received the offer to remain on the land, she occupied it under a licence and her possession was no longer adverse. It did not matter that she never accepted the offer.

10. The Court of Appeal did not, however, examine whether the procedure adopted was adequate to the determination of the rights that were extinguished by its outcome, or whether the court had information before it indicating a substantial dispute as to proprietary rights that required a different procedural approach.

_______________________________________________________________

PART C: THE PROCEDURAL FAILURE ALLEGED

11. The Claimant alleges that the court in 1987 committed a fundamental procedural failure: it permitted the dispute to proceed under a procedural framework defined by the Defendant's requested remedy (possession) rather than by the full nature of the rights affected.

12. The court thereby considered the question:
    "Who has the immediate right to possession?"
    when it should first have considered:
    "What competing rights exist in the land, and can those rights lawfully be extinguished through a possession procedure?"

13. The procedural classification of the case determined the scope of inquiry and the protections available. A party should not obtain the practical equivalent of extinguishing longstanding land rights merely by selecting possession as the form of remedy sought.

14. The Claimant does not allege that the court reached the wrong conclusion on the facts as presented. The Claimant alleges that the court asked the wrong question, with the wrong procedural safeguards, and thereby reached a conclusion that did not reflect the true nature of the dispute and did not lawfully determine the rights that were extinguished.

_______________________________________________________________

PART D: THE PROCEDURE USED DID NOT LAWFULLY DETERMINE THE RIGHTS EXTINGUISHED

15. The procedure adopted in 1987 was a possession procedure designed to determine immediate entitlement to possession. It was not a procedure designed to determine:
    (a) competing proprietary claims;
    (b) equitable interests;
    (c) overriding interests under the Land Registration Act 1925;
    (d) rights arising from long possession;
    (e) customary or prescriptive rights.

16. Yet the outcome of the procedure — the possession order — had the practical effect of extinguishing all of these rights. The procedure therefore determined the consequence (loss of the home and land) without determining the rights that were affected by that consequence.

17. This is the core of the Claimant's case: the procedure used did not lawfully determine the rights that were extinguished. It determined immediate entitlement to possession, but it did not determine whether the family's competing proprietary, equitable, and historical claims could lawfully be extinguished through that procedure.

18. The court had information before it indicating a substantial dispute as to proprietary rights. The family's long occupation, their assertion of rights arising from the 1871 arrangement, and their actual occupation of the Property were matters that indicated a dispute going far beyond the immediate right to possession.

19. The court should therefore have considered whether the procedure adopted was adequate to the determination of those rights, or whether the case required transfer, joinder of issues, or directions appropriate to a title and equity dispute.

_______________________________________________________________

PART E: THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN LEGAL TITLE AND EQUITABLE RIGHTS

20. The courts of England and Wales historically recognised that strict legal title did not always represent the complete extent of rights and obligations concerning land.

21. The principles of equity required consideration of matters including:
    (a) unconscionable conduct;
    (b) reliance;
    (c) equitable interests;
    (d) beneficial ownership;
    (e) circumstances surrounding acquisition of title.

22. Where a party sought possession but the opposing party asserted a substantial competing interest in the land, the court was required to determine the true nature of that interest rather than merely accept the claimant's chosen procedural description.

23. The Williams/Buckler family's claim was not founded upon recent occupation but upon alleged generations of continuous occupation and connection with the land. Such a claim engaged historical legal concepts including:
    (a) possession as a source of title;
    (b) adverse possession;
    (c) prescriptive rights;
    (d) customary interests;
    (e) the protection of longstanding occupation.

24. The court should therefore have first determined whether the procedure adopted was adequate to the determination of these competing rights, or whether a different procedural approach was required.

_______________________________________________________________

PART F: REGISTRATION OF TITLE WAS NOT THE END OF THE INQUIRY

25. The proceedings relied upon BP's registered title following registration under the Land Registration Act 1925.

26. However, registration under the Land Registration Act 1925 was not intended to eliminate the court's duty to examine overriding interests, equitable interests, or competing rights where properly raised.

27. Section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 expressly protected "the rights of every person in actual occupation of the land or in receipt of the rents and profits thereof, save where enquiry is made of such person and the rights are not disclosed."

28. This provision was specifically designed to protect occupying beneficiaries and others with equitable interests in registered land. As Russell L.J. observed in Hodgson v Marks [1971] Ch 892, CA: "It must depend on the circumstances, and a wise purchaser or lender will take no risks. Indeed, however wise he may be he may have no ready opportunity of finding out; but, nevertheless, the law will protect the occupier."

29. A registered proprietor's claim to possession remained subject to the law governing the creation, protection, and enforcement of competing interests in land.

30. The court should have examined whether the family's actual occupation gave rise to overriding interests or equitable rights that could not be extinguished by a simple possession order. This examination required a procedure directed to the determination of those rights, not merely a procedure directed to the immediate right to possession.

_______________________________________________________________

PART G: THE CONSEQUENCES REQUIRED ENHANCED SCRUTINY

31. The court was aware, or should have been aware, that the result would affect:
    (a) a family home;
    (b) children residing at the property;
    (c) an historic building (Great House Farm, Ty Mawr);
    (d) archaeological and heritage interests;
    (e) a location with evidence of ancient occupation.

32. The destruction of the property meant that any later remedy would be incapable of fully restoring the previous position.

33. A procedure resulting in irreversible consequences required careful examination before final orders were made.

34. The court failed to apply the enhanced scrutiny that the nature of the case demanded.

_______________________________________________________________

PART H: THE ALLEGED PROCEDURAL FAILURE IN DETAIL

35. The alleged error was not simply that the court reached an unfavourable conclusion on the facts or the law as presented.

36. The alleged error was that the court permitted the dispute to proceed under a procedural framework defined by the Defendant's requested remedy rather than by the full nature of the rights affected.

37. By characterising the case as a simple possession claim, the court:
    (a) limited the scope of inquiry to the immediate right to possession;
    (b) excluded from consideration the family's competing proprietary and equitable claims;
    (c) failed to apply the procedural safeguards appropriate to a case involving the deprivation of a home and longstanding family occupation;
    (d) permitted the Defendant to obtain the practical equivalent of a declaration of superior title without having to prove that superior title in a procedure suited to that determination.

38. The procedural classification of the case therefore determined the scope of inquiry and the protections available.

PART I: CONSTITUTIONAL BACKGROUND

39. The constitutional principle expressed in Magna Carta 1215, Clause 39 — that no free person shall be deprived of land except by lawful judgment or law of the land — reflects a longstanding principle against arbitrary dispossession.

40. While Clause 39 is not itself a direct procedural rule governing the 1987 proceedings, its underlying principle remains relevant as a constitutional background: deprivation of land requires proper lawful determination.

41. The issue was therefore not merely possession, but whether the process used was proportionate and appropriate to the seriousness of the rights affected.

_______________________________________________________________

PART J: THE FAMILY'S COMPETING RIGHTS

42. The Claimant particularises the family's competing rights as follows:

    (1) RIGHTS ARISING FROM LONG POSSESSION
    The family had been in occupation of the Property for generations, from at least the late 19th century. This long possession gave rise to rights under the Limitation Act 1980, including the potential for title by adverse possession.

    (2) RIGHTS ARISING FROM THE 1871 ARRANGEMENT
    The family asserts that an arrangement with Daniel Thomas in 1871 concerning lime and land rights gave rise to proprietary and equitable interests in the Property. This arrangement was not properly examined in the 1987 proceedings.

    (3) EQUITABLE INTERESTS AS OCCUPYING BENEFICIARIES
    The family occupied the Property as beneficiaries of a trust or similar arrangement, giving rise to equitable interests that could not be extinguished by a simple possession order.

    (4) OVERRIDING INTERESTS UNDER SECTION 70(1)(g) LRA 1925
    The family's actual occupation of the Property gave rise to overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925, which were not properly examined.

    (5) CUSTOMARY AND PRESCRIPTIVE RIGHTS
    The family's long use of the Property gave rise to customary and prescriptive rights that required separate determination.

PART K: RELIEF SOUGHT

43. The Claimant respectfully requests that the Court:

    (1) DECLARE that the proceedings in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 failed to determine the competing proprietary, equitable, and historical rights that were extinguished by the possession order;

    (2) DECLARE that the procedure adopted did not lawfully determine the rights that were extinguished;

    (3) DECLARE that the principles of due process, natural justice, and lawful protection of property require that procedure must reflect substance, and must be adequate to the determination of the rights affected;

    (4) DECLARE that future cases involving the loss of homes and longstanding family occupation should require examination of underlying proprietary and equitable rights before irreversible remedies are granted;

    (5) MAKE SUCH OTHER ORDER as the Court thinks fit, including:
        (a) an order for inquiry into the circumstances of the 1987 proceedings;
        (b) an order for compensation;
        (c) an order for restitution;
        (d) such other relief as may be appropriate in all the circumstances.

PART L: GROUNDS FOR RELIEF

44. The Claimant relies upon the following grounds:

    GROUND 1: FAILURE TO PROPERLY DETERMINE COMPETING PROPRIETARY/EQUITABLE INTERESTS
    The court failed to determine the family's competing proprietary and equitable interests before granting a possession order. The procedure adopted was not adequate to that determination.

    GROUND 2: ACTUAL OCCUPATION / OVERRIDING INTERESTS UNDER LRA 1925
    The court failed to examine whether the family's actual occupation gave rise to overriding interests under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925.

    GROUND 3: PROCEDURAL FAIRNESS AND IRREVERSIBLE CONSEQUENCES
    The court failed to apply the procedural safeguards appropriate to a case involving the deprivation of a home and the irreversible destruction of an historic building.

    GROUND 4: TRANSFER/DIRECTIONS SHOULD HAVE BEEN CONSIDERED
    The court had information before it indicating a substantial dispute as to proprietary rights, yet proceeded without determining whether possession proceedings were the appropriate vehicle, or whether transfer, joinder of issues, or directions appropriate to a title and equity dispute were required.

    GROUND 5: BREACH OF PRINCIPLES OF NATURAL JUSTICE
    The procedural framework adopted did not permit the full and fair determination of the real issues between the parties.

    GROUND 6: BREACH OF DUE PROCESS
    The family's rights were determined by a procedure that did not reflect the substance of the dispute and was not adequate to the determination of the rights affected.

    GROUND 7: FAILURE TO CONSIDER EQUITABLE PRINCIPLES
    The court failed to consider the principles of equity, including unconscionable conduct, reliance, and beneficial ownership.

    GROUND 8: FAILURE TO CONSIDER THE 1871 ARRANGEMENT
    The court failed to examine the family's rights arising from the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas.

    GROUND 9: FAILURE TO CONSIDER CUSTOMARY AND PRESCRIPTIVE RIGHTS
    The court failed to examine the family's customary and prescriptive rights arising from long use of the Property.

PART M: PARTICULARS OF THE PROCEDURAL FAILURE

45. The Claimant particularises the procedural failure as follows:

    (1) The Defendant presented the claim as a simple possession claim, when in fact the claim involved the extinguishment of longstanding family rights in land.

    (2) The court accepted the Defendant's procedural characterisation without independent examination of whether that characterisation reflected the true nature of the dispute.

    (3) The court had information before it indicating a substantial dispute as to proprietary rights, including the family's long occupation, their assertion of rights arising from the 1871 arrangement, and their actual occupation of the Property.

    (4) The court failed to consider whether this information required transfer, joinder of issues, or directions appropriate to a title and equity dispute.

    (5) The court failed to consider whether the family's claim to longstanding occupation, equitable interests, and proprietary expectations required a different procedural approach.

    (6) The court failed to consider whether the family's rights under section 70(1)(g) of the Land Registration Act 1925 (rights of persons in actual occupation) required separate determination.

    (7) The court failed to consider whether the consequences of the possession order — the permanent loss of a family home and the destruction of an historic building — required enhanced procedural safeguards.

    (8) The court permitted the Defendant to obtain the practical equivalent of a declaration of superior title without having to prove that superior title in a procedure suited to that determination.

    (9) The court failed to examine the 1871 arrangement with Daniel Thomas and its effect on the family's rights.

    (10) The court failed to examine whether the family's long possession gave rise to adverse possession or other proprietary rights.

    (11) The court failed to examine whether the family's occupation gave rise to equitable interests as occupying beneficiaries.

    (12) The court failed to examine whether the family's use of the Property gave rise to customary or prescriptive rights.
