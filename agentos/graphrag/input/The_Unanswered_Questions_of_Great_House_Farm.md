# PREAMBLE
## The Living Heart of Llandough

For more than three hundred years, Great House Farm—*Ty Mawr* to all who loved her—stood as a living testament to family, faith, and the enduring bond between a people and their land. Nestled in the shadow of the ancient parish church of St Dochdwy in Llandough, near Penarth, the farmhouse and its barns were wrought from the local lias limestone that had sheltered this corner of South Glamorgan since time out of mind. Within her walls, the Williams Buckler family welcomed generations of children into the world, laid their ancestors to rest with Christian dignity, and opened their doors to the wider community as naturally as drawing breath.

The family's recorded habitation reaches back to at least 1667, though the roots likely run deeper still, tangled in the same sacred soil that yielded the early Christian enclosure of St Dochdwy. For centuries, Ty Mawr was more than a residence; it was a spiritual and social anchor. The family's Christian faith was not merely professed but practised in the daily rhythm of life beside the church, within whose grounds their forebears had long worshipped. The house was open to the public from time to time, a place where neighbours, historians, and the simply curious could walk through rooms that held the warm patina of four centuries of continuous care. It was, in the truest sense, a home without locks against friends.

The farm's reputation extended far beyond the parish boundaries. Guglielmo Marconi, the father of wireless radio, is remembered as having stayed beneath this roof during his pioneering work in South Wales, adding a chapter of national scientific heritage to the family's story. By 1974, the architectural significance of the house was formally recognised when H.J. Thomas of the Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales surveyed the ground floor and committed its plan to the permanent national record under catalogue reference ST 16817331. The Glamorgan-Gwent Archaeological Trust noted that the property had been referred to locally as a "county treasure." A public petition for its preservation gathered 1,700 signatures from across the community. The interior spoke of a seventeenth-century origin, with vernacular details suggesting a possible medieval core; the plan was of the sub-medieval direct-entry type, a rare survival that spoke of ancient Welsh building traditions and the craftsmanship of forgotten hands.

This was a house where history was not imprisoned behind glass but lived. Children grew up knowing the same corners their great-great-grandparents had known. A carved dressed-stone capital stood by the front door, weathered by centuries of Welsh rain and witness to countless comings and goings. The family did not merely occupy the land; they tended it, defended it, and stewarded it as a sacred trust through wars, recessions, and the slow erosion of time. Ty Mawr was a multi-generational family home—a place of peace, prayer, and open hospitality, where the kettle was always warm and the welcome was genuine.

To understand what was lost, one must first understand what existed. The house was not a ruin. It was not a dilapidated utility building. It was a home, a museum without walls, a sanctuary of memory, and a repository of the intangible heritage of the Welsh family who had kept her alive.

What follows is the story of how such a place—so peaceful, so Christian, so rooted in family and community—was transformed into a battlefield.

# INTRODUCTION

This book seeks to recover the truth of Great House Farm, not by beginning with the rubble, but by beginning with the living house. It asks the reader to hold in mind the image of Ty Mawr as she was: a white farmhouse glowing in the green embrace of Llandough, smoke curling from chimneys that had warmed three centuries of Bucklers, the sound of children in the yard and the church bells beyond the garden wall.

Only then can one properly measure the distance between what stood in December 1988 and what remained by breakfast time on the morning of the 6th. Only then can one understand why the central question of this history is not merely legal, but moral: *What did the principal authorities know, when did they know it, and what documentary record remains to explain why a county treasure was allowed to disappear overnight?*

The chapters that follow trace the path from sanctuary to siege. They recover the legal and historical record that led from the family's quiet possession to the clang of poleaxes and the roar of bulldozers. They examine the registration of title, the construction of the 1987 judgment, and the institutional decisions that left a pregnant woman and three small children standing in the winter dark while their home was erased from the earth.

But first, the chronicle of what was done.

# CHAPTER 1
## From Sanctuary to Battlefield

*A Chronology of Atrocity*

### The Early Twentieth Century: The First Assault on Title

Long before the bulldozers came, the first blows were struck with ink and paper. In the years between 1895 and 1905, transactions involving Daniel Thomas & Son sought to recast the Williams Buckler family from hereditary owners of their homestead into mere tenants. The family, who had occupied Ty Mawr since at least 1667 and who had never accepted the legitimacy of any superior claim to the plot they occupied, refused to pay rent to those who challenged their title. They understood themselves to be the outright owners—at minimum of the farmhouse and garden plot, and potentially of the wider agricultural lands that surrounded their home. The conveyance papers from this period, which might have clarified the true state of title, have since vanished from public collections. What remains is the family's unbroken physical presence and their steadfast refusal to surrender their birthright.

### 1955 & 1962: Possession Proceedings

The legal machinery ground forward. In 1955 and again in 1962, possession proceedings were brought against the family. The farmhouse and its immediate garden—the ancient core of the family's existence—were treated as merely part of a wider agricultural holding, denying the possibility that the residential plot had always been legally distinct from the surrounding farmland. The family remained. The house remained. But the shadow of legal threat had fallen across Ty Mawr.

### 1974: The Unilateral "Licence"

On 31 October 1974, an eviction warrant against Mary Williams was withdrawn. On 4 November 1974, BP Properties Ltd issued a unilateral letter offering Mary a "personal lifetime licence" to remain in the home she had always believed was hers. Mary Williams refused to sign. She refused to pay rent. She explicitly rejected BP's ownership claims and actively asserted her independent title. The letter was a trap dressed in courtesy: by offering what she already believed she owned, the company sought to transform centuries of rightful occupation into a revocable permission. Mary would not be folded into tenancy. Meanwhile, the community recognised what the law would not: a public petition for the preservation of Great House Farm gathered 1,700 signatures.

### 1982–1983: Registration and Death

In 1982 and 1983, while Mary Williams was in active physical occupation and asserting independent rights, BP Properties Ltd succeeded in registering absolute title under Title Numbers WA231076 and WA240304. The family's presence—their furniture, their fires, their lives—did not prevent the registration of the land in another's name. In 1983, Mary Williams died. The timing haunts the record.

### 1987: The Court of Appeal

In *BP Properties Ltd v Buckler* [1987] EWCA Civ 2, the Court of Appeal upheld the paper-title owner's claim to possession. Yet the judgment never ruled on the family's underlying title. The court never found that the Buckler family's interest had been extinguished. It simply looked away from the question of who truly owned the land, preferring the neatness of registered paper to the mess of historical justice. William Buckler's appeal was dismissed. The route to the European Court of Human Rights was blocked. The family was left with nowhere left to turn.

### 1988: The Final Year

The year opened with brutality. Branwen Buckler suffered a shoulder dislocation while serving the writ—injured in the very act of defending her family's right to be heard. Furniture from Cardiff Castle, heirlooms of historical significance that had belonged to the family, was looted from the property.

On 29 July 1988, Cadw inspectors visited Ty Mawr. They were not ignorant of what stood before them. They had the 1974 RCAHMW survey. They had the local designation as a "county treasure." They had the 1,700-signature petition. They concluded that the building did not meet listing criteria. Only two photographs were taken. No protective designation was applied. The farmhouse that had sheltered a family since 1667 was left naked before the law.

An emergency-listing evaluation is recorded as having been underway during a critical window of approximately forty-eight hours that overlapped with the eviction and demolition. No protective designation was in force when the machinery arrived.

### December 5–6, 1988: The Eviction

On the winter night of 5 December 1988, five bailiffs and approximately twenty police officers attended Great House Farm to enforce an eviction. Inside were a pregnant woman—Branwen Buckler—and three young children.

Bill Buckler blocked the narrow drive with a tractor. He locked the farmhouse doors and barred the windows. The bailiffs smashed the lock on the gate, walked around the tractor, and began smashing the doors with poleaxes. Bill Buckler defended his home with a chainsaw. "That's when I started my chainsaw and pushed it through the door. They backed off then," he told reporters. His wife Branwen later left the farm with the children; several friends and tenants remained with him through the night.

Police waited outside during the four-hour confrontation. A South Wales Police spokesman emphasised that officers were present only to prevent a breach of the peace and not to assist the bailiffs. Bill Buckler told reporters: "I am here to stay and I will fight this to the death."

The final clearance and demolition followed swiftly. Just after 4 a.m. on 6 December, a twenty-strong team moved in with bulldozers. By breakfast time, nothing was left standing.

Branwen Buckler, watching with her three children, said the family were "absolutely devastated." She described Great House Farm as "more than just a house, it was the family history with all the people who have been born or died there over the last 400 years. It is irreplaceable." The family claimed approximately £30,000 worth of personal possessions had been destroyed or lost. A carved dressed-stone capital that had stood by the front door for centuries was lost in the rubble.

The GGAT Historic Environment Record notes that the house and farm buildings were "suddenly and completely demolished by B.P. Properties Ltd. on 6th December 1988 amid considerable local controversy."

The RCAHMW survey sheet ST 16817331, prepared with such care by H.J. Thomas on 26 April 1974, received a contemporary manuscript annotation: destroyed 12 December 1988.

### 1989: The Language of Erasure

In the months that followed, outline planning application 89/01396/OUT was submitted for residential development. Contemporary planning documentation classified the vanished structure as a "dilapidated utility building" or "ruined barn." The language stands in grotesque tension with every prior record of the house's significance—the RCAHMW survey, the "county treasure" designation, the 1,700 signatures, and the centuries of family life.

### 1992: The Death of Billy Buckler

William "Billy" Buckler died in 1992, four years after watching his family home reduced to rubble. He had fought with a chainsaw, with the law, and with every fibre of his being to protect the house his mother had defended before him. He died without justice, without recompense, and without the home that had been his birthright.

---

The reader who has followed this chronology from the family's quiet centuries of possession to the clang of poleaxes, the roar of bulldozers, and the early death of the man who fought to the end must surely ask: How did a place of such peace, such faith, and such continuous family love become a warzone? How did a Christian home beside an ancient church become a site of such violence? And how did every institution that might have stopped it—the heritage services, the courts, the police, the planners—either stand aside or assist in the destruction?

The chapters that follow seek the answers.

# CHAPTER 2
## Historical Tenure and the Separate-Plot Hypothesis

The surviving documentary material identified to date indicates family occupation of Ty Mawr from at least 1667. While the records include evidence of a long-term hereditary lease arrangement, the precise legal character of the family's historic interest remains a matter requiring further documentary recovery.

### Working Hypothesis: Separate-Plot Status

A central question is whether the farmhouse and immediate garden were historically and legally treated as a distinct plot separate from the wider agricultural holding during key title transitions, including the 1955 and 1962 possession proceedings. Until original first-registration plans and filed instruments are inspected, this remains a working hypothesis to test whether legal displacement of agricultural land applied equally to the residential core.

### Cartographic and Administrative Evolution

Cartographic and administrative records across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries reveal shifts in terminology that demand explanation:

- **Descriptive Evolution:** Public references to the site changed across successive decades, appearing variously as "Great Court House," "Great House Farm," and "Grange." When did the property cease to be described as the family's historic house, and why?
- **Record Designations:** Mary Doreen Williams—whose maternal ancestors held the land across generations—was recorded in later legal proceedings primarily as "Mrs Buckler," adopting her late husband's name. The erasure of the Williams matrilineal line from the legal record mirrors the broader erasure of the family's status as owners rather than occupiers.

### The Question of Title

If the farmhouse and garden were historically distinct from the agricultural acres, then the possession proceedings of 1955 and 1962 may have proceeded on a false premise: that the entire holding was a single unit of agricultural land subject to landlord-tenant law, rather than a freehold homestead encircled by tenanted fields. The family's refusal to pay rent to challengers was not mere obstinacy; it was the assertion of a fundamentally different legal relationship—one of ownership, not tenancy.

# CHAPTER 3
## The 1974 Correspondence and the 1987 Court of Appeal Judgment

The legal history requires distinguishing four separate evidential questions surrounding the 1974 events and their subsequent treatment in *BP Properties Ltd v Buckler* [1987] EWCA Civ 2:

1. **Date of Letters:** The physical issuing of the unilateral letter by BP Properties Ltd on 4 November 1974, offering a personal lifetime licence to Mary Williams following the withdrawal of an eviction warrant on 31 October 1974.
2. **Receipt:** The delivery of the correspondence to Mary Williams.
3. **Mary's Response:** Mary Williams' refusal to sign the licence, her non-payment of rent, her explicit rejection of BP's ownership claims, and her active assertion of independent title.
4. **Court Construction:** Following Mary's death in 1983, the Court of Appeal treated the 1974 correspondence as sufficient to establish permissive occupation, concluding that this unilateral grant interrupted the period of adverse possession.

### The Key Legal Question

How correspondence that an occupant expressly rejected came to have the legal effect attributed to it remains the legal heart of the case. What exactly did the Court decide regarding the nature and duration of her occupation, and its subsequent application to her son William Buckler? The judgment upheld the paper title without ever extinguishing the family's underlying claim. It ruled on possession, not on ownership. It found for BP without finding against the Bucklers' historical right.

The family's appeal was dismissed. The European Court of Human Rights was never reached. The legal door was closed, but the historical question was never answered.

# CHAPTER 4
## Land Registration and the 1982–1983 First-Registration Bundles

In 1982 and 1983, BP Properties Ltd registered absolute title under Title Numbers WA231076 and WA240304. At the time of registration, Mary Williams was in active physical occupation and asserting independent rights. Obtaining the 1982 first-registration bundle from HM Land Registry represents Priority 1 for this investigation.

### The Priority 1 Target

Inspecting the original application forms, filed deeds, and deposited plans will establish:

- What exact land boundaries were claimed and registered.
- Whether the farmhouse/garden plot was identified as legally distinct.
- What disclosures were made regarding actual occupation under the Land Registration Act 1925.
- What direct correspondence occurred between the Land Registry, BP, or third parties during registration.
- How the registered boundaries compare directly against the land affected by the 1955 and 1962 possession proceedings.

### Possession vs. Registration

The central tension is stark: a family in physical occupation, asserting independent title, while another party succeeded in registering the absolute title to the same land. The first-registration bundles hold the answer to whether the registry was informed of the dispute, whether the family's presence was disclosed, and whether the boundaries registered reflected the reality on the ground.

# CHAPTER 5
## Archival Anomalies and Missing Records

An examination of local archives reveals missing documentary links that cannot be ignored:

- **Conveyance Papers:** Historical agreements dated between 1895 and 1905 involving Daniel Thomas & Son, which detailed title transactions connected to the Williams line, are no longer locatable in public collections.
- **Catalogue References:** Corresponding archive index cards and catalogue references previously cited in mid-twentieth-century record collections cannot currently be located in public search facilities.

### Investigative Methodology

The absence of a document does not establish that it was deliberately removed. The investigation therefore asks a narrower question: when was the document last recorded, who held it, and what happened to the relevant collection thereafter? The silence of the archive is itself a fact that demands explanation.

# CHAPTER 6
## The RCAHMW Architectural Record and Historic Designations

Under Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales (RCAHMW) catalogue reference ST 16817331, primary research has located the original floor plan surveyed by architectural historian H.J. Thomas, dated 26 April 1974. This primary record establishes that the building had been formally documented by RCAHMW before its demolition. The sheet features a contemporary manuscript annotation noting destruction on 12 December 1988, serving as an explicit record of the later demolition date noted on the commission's file.

Furthermore, Glamorgan-Gwent Archaeological Trust (GGAT) planning papers contemporaneous with the development process noted that the property had previously been referred to as a "county treasure." This pre-demolition local attribution in GGAT documentation underscores the central question of institutional awareness across the responsible authorities.

### Timeline: What the Surviving Record Shows

- **Before 1988:** Historical family occupation indicated from 1667. Prior local finds indicate site antiquity. RCAHMW floor plan surveyed by H.J. Thomas on 26 April 1974 (Ref: ST 16817331). Referred to as a "county treasure" in GGAT planning material.
- **December 1988:** Emergency listing evaluation conducted by Cadw across a ~48-hour window. Night-time eviction executed. Building demolished overnight (annotated as 12 December 1988 on RCAHMW sheet).
- **1989:** Outline planning application 89/01396/OUT submitted for residential development; decision notice issued subject to pre-commencement conditions including archaeological requirements.
- **1993:** Groundworks and earthmoving commence. Glamorgan-Gwent Archaeological Trust identifies unfulfilled archaeological mitigation conditions. Emergency archaeological intervention initiated.
- **1994:** Emergency excavations by GGAT record more than 1,026 early-medieval inhumation burials, along with an early Christian enclosure (*lann*) linked to St Dochdwy, Roman villa structures, and Iron Age settlement evidence.

# CHAPTER 7
## Planning Permission 89/01396/OUT and the Condition 17 Compliance Question

The archaeological issue was formally addressed during the consideration of planning application 89/01396/OUT. When the planning decision notice was issued in 1989, outline permission was made subject to explicit pre-commencement obligations under Conditions 16–18. Condition 17 explicitly stipulated requirements that establish a direct, targetable documentary compliance test:

- **The Assessment Report:** Where is the documentary record of the evaluation survey and archaeological assessment report carried out by a qualified archaeologist within three months of consent?
- **Formal Sign-off:** Where are the LPA officer, committee, or correspondence records establishing that the survey and report were accepted as "to the satisfaction of the Local Planning Authority"?
- **Monitoring & Enforcement:** If no such report or written approval exists in the file, what administrative, monitoring, or enforcement records exist showing how the authority handled the Condition 17 requirement prior to ground clearance?

The compliance question is binary: either the condition was met, and the record should exist, or it was not, and the explanation should be documented. Neither record is currently locatable.

# CHAPTER 8
## 1993–1994—The Cemetery Discovery and Exhumation

When emergency excavations were initiated in early 1994 by the Glamorgan-Gwent Archaeological Trust (GGAT), archaeologists uncovered an extensive early-medieval complex that vindicated every prior suspicion about the site's significance:

- **Recorded Burials:** Available GGAT summary records indicate that more than 1,026 early-medieval inhumation burials were recorded within the excavated boundary; this figure remains provisional pending detailed verification against the primary published excavation report.
- **Historical Context:** Excavations revealed structural evidence of an early Christian ecclesiastical enclosure associated historically with St Dochdwy, along with underlying Roman villa remains and Iron Age settlement evidence.

Following excavation, human remains were removed under the applicable statutory exhumation regime, and residential construction proceeded. Identification of the specific exhumation licences and custody records remains an active research priority.

The discovery of over a thousand burials beneath the footprint of Ty Mawr transforms the question from one of property law into one of sacred ground. The family had lived for centuries atop an early Christian cemetery without ever knowing the full extent of what lay beneath their floorboards. The authorities, once informed, proceeded to excavate and develop.

# CHAPTER 9
## The Ten Key Unanswered Questions

The surviving record contains enough unresolved documentary questions to justify a systematic reconstruction of what happened at Great House Farm. The purpose of that reconstruction is not to predetermine the answer, but to establish what can be proved, what can be disproved, and what remains unknowable because the underlying records no longer survive. The discovery of over a thousand early-medieval burials makes the question considerably bigger than a family property dispute.

To establish the full truth of what occurred, ten primary questions remain to be answered:

1. **Separate-Plot Legal Status:** Did 1955/1962 possession proceedings and title deeds treat the farmhouse/garden as legally distinct from the surrounding agricultural land?
2. **First-Registration Application (Priority 1):** What precise plans, deeds, and actual occupation disclosures were included in BP's 1982 first-registration bundle for Titles WA231076 and WA240304?
3. **Archive Integrity:** When were the 1895–1905 conveyances and associated catalogue cards last recorded, who held them, and what happened to that archive collection?
4. **Legal Construction:** What exactly did the Court of Appeal decide regarding the legal effect of the unaccepted 1974 correspondence on Mary Williams and her heirs?
5. **Architectural Record:** What weight was given to the H.J. Thomas RCAHMW floor plan (ST 16817331, dated 26 April 1974) and GGAT "county treasure" reference by planning and heritage authorities prior to demolition?
6. **Heritage Timing:** What information was exchanged between local authorities and Cadw during the ~48-hour emergency listing window in December 1988?
7. **Condition 17 Compliance:** Was the archaeological assessment report required by Condition 17 of planning permission 89/01396/OUT carried out to the LPA's satisfaction within three months of consent, and where is the formal sign-off logged?
8. **Human Remains & Custody:** What became of the human remains after excavation; which authority issued each statutory exhumation licence; where were the remains deposited, studied, or reinterred; and what custody records survive?
9. **Site Protection:** Given the archaeological discoveries made in 1993–94, why was no subsequent heritage protection considered for the surviving archaeological site?
10. **Institutional Knowledge:** What did each principal authority actually know about the site's history and archaeology, when did it know it, what did it do with that knowledge, and what documentary record survives to show why?

# EVIDENTIAL VERIFICATION & RESEARCH STATUS

To maintain investigative discipline, the evidence behind this proposal is mapped against primary sources, current status, and specific verification targets. The framework is designed to ensure that every claim is traceable, every absence is noted, and every question is directed toward a recoverable document.

The house is gone. The cemetery was excavated. The development was built. But the documentary record remains incomplete. The question now is not simply what happened at Great House Farm, but what the institutions responsible for land registration, heritage protection, planning, and archaeology knew at each stage, what they did with that knowledge, and what their records can still tell us.

The central question connecting every stage of this history is direct and testable: *What did the principal authorities actually know about the site's history and archaeology, when did they know it, what action did they take, and what documentary record remains to demonstrate why?*
