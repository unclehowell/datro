3.1  The Farm at the Turn of the Century: Continuity and Change
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The year 1900 found Great House Farm in a state of uneasy equilibrium. The
industrial transformation of Glamorgan was now well advanced: the coal mines of
the northern valleys were producing millions of tons a year, the iron and steel
works of Cardiff and Newport were among the largest in the world, and the
railway network had stitched the rural parishes into an urban economy that was
changing everything it touched. Yet the farm itself — the ancient stone house,
the barns, the fields, the orchard, the well — remained, outwardly at least,
much as it had been for generations. The Williams family still occupied the
farm, still worked the land, still believed in their hereditary right to the
property.

The Llandough Limeworks, which had severed 33 acres from the farm in 1876,
were still in operation, and their presence was a constant reminder of the
industrial world that pressed against the farm's boundaries. The quarrying had
altered the landscape: the pits, the kilns, the railway sidings, and the
cottages of the quarry workers had transformed a corner of the parish from
rural farmland into an industrial zone. The dust from the kilns would have
settled on the farm's fields, the noise of the blasting would have disturbed
the animals, and the traffic of carts and wagons would have worn ruts in the
lanes. Yet the limeworks also provided employment for local men, and the
Williams family may have had dealings with the quarry — selling produce,
grazing rights, or labour — that supplemented the farm's income.

The Daniel Thomas arrangement — the agreement whereby the family believed they
had acquired title to the farmhouse and garden plot in return for quarrying
rights — was, by 1900, already a matter of family tradition rather than active
negotiation. The tree that had been planted to mark the transfer would have been
a young sapling, growing slowly in the farmyard or the garden, a living symbol
of the family's claim. The missing deeds, if they had ever existed, were
already becoming the stuff of legend: the family knew they had once had proof
of their title, and they knew that the proof was now gone, but they continued
to believe, and to act as if the belief were enough.

3.2  Jim Driscoll and the Barns: Sport and Community (c. 1901)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE]** Heritage records state that Welsh boxing champion Jim ("Peerless")
Driscoll fought his earliest contests in the barns of Great House Farm before
turning professional in 1901. The association is recorded within the National
Monuments Record for Wales and has subsequently been cited during heritage
campaigns concerning the site.

The connection between Great House Farm and Jim Driscoll — one of the greatest
boxers Wales has ever produced — is one of the most colourful episodes in the
farm's history, and it is also one of the most poorly documented. Driscoll,
who was born in 1880 in the Newtown area of Cardiff, began his boxing career
in the informal contests that were a feature of working-class life in south
Wales at the turn of the century. These contests were not the regulated,
sanctioned bouts of modern boxing; they were rough, often brutal affairs, held
in barns, back rooms, and makeshift rings, with few rules and little
protection. The barns of Great House Farm — large, high-ceilinged, and
discreetly located — would have been an ideal venue for such events.

The National Monuments Record for Wales (now part of the RCAHMW/Coflein
database) records the Driscoll association, but the precise source of the
information is not clear. Was it based on family testimony? On local
recollection? On a newspaper report? The record does not say, and the original
document has not been examined. The association has been cited in heritage
campaigns — most notably in the 1970s, when the farm's demolition was being
contested — as evidence of the building's cultural significance, but its
historical accuracy has not been independently verified.

If the story is true, it places Great House Farm at the centre of a remarkable
moment in Welsh sporting history. Driscoll turned professional in 1901 and went
on to become the featherweight champion of Britain, winning the Lonsdale Belt
outright in 1910. He fought in New York, Paris, and London, and he was revered
in Wales as a working-class hero who had risen from poverty to international
fame. The barns where he had fought his earliest contests — where he had
learned his craft, honed his skills, and earned his nickname "Peerless" —
would have been hallowed ground for Welsh boxing enthusiasts, and their
destruction in 1988 would have been a loss not merely to the Williams family
but to the cultural heritage of the nation.

3.3  The Great War: A Farm at War (1914–1918)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE/FT]** Great House Farm hosted a wartime Chapel Sale of Work fundraising
fete during the First World War (1914–1918).

The First World War transformed every aspect of British life, and Great House
Farm was no exception. The farm's men — the Williams sons, the hired labourers,
the neighbouring farmers — would have been among the first to enlist, drawn by
patriotism, by economic necessity, or by the pressure of community expectation.
The farm's women — the wives, mothers, and daughters — would have taken on the
work of the men, managing the fields and the animals while their husbands and
sons were away at the front. The farm's produce — milk, meat, grain, and
vegetables — would have been in heavy demand, as the nation struggled to feed
itself while German submarines threatened the supply of imported food.

The Chapel Sale of Work fundraising fete was a characteristic product of the
war years. These events — part bazaar, part social gathering, part patriotic
demonstration — were organised by chapel congregations across Wales to raise
money for the war effort, for the relief of refugees, and for the support of
soldiers' families. The fact that Great House Farm hosted such a fete
indicates that the family were active members of the chapel community, and that
the farm was still regarded as a place of gathering and hospitality. The fete
would have been held in the barns or in the farmyard, with stalls selling
cakes, jams, and handiwork, with games and competitions for the children, and
with speeches and prayers for the victory of the Allied cause.

The war also brought changes to the farm's labour force. With the men away at
the front, the farm would have relied increasingly on women, on older men, and
on the labourers from the limeworks who were exempt from military service. The
Women's Land Army, established in 1917, may have supplied workers to the farm,
though there is no record of this in the current materials. The farm's horses
— essential for ploughing, hauling, and transport — may have been requisitioned
by the army, and the transition from horse power to mechanical power, which was
already under way in British agriculture, may have been accelerated by the
wartime shortage of equine labour.

3.5  The 1916 Tenancy: The Documentary Divergence
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE]** In February 1916, a yearly agricultural tenancy was granted by the
Marquess of Bute to John Williams. This marks the clearest documentary
divergence between estate paperwork (tenant) and family belief (owner).

The 1916 tenancy agreement is one of the most important documents in the entire
history of Great House Farm, and it is also one of the most contentious. On its
face, it is a straightforward agricultural lease: the Marquess of Bute, as
landlord, granted to John Williams, as tenant, a yearly tenancy of Great House
Farm, at a rent to be agreed, subject to the terms and conditions of the
Agricultural Holdings Act. The agreement was in writing, it was signed by both
parties, and it was registered with the estate. It is, from the estate's
perspective, the definitive statement of the legal relationship between landlord
and tenant at this date.

For the Williams family, however, the 1916 tenancy was something quite
different. According to family testimony, the agreement was not a genuine lease
but a formality — a piece of paper that the family signed in order to maintain
peace with the estate, while their true ownership of the farmhouse and garden
plot remained unaffected. The family believed that their title, acquired through
the 1667 transaction and confirmed by the Daniel Thomas arrangement, was
superior to any tenancy agreement, and that the 1916 document was merely a
recognition of their continued occupation of the surrounding farmland. This
belief — whether legally justified or not — would sustain the family through
the decades of litigation that followed.

The divergence between the estate's view and the family's view is the central
paradox of the Great House Farm case. The estate had the documents: the 1916
tenancy, the rentals, the notices to quit, the court orders. The family had the
memory: the 1667 lease, the Daniel Thomas arrangement, the tree-planting
ceremony, the generations of occupation. Which was the truth? The 1987 Court of
Appeal, which had access only to the estate's documents, found in favour of the
estate. But the family's claim — that the documents told only part of the
story, and that the missing deeds would have told the rest — has never been
judicially tested.

3.6  The 1924 Estate Sale: Packaging the Manor
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE]** In 1924, the Llandough Estate sale took place. "Great House" appears
in the sale (Glamorgan Archives reference DSA/6/701), suggesting the Bute/Byass
estates were packaging manorial lands.

The 1924 estate sale was part of the wider process of estate rationalisation
that had begun with the Bute consolidation of 1824 and that would continue
throughout the twentieth century. The Marquess of Bute — by 1924, the 4th
Marquess, John Crichton-Stuart — was engaged in a systematic programme of
selling off peripheral lands in order to concentrate his resources on the core
estates and on the industrial enterprises that were the true source of his
wealth. The Llandough lands, which had been acquired piecemeal over the
preceding century, were now being packaged for sale as a single unit.

The appearance of "Great House" in the sale particulars (DSA/6/701) is
significant, for it suggests that the property was still regarded as a
substantial holding — a "Great House" rather than a mere farm — at this date.
The sale was presumably advertised in the local and national press, and it
would have attracted the attention of investors, developers, and speculators
who were looking for land in the expanding Cardiff area. Whether the Williams
family were consulted about the sale, whether they were offered the chance to
buy the freehold, or whether they were simply informed that their landlord had
changed, is not known.

The 1924 sale did not result in the dispossession of the Williams family. They
remained in occupation, presumably under the terms of the 1916 tenancy or a
successor agreement, and the farm continued to operate much as before. But the
sale marked a further step in the separation of the estate from the land: the
Bute family, which had once been the direct landlord of the Williams family,
was now retreating into the background, and the new owners — whoever they were —
would bring a different set of priorities and a different style of management
to the property.

3.7  Mountjoy Ltd: The Intermediate Titleholder (1926–1939)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE]** From 1926 to 1939, the intermediate titleholder was Mountjoy Ltd, a
private family company for the Bute Estate.

Mountjoy Ltd was a vehicle created by the Bute family to hold and manage
certain of their Glamorgan properties. As a private company, it was not subject
to the same public scrutiny as the estate itself, and its records are
correspondingly obscure. The company's relationship to the Williams family —
whether it collected the rent, issued the notices, or managed the property
directly — is not known, but its existence as the legal owner of Great House
Farm during this period adds another layer of complexity to the chain of title.

The period 1926–1939 was one of profound economic and social change in Britain.
The General Strike of 1926, the Great Depression of the 1930s, and the rise of
unemployment and poverty in the industrial areas of south Wales all affected
the rural parishes, as displaced workers sought refuge in the countryside and
as the demand for agricultural produce fluctuated with the state of the urban
economy. For Great House Farm, these changes would have meant both challenges
and opportunities: the challenge of maintaining profitability in a depressed
market, and the opportunity of supplying food to a growing population of
urban consumers.

The 1930s also saw the beginnings of suburban development around Cardiff. The
coastal plain of Glamorgan, with its good roads, its railway connections, and
its proximity to the city, was an obvious target for housing estates, and the
pressure to develop agricultural land for building was already being felt in
parishes like Llandough. The Williams family, who had watched the limeworks
take 33 acres of their land in 1876, would have been acutely aware of the
threat that development posed to their remaining holding.

3.8  The 1938 Sale: The Auction That Did Not Result in Possession
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE]** In 1938, Western Ground Rents Ltd bought the urban leaseholds of the
Bute Glamorgan Estate from Mountjoy Ltd. The reversion was conveyed to Western
Ground Rents Ltd, a Cardiff-based ground-rent company.

On 16 November 1938, the Western Mail (Page 12) reported that Great House Farm
was advertised as "sold" at auction by Herbert R. Thomas (Bute Estate /
Mountjoy Ltd nominee). The vendor was recorded as D.J. Jenkins. The Williams
family remained in physical occupation.

The 1938 sale was a turning point in the history of Great House Farm, for it
brought a new kind of owner to the property — one that was not an aristocratic
estate, not a family company, but a commercial ground-rent company whose
business was the collection of rents and the enforcement of leasehold
covenants. Western Ground Rents Ltd was a Cardiff-based company that had
acquired the urban leaseholds of the Bute Glamorgan Estate, and its interest
in Great House Farm was purely financial: it wanted the rent, and it wanted
the power to enforce its rights as landlord.

The auction of 16 November 1938 was the public face of this transfer. Herbert
R. Thomas, a well-known auctioneer from Cowbridge, conducted the sale on behalf
of the Bute Estate / Mountjoy Ltd, and the property was advertised as "sold" in
the Western Mail. The vendor was recorded as D.J. Jenkins — presumably a
nominee or agent of the estate — and the sale was presumably conducted in the
usual manner, with a reserve price, a deposit, and a completion date. Yet the
sale did not result in the dispossession of the Williams family. They remained
in physical occupation, and the farm continued to operate under their
management.

Why did the advertised sale not result in possession? There are several
possible explanations. The purchaser may have been a speculator who bought the
property as an investment, with no intention of taking possession. The
Williams family may have had a legal right to remain — a protected tenancy
under the Agricultural Holdings Act, or a customary right that the sale could
not extinguish. Or the sale may have been flawed in some way — the title may
have been defective, the notice may have been inadequate, or the family may
have challenged the transaction in court. Whatever the reason, the fact that
the family remained in occupation after the 1938 sale is one of the most
significant features of the farm's history, for it demonstrates that the
transfer of paper title did not necessarily mean the transfer of physical
possession.

3.10  The Buckler Succession: Frederick and Mary Take Over (2 February 1949)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE/FT]** On 2 February 1949, a new tenancy was granted to Mr Frederick
Buckler. Mrs Mary Williams (Mr Williams' daughter) and her husband Frederick
Buckler took over from Mr Williams' agricultural tenancy. Mrs Williams,
although married, retained her maiden name. No written agreement was executed.

The arrival of Frederick Buckler at Great House Farm in 1949 marked the
beginning of a new chapter in the family's history — and the beginning of the
end. Frederick, who had been born in 1888 and who had married Mary Williams
some years earlier, was now the formal tenant of the farm, and his name
appeared on the estate's records as the person responsible for the rent and
the covenants. Yet the transition was not straightforward. Mary Williams,
although married, retained her maiden name in daily life and in her dealings
with the estate — a practice that would later become a source of confusion and
contention in the legal proceedings.

The 1949 tenancy was, according to the current record, not a written agreement.
This is significant, for it suggests that the arrangement was informal — a
verbal understanding between the family and the estate, or a simple renewal of
the existing tenancy with a change of name. The absence of a written agreement
would later be used by the estate to argue that the tenancy was not protected
by the Agricultural Holdings Act, and that the family had no legal right to
remain after the notice to quit was served. The family, for their part, would
argue that the absence of a written agreement was evidence of their ownership —
that they did not need a lease because the property was already theirs.

The 1949 tenancy also marked a generational shift. Frederick Buckler was not a
Williams; he was an outsider, a man who had married into the family and who
brought his own name, his own history, and his own expectations to the farm.
Whether the estate regarded him with suspicion, as a potential troublemaker, or
with indifference, as merely the husband of the tenant's daughter, is not
known. But the fact that the estate chose to grant the tenancy to Frederick
rather than to Mary — or to both of them jointly — suggests that the estate was
already beginning to separate the legal title from the family's claim of
hereditary right.

3.12  1953: The Last Rent Payment
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE]** In 1953, Mr Frederick Buckler made his last rent payment, under a
judgment for arrears of rent. His periodic tenancy, protected by the
Agricultural Holdings Act, was terminated with a notice to quit expiring on
2 February 1955.

The last rent payment of 1953 was a watershed moment. For more than a century —
perhaps for more than two centuries — the Williams family had paid rent to the
estate, and the payment had been the outward sign of their subordinate legal
position. The cessation of rent payments in 1953 marked a break with this
tradition, and it set the stage for the confrontation that would follow.

The circumstances of the last payment are significant. It was made "under a
judgment for arrears of rent" — which suggests that the family had fallen
behind with their payments, and that the estate had obtained a court order
compelling them to pay. The fact that the payment was made under compulsion,
rather than voluntarily, indicates that the relationship between the family and
the estate was already strained, and that the family were either unable or
unwilling to meet their financial obligations.

The notice to quit, which expired on 2 February 1955, was the formal
instrument by which the estate sought to end the tenancy. Under the
Agricultural Holdings Act, a periodic tenancy could be terminated by a notice
to quit, provided that the notice was given in the proper form and at the
proper time. The estate's decision to serve such a notice — after decades of
apparently tolerating the family's occupation — suggests a change of policy, a
new determination to assert the estate's rights, and a willingness to use the
courts to achieve its ends.

3.13  2 February 1955: The Tenancy Formally Ends
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[EF]** On 2 February 1955, the original tenancy was formally terminated. The
agricultural tenancy formally ended. Both parents were found to have the
required animus possidendi (intention to possess).

The termination of the tenancy on 2 February 1955 was the legal moment at
which the family's status changed from tenants to occupiers without the
landlord's consent. The concept of animus possidendi — the intention to possess
— is a technical one, but it is crucial to understanding the family's position.
Under English and Welsh law, a person who occupies land without the owner's
permission may acquire a right to possession through adverse possession, provided
that the occupation is open, continuous, and accompanied by the intention to
possess. The finding that both Frederick and Mary Buckler had the required
animus possidendi meant that they were not mere squatters or trespassers; they
were persons who intended to hold the land as their own, and whose occupation
might, in time, ripen into a legal right.

The significance of this finding would not become fully apparent until the
litigation of the 1960s and 1970s, when the family would argue that their
occupation since 1955 was protected by the Limitation Act 1939, and that the
estate's claim to possession was therefore barred by the passage of time. The
estate, for its part, would argue that the family's occupation was not adverse
— that it was permissive, or licensed, or subject to some agreement that
prevented the running of the limitation period. The dispute over the nature of
the family's occupation — whether it was adverse, permissive, or something in
between — would become the central legal issue of the case.

3.14  1955: The High Court Possession Proceedings
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[EF]** In 1955, Western Ground Rents Ltd brought possession proceedings in
the High Court under Order 14 and obtained a possession order for the whole
farm.

The High Court possession proceedings of 1955 were the first major legal
confrontation between the family and the estate. Western Ground Rents Ltd,
which had acquired the property in 1938, was now determined to assert its
rights as landlord, and it chose to bring its action in the High Court — the
most powerful and most expensive court in the land. The use of Order 14, which
allowed for summary judgment in cases where there was no defence, suggests that
Western Ground Rents believed that the family's claim was so weak that it could
be disposed of without a full trial.

The possession order for the whole farm was a sweeping victory for the estate.
It gave Western Ground Rents the right to take possession of the entire
property — the farmhouse, the outbuildings, the fields, the garden, and
everything else — and to eject the family by force if necessary. The order was
a declaration of war, and the family's response — their refusal to leave, their
insistence on their ownership, their appeal to public opinion and to the courts
— would shape the course of the dispute for the next three decades.

3.15  4 July 1955: Partial Enforcement and Mary's Leg Amputation
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[EF]** On 4 July 1955, the possession order was enforced for the farm except
the farmhouse and garden. Mrs Mary Williams's strong objections, following her
leg amputation and hospitalisation, prevented the enforcement against the
farmhouse. The Williams' occupation of the farmhouse and garden remained
undisturbed.

The partial enforcement of 4 July 1955 is one of the most dramatic episodes in
the farm's history, and it is also one of the most revealing. The estate had
obtained a possession order for the whole farm, and it had the legal right to
enforce that order in its entirety. Yet it chose — or was compelled — to
enforce it only partially, leaving the family in possession of the farmhouse
and garden. The reason for this partial enforcement was Mrs Mary Williams's
"strong objections" following her leg amputation and hospitalisation.

The amputation of Mary Williams's leg is a detail that illuminates the human
cost of the legal dispute. Mary was not merely a litigant; she was a woman who
had suffered a serious medical emergency, who had been hospitalised, and who
was now facing the loss of her home at the very moment when she was least able
to resist. The estate's decision — or the bailiffs' decision, or the court's
decision — to spare the farmhouse was presumably motivated by compassion, or by
practicality, or by the fear of bad publicity. Whatever the reason, the result
was that the family remained in the farmhouse, and their occupation of that
particular building — the core of their ancestral home — continued undisturbed.

The partial enforcement is also significant for the Separate Plot Hypothesis.
If the estate had obtained possession of the whole farm, why did it not enforce
the order against the farmhouse? One possible explanation is that the estate
recognised — consciously or unconsciously — that the farmhouse was a separate
legal entity from the surrounding farmland, and that its possession required a
separate legal process. Another explanation is that the estate simply did not
want to evict a sick woman from her home. The truth is not known, but the
partial enforcement remains a crucial piece of evidence for anyone seeking to
understand the legal and moral complexities of the case.

3.16  1959: The Tenancy Offer Refused
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE/FT]** In 1959, Mrs Williams believed she had title to the property
through her grandfather. She produced no documents. She was offered a tenancy
of the farmhouse and garden by Western Ground Rents but refused to accept it.

The 1959 tenancy offer was a critical moment in the dispute. Western Ground
Rents, having obtained possession of the farmland in 1955, was now prepared to
offer the family a formal tenancy of the farmhouse and garden — a recognition,
however grudging, that the family were not going to leave voluntarily. The
offer was presumably made in the hope of regularising the situation, of
obtaining a reliable rent, and of avoiding the costs and uncertainties of
further litigation. For the family, however, the offer was an insult. Mary
Williams believed that she had title to the property through her grandfather,
and she was not prepared to accept a tenancy that would confirm her status as a
mere tenant rather than an owner.

The refusal of the 1959 offer set the pattern for the family's response to all

tenancy implied subordination. They would not sign a document, because a
document might be used against them. They would not negotiate, because
negotiation implied a willingness to compromise a right that they believed was
absolute. This stance — principled, stubborn, and ultimately self-defeating —
would characterise the family's conduct throughout the litigation, and it would
contribute to the tragedy of their eventual dispossession.

3.18  1962: The Second Possession Order — Judge Temple Morris QC
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[EF]** In 1962, Western Ground Rents brought a fresh action in Cardiff County
Court against Mr Frederick Buckler and Mrs Buckler (sued as Mrs Williams) for
possession of the farmhouse and garden and mesne profits since 1955.

On 11 December 1962, Judge Temple Morris QC made a possession order together
with an order for payment of mesne profits. The claim did not result in
eviction.

The 1962 proceedings were a new phase in the litigation. Having failed to
dislodge the family from the farmhouse in 1955, Western Ground Rents now
brought a fresh action in the County Court — a lower and less expensive forum
than the High Court — seeking possession of the farmhouse and garden, and
mesne profits (compensation for use and occupation) for the period since 1955.
The defendants were Mr Frederick Buckler and Mrs Buckler, who was sued as Mrs
Williams — a confusion of names that would plague the litigation for decades.

Judge Temple Morris QC was a distinguished lawyer who would later become a
leading figure in the Welsh judiciary. His possession order of 11 December 1962
was a formal declaration that the family had no legal right to remain in the
farmhouse, and that they were liable to pay compensation for their occupation.
Yet the order did not result in eviction. The family remained in the farmhouse,
and the order was not enforced — at least, not immediately. The reasons for
this non-enforcement are not fully understood, but they may have been
practical: the estate may have been reluctant to evict an elderly couple, or
the bailiffs may have been unable to carry out the order without excessive
force, or the family may have obtained a stay of execution on the grounds of
hardship or legal technicality.

The 1962 order would become a central document in the later litigation. It was
the order that BP Pension Trust Ltd would seek to enforce in 1974, and it was
the order that would be cited in the Court of Appeal in 1987 as evidence of
the family's long-standing liability to possession. Yet the fact that the order
was not enforced for more than a decade — that the family remained in the
farmhouse from 1962 to 1983 — suggests that the legal position was more
complex than the order itself indicated.

3.20  June 1963: Suspended Committal Order Against Frederick Buckler
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[EF]** In June 1963, a suspended committal order was made against Mr
Frederick Buckler, in reference to the 11 December 1962 order for payment of
mesne profits.

The suspended committal order of June 1963 was a further step in the estate's
campaign of legal pressure. A committal order is an order sending a person to
prison for contempt of court — in this case, for failing to pay the mesne
profits that Judge Temple Morris QC had ordered in December 1962. The fact
that the order was suspended — that is, that it was not immediately enforced —
suggests that the court was willing to give Frederick Buckler a final chance
to pay, or that the estate was not yet prepared to take the drastic step of
imprisoning an elderly farmer.

The suspended order would have hung over Frederick Buckler like a sword of
Damocles. He knew that if he failed to pay, he could be sent to prison. Yet he
also knew that paying would be an admission of defeat — a recognition that the
estate's claim was valid and that his family's occupation was merely
permissive. The dilemma was cruel, and it was typical of the legal warfare
that Western Ground Rents waged against its tenants: not merely the pursuit of
legal rights, but the systematic application of pressure through threats,
penalties, and the fear of imprisonment.

3.21  October 1964: The Agricultural Tenancy Officially Ends
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE]** In October 1964, the agricultural tenancy of Great House Farm (held
by the Williams family) officially came to an end. Mr Frederick Buckler and
his wife remained in the farmhouse.

The official end of the agricultural tenancy in October 1964 was a formality —
a legal marker that had little practical effect on the family's position. They
had already been without a formal tenancy since 1955, and their occupation had
long been treated as "adverse" by the estate. Yet the formal termination was
significant, for it removed any lingering doubt about the family's legal status.
They were no longer tenants; they were occupiers without the landlord's consent,
and their only legal protection was the possibility that their occupation might,
in time, ripen into a right of adverse possession.

The fact that Frederick Buckler and his wife remained in the farmhouse after
the tenancy ended — that they did not leave, did not pay rent, and did not
acknowledge the estate's title — was the foundation of their later claim of
adverse possession. Under the Limitation Act 1939, a person who occupied land
without the owner's permission for twelve years could acquire a right to
possession that was enforceable against the owner. The family's occupation
from 1955 to 1964 was nine years; from 1955 to 1974, it would be nineteen
years — well beyond the statutory period. If the occupation was truly adverse —
if it was open, continuous, and without the owner's permission — then the
family's claim would be strong.

3.23  Late March 1965: The Refusal and the Beginning of "Adverse" Occupation
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[DE]** In late March 1965, Mrs Williams and Mr Frederick Buckler did not sign
or return the tenancy agreement. They remained in the house, and because they
did not pay rent, their occupation began to be treated as "adverse" by the
owners.

The refusal of the 1965 tenancy offer was the moment at which the family's
occupation became formally "adverse" in the eyes of the law. By refusing to
sign the agreement, by refusing to pay rent, and by remaining in the house
without the owner's permission, the family were asserting their claim of
ownership in the most direct way possible. They were not merely arguing that
they had a right to the property; they were acting as if the property were
theirs, and they were daring the estate to dislodge them.

The legal consequences of this refusal were profound. Under the Limitation Act
1939, the period of adverse possession began to run from the moment at which
the owner's right to possession was first infringed. If the family's occupation
from March 1965 was adverse — if it was open, continuous, and without the
owner's permission — then the twelve-year limitation period would expire in
March 1977, and the family's claim would be barred. The estate's strategy,
therefore, was to prevent the running of the limitation period by obtaining the
family's acknowledgment of the estate's title, or by obtaining a court order
that interrupted the adverse possession. The 1974 unilateral licence — the
"Mrs Buckler" letters — would be the instrument by which the estate sought to
achieve this.

3.26  1969: "The Worst in Wales" and the Sale to BP Pension Trust
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**[EF]** In 1969, Western Ground Rents was explicitly labelled "The Worst in
Wales" in the House of Commons.

The 1969 label — "The Worst in Wales" — was the culmination of a decade of
Parliamentary criticism. It was not merely a description of the company's
conduct; it was a moral judgment, a declaration that Western Ground Rents had
no equal in its disregard for the welfare of its tenants. The label would have
been devastating to the company's reputation, and it may have contributed to
the decision to sell its interests in Great House Farm.

**[DE]** In December 1969, Western Ground Rents sold its interest in Great House
Farm to BP Pension Trust Ltd. BP Pension Trust Ltd acquired the interest in
Great House Farm, including the farmhouse and garden.

The sale to BP Pension Trust Ltd in December 1969 was a new chapter in the
dispute. BP Pension Trust was not a ground-rent company; it was the pension
fund of one of the largest corporations in the world, and its interest in Great
House Farm was presumably as an investment — a piece of real estate that would
appreciate in value as the Cardiff area developed. The sale price is not
recorded, but it was presumably substantial, reflecting the potential of the
land for future development.

For the family, the sale to BP Pension Trust was a mixed blessing. On the one
hand, it removed Western Ground Rents — "The Worst in Wales" — from the scene,
and it brought a new owner who might be more willing to negotiate. On the other
hand, it brought a corporate giant with immense legal and financial resources,
and with no emotional connection to the land or to the family. BP Pension Trust
was not interested in the farm's history, its archaeology, or its community
significance; it was interested in its value as a development site, and it would
pursue that interest with the full force of the law.

The sale also had a political dimension that would become significant in later
years. BP was a nationalised industry until 1979, and the government retained a
substantial shareholding even after privatisation. At the time of the 1987
Court of Appeal decision, the UK government still held approximately 31.5% of
BP's shares — a fact that would later be cited by the family as evidence that
the state and BP were not fully separate actors, and that the government's
interests were aligned with the company's.
