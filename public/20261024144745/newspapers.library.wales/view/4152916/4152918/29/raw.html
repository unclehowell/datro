Despite recent complaints, the Cardiff city fathers have now had an oppor- tunity of judging for themselves whether the music at Loudoun-equare thas the un- looked-for effect of ruining public pro- perty. A number of councillors were on I the spot last evening, and whilst. the band played some hundreds of youngsters danced and rolled upon the turf. No one will find fault with them for this. ''Keep off the grass" is the eleventh command- i ment in England, but we doubt whethei the Corporation will dream of enforcing it under circumstances such as this. This is the last day of a month which I f f has proved once again—if proo f were needed—the hopelessly capricious cha- I racter of the British climate. April was a wet, dreary, miserable month; but scarcely had May come in than all this was changed. Rain passed away., and more of it fell in one day of April than in the whole of the month now making 'I its exit. These sudden changes are the despair of meteorologists, who survey their statistics, averages, and what not, and are scarcely any wiser at the end than they were at the beginning. Who would have ventured to suggest in April that we would have had such a wonderful May? Who would have thought, when everything was dripping, that in three or four weeks everyone would be complain- ing of the heat and the drought? This, being the case, what is to prevent June being as wet as April? Who will venture] a prophecy? Not the wise man, at all events. Loud and long are the complaints both in town and country as to the dusty roads. One paper feelingly says:- For miles around London the hedges bor- dering the main roads are white instead of green, and as ea-ch motor-car or motor-; bicycle phases fresh layers of dust are, added. Goggles and dust cloaks are, indeed, far more necessary to the pedestrian than i to the motorist. This is almost as true of the provinces as oi the Metropolis. We, therefore, rejoice to know that motoring authorities themselves are alive to the nuisance, and that on Saturday next the anti-dust com- mittee of the Automobile Club will carry out various experiments over a course thickly laid with dust from flour mills. Deflection plates of various sizes and shapes will be fitted under the cars in the hope that the dust will be deflected to the ground. We can only trust these experiments will be crowned with success, so that the motor folk may before long enjoy their recreation without annoyance to pedestrians and other users of the public roads. The museum question has been largely to the front lately, and in this connection we notice a capital little brochure from the able pens of Messrs. John Ward, F.S.A., and T. H. Thomas dealing with the problem at large. Reference is made to some of the smaller museums of Wales, a phase of the subject which has hitherto attracted very little attention. Every I place that aspires to be called a town; should have its reading-room and library, and it should also have its museum. But how many places are there in Wales that are absolutely destitute of all, or even any one, of these natural accom- paniments of civilisation. Caer- .philly is an instance of what we mean, and we hope the local people will yet take the bull by the horns, as Whit- church has done, and establish a library j and reading-room worthy of an old, his- j I toric, and increasingly important place. It is little good religious teachers and the like inveighing against drunkenness and immorality when no attempt is made to, provide rational attractions for the masses of the people. But when we turn to museums in par- ticular, we find that all but half-a-dozen I' Welsh towns are miserably served or not served at all. Let us take Llanelly as an instance df this. Llanelly is a town bursting with patriotism and enterprise, and, as the most important Welsh ind-us-, trial centre out of Glamorgan, a museum would be, not only of interest, but of value. Yet the town possesses the name, but not the reality. Forty years ago a I start was made in that direction, and a most valuable collection of minerals and the like was presented by the late Lord Cawdor, but the collection got hopelessly jumbled up. The specimens are still there in the so-called museum in a melancholy and disgraceful state. There are also a couple of paintings. Now, what Llanelly wants is to take this matter in hand, and mBke its museum as much a reality as its public library, the latter being (we may add) a credit to the district. We mention Llanelly merely as a type, i Every town could start a small museum, and, with a little care and interest, the iiioveTucnL wou l d grow.. movement would grow. Moreover, such small museums would be of positive use to the central national institution when it is founded, and the latter, in its turn, could, be of service to the local ones. In these days, when so much is said about Welsh education, this is a matter that ought not to be neglected. The same remarks apply to Art. Collections of local portraits, maps, and views could be made w?ta v"rV" little trouble, and would prove in time1 of inestimable value. We merely throw: out these suggestions. There are many hundreds of places in Wales which would be the better for their being practically i taken up. In this connection the little brochure compiled by Messrs. Ward and T. H.     Transporter v. Steamerl IWHAT HAPPENED IN A COLLISION The transporter bridge ovpt the River Mersey between Widnes and Runcorn was formally opened for traffic on Monday. The Newport transporter bridge was not be-gun until the end of 1902. The work is not, therefore, so far advanced as at Runcorn, but the main cables were got across a month ago, and the bridge should be open for traffic in nine months' time. The engineers are Mr. Robert H. Haynes, who is the protagonist of transporter bridge construction in this country, and M. Arnodin, who buUt both the Bilba-o and Rouen transporters, and the oon. tractor is Mr. Alfred Thorne, of West- minster. The principal dimensions of the bridge are as follow:—Span, centre to centre of towers. 645ft.; span between faces of pieTo. 592ft.; pier height to underside of stiffening girder from high-water mark, 177ft.; height of tower from level of approach road to saddle, 241ft. 6in.; distance between centres of anchorages, 1,545ft.; and centre of tower to centre of anchorage, 450ft. The found-ation works in the alluvial deposit of the lower reaches of the Usk proved, as was feared, a long and arduous undertaking. The journey on a transporter car is said to be an exhilarating experience. The one and only objection advanocd against the trans- porter bridge is that sooner or later the inevitable will happen—namely, a collision between the car and a passing vessel. In the history of these bridges, however, only one such accident is recorded. A steamer ran into the car of the Bilbao transporter, which car, be it remembered, is not under the control of an operator on board. Contrary to what might have been expected, it was the vessel which was seriously damaged, for the equilibrium of the oar was not upset, and the pa-ssengera escaped with a slight shaking.
