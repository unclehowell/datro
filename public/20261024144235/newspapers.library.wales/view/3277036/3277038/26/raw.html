    WHAT WE THINK. The Home Secretary's answer to Mr. D. A. Thomas in the Cordtnons last night can hardly be regarded as a satisfactory one. It is evi- dent. if we take the answer as embodying the news received from Mountain Ash by the Home Secretary, that the dispatches sent from the seat of war, so to speak, are watered to suit the taste of those responsible for the presence of the military in Wales. An important por- tion of the Welsh member's query went un- answered, we may presume because the answer would not bear out the necessity of the action of the authorities. Mr. D. A. Thomas plainly asked "whether there had been any riotous conduct amoiig any section of the populace at. Mountain Ash or among any of the inhabitants (50.000) of the Aberdare Valley since the coal lock-out began." The Home Secretary re- mained significantly silent on that point. Why ? He could not discover any symptoms of the probable occurrence of disturbance sufficient to warrant the presence of the military at all. And yet the soldiers are allowed to remain-so hard up for work that they create a riot amongst themselves in default of any more legitimate need for their activity. The very fact that the unwarrantable insult of military supervision has been offered to the strikers would have goaded iess peaceable men to riot and revolt. Did the authorities, under the influence of the coalowners, mean to invite such a revolt? If so, no more dastardly move has yet been chronicled in the annals of labour wars. Yet no other adequate reason for the authorities' course can even be discovered by the Home Secretary, with plenty of time and opportunity given him to frame an answer to a straightforward question. Mr. D. A. Thomas wound his query up with as neat a bit of sarcasm as we remember to have heard from his lips. Would the Home Secretary" suggest to the proper authorities the advisability of augmenting the police force during the stay of the military, so that the normal peaceful condition of the district might be maintained?" The Home Secretary might well have been exacted to say a word in answer, even to that -but the official mouth is closed. Only one recfon can be assigned—and that we have alr< ady. Might we not go a step further than Mr D. A. Thomas, and ask that the depart- mental force be strengthened during the strike to exercise proper supervision over the autho- rities responsible for piling aggravation on the colliers and expense upon the county. i

    Excise Prosecution. IMPORTANT JUDGMENT GIVEN AT CARDIFF POLICE-COURT. Shortly after the opening of the Cardiff First Police-court on Wednesday, Mr. R. E. Spencer delivered judgment in the case of Windsor Mansel Thomas, who was charged at the instance of the Inland Revenue authorities with selling beer at a shop in Albany-road in connec- tion with which he did not hold a licence, although he held a licence in respect of other premises in Woodville-road. The defence was that orders only were taken at Albany-road, but were executed at Wo odville-road. Mr. Spencer said that, considering the whole of the facts, he could draw a distinction be- tween this case and that of Rogers and Co. a few days ago. It was clear that the order was given, and also upon what terms the order was accepted by the defendant—the notice exhibited by him in the shop that he undertook to deliver the beer. The intention of the defendant was very evident in his offer to accept payment for the beer. The terms of the contract were reduced into writing in the bill. The whole transaction brought no doubt to his mind that an offence had been committed. The defendant, hower, appeared to have acted in a bona-fide manner, and seemed to have had no intention of breaking the law. The nominal penalty of 5s would be inflicted. Mr. Spencer suggested to the Excise authorities that in future prosecutions of a like character should be taken under another section of the Act, which referred to the taking of an order. There seemed to be considerable doubt as to the intention of this section. If a decision was given upon this and tested in the Court of Appeal 'ts meaning conM be Refined. Mr. Mevrick: Parties are carrying on their businesses undir very considerable doubt just now, as they arc not clear upon the section. An application was made for coats, but court fees only were allowed against defendant.

    ACCIDENTS OF THE WHEEL. Charles Pepworth. a rural postman, was on Tuesday a quarter of an hour late on his round, and in orcler to recover time rode down a dangerous hill at Cirencester on his bicycle. When turning a bridge at the foot of the hill he collided with the stonework. He was thrown from his bicycle and had his head smashed, death being instantaneous. John Clements Morris, aged 40, an artist, of Brighton-road, Croydon. was, returning on Saturday from Boxhill with three friends on bicycles, and in descending an incline was pedalling hard to gain the momentum necessary to climb another hill. when his foot slipped and he fell with great violence to the ground. He died on Monday morning from a fractured skull. At the inquest on Tuesday a verdict of "Accidental death" was returned.

    WARFARE EXTRAORDINARY In the current issue of the "Rambler" an interesting collection of suggested methods of aerial fighting in the Spanish-American war is given. Some of the ideas put forward are decidedly startling. Among the more sensa- tional forms of aerial warfare may be men- tioned the dynamite balloon, which is being considered in connection with the present war in Cuba. The balloon is to be a steerable one, and is to carry over a ton of dynamite. By these means it is calculated that Havannah could be reduced to ashes within an hour, and Spanish rule in the "Pearl of the Antilles" become a thing of the past. Professor M'Ewen, of Michigan, U.S.A., has invented a rocket for the purpose of military reconnaissance which is to startle the world. It is made of alumi- nium. is 60ft. long, and the professor proposes, as soon as he has perfected the idea, to take up excursion parties. These parties are to be taken up at the rate of 100 miles a minute! Awaiting the decision of the Patent Office at Washington is another marvellous idea for use in war time. India. December 9, 1896. Dear Mr. Kemp,-I now answer your letter, which I received by the last mail. I will give you as much information as I can as regards your son's death. I saw him for the last time about three days before he died. He seemed quite cheerful and full of hope of getting better. The doctor expected him to get over his illness then. But I was sorry to hear the next day that he had been taken worse. I didn't see him after, as I had to go about four miles from here to do my annual course of musketry, so I didn't have the chance of seeing him again. It must have been a great blow to you when yc u heard the news at home. I mus.t now bring this letter to a close, as I've told you as .much as I know at present. If there is anything else I can do for you I shall only be too pleased to do it.-I remain, your sincere friend. PRIVATE S. VICKERY.
