The office copy under the seal of the court of anoriginal will in Welshcouldnotbegiven, because probate was not granted of such original will, but of the translation which was attached to the original. It simply enacts that the same power of making a repre- sentation with respect to allotments and of applying for the ejection of allotment managers as is conferred on Parliamentary electors by the ALLOTMENTS ACTS OF 1887 AND 1890, but without prejudice to the powers of those e ors should be transferred to the parish councils. The Allotment Act of 1887 provides that any six Parliamentary electors in a parish may make a representation in writing to the sanitary authority (now the district council) that the circumstances of the parish are such that it is the duty of the sauitaiy authority to take proceed- ings, and that the sanitary authority shall take the representation into consideration and if, after inquiry, they are of the opinion that there is a demand for allotments, they shall take the necessary steps to provide them. The Allotment Act of 1890 supplements the Act of 1887. It provides that if the sanitary authority fail to acquire land for allotments, the six Par- liamentary electors are to petition the county council, which body, as a rule, appoints a stand- ing committee to deal with such matters. The departure made in the present Act is to substitute the parish councils for the six electors (without, however, depriving them of their right), and for the managers which, under the old law, were to be appointed to look after the allotments. These managers will no longer be elected, for all the powers relating to allotments which hitherto have been enjoyed by elected managers will be exercised by parish councils. That is to say, the parish councils, having thus obtained land for allotments, may improve it and adapt it for that purpose by draining, fencing, and dividing it, acquiring approaches, making roads, and doing anything else they think fit, except; that they may not break up any permanent pasture without the written assent of the land- lord. They may also from time to time do what is necessary for maintaining the drains, fences, &c., in order to keep the allotments in a proper condition. The council may also make or alter its REGULATIONS WITH RESPECT TO ALLOTMENTS from time to time, but no regulation will have any force until it has been confirmed by the Local Government Board. The regulations may, among other things, deene:- 1. The persons eligible to be tenants. 2. The notices to be given for letting the allot- ments. 3. The size of the allotments. 4. The conditions under which they are to be cultivated. 5. The rent to be piud for them. With regard, however, to the size of the allot- ments, there is one rule which cannot be altered by the parish council. If the land is hired com- pulsorily, one tenant may not hold more than four acres of pasture, or one acre of arable and three acres of pasture, and under no circumstances can an allotment be sub-let. If any allotment cannot be let according to the pro- visions enumerated above, it may be temporarily let for a period of 12 months, to any person what- ever, at the best annual rent which can be obtained for it. The RENTS are to be fixed at au amounb not less than such as may reasonably be expected to insure the parish council from loss, buc in calculating such loss, any expenses incurred in an unsuccessful attempt to acquire land for allotments are to be excluded. Where the council deem it necessary that rents should be paid in advance, they are not to require more than a quarter's rent in advance. RATES. Although the council are to be deemed the occupiers of the allotments for all the purposes of rates, taxes, and tithe-rent charge, they must, nevertheless, cause the sums so spent to be apportioned among the tenants, this sum to be added to the rent of each tenant. If a tenant has failed to pay his rent 40 days after it has fallen due, or if the council thmk that for three months he has failed to observe the conditions of his tenancy, or if ho has gone to live more than a mile away from the parish, the council may either serve at his last place of abode and fix in some conspicuous manner on the allotment a written notice determining his tenancy at the end of one month after the notice has been 60 served or affixed. BUILDINGS. No building other than a stable, cow-house, barn, tool-house, shed, greenhouse, fowl-house, or pigstye can be erected on any parb of an allot- ment, and if any other building is erected the council can pull it down and sell the material. No compensation will be paid to an outgoing tenant for any buildings he may leave on his allot- ment, but he will be at liberty to remove such buildings at the end of his tenancy. (This will not, of coarse, prevent the outgoing tenant from making a private arrange- ment with the incoming or other tenant with regard to the buildings.) The same rule applies also to fruit trees. NVith regard to improve- ments, the provisions of the Agricultural Hold- ings Act of 1883 will apply. Some improve- ments, such as the erection of buildings, etc., will require the consent of the landlord others, such as drainage, will require giving notice to the landlord and others, such as liming, etc,, can be done without the consent of the landlord. In the same way, the compensation which could be claimed by the tenant for unexhausted improve- ments under the Act of 1883 will be due to allot- ment holders under this Act.
