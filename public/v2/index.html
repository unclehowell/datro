31 July 1987 · Court of Appeal: the licence

The nature of Mrs Buckler's possession after receipt of the letters . It must depend even more, on this aspect of the case, on the position as seen from the standpoint of the person with the paper title. What could that person have done? The rule that possession is not adverse if it can be referred to a lawful title applies even if the person in possession did not know of the lawful title; the lawful title would still preclude the person with the paper title from evicting the person in possession. So far as Mrs Buckler was concerned, even though she did not 'accept' the terms of the letters, BP Properties Ltd would, in the absence of any repudiation by her of the two letters, have been bound to treat her as in possession as licensee on the terms of the letters. They could not have evicted her (if they could have done so at all) without determining the licence. I can see no escape therefore from the conclusion that, , from the time of her receipt of the letters, Mrs Buckler was in possession of the farmhouse and garden , and her possession was no longer adverse within the meaning of section 10 of the 1939 Act.

The route to reopen
  • CPR 52.30(1): “The Court of Appeal or the High Court will not reopen a final determination of any appeal unless— (a) it is necessary to do so in order to avoid real injustice; (b) the circumstances are exceptional and make it appropriate to reopen the appeal; and (c) there is no alternative effective remedy.” Civil Procedure Rules, r.52.30(1)
  • Limb (c) narrows this route. Where a fresh claim to set the judgment aside is open, CPR 52.30 does not apply. Reopen-loop test notes; counsel to confirm
  • A judgment obtained by concealment or misrepresentation, to be proved, can be set aside by a fresh claim. Takhar was such a claim. Takhar v Gracefield Developments Ltd [2019] UKSC 13
  • The test needs “conscious and deliberate dishonesty” in evidence given, a statement made or a matter concealed. Takhar [2019] UKSC 13, para 56, adopting RBS v Highland Financial Partners [2013] EWCA Civ 328, para 106
  • Materiality is judged “by reference to its impact on the evidence supporting the original decision”. Takhar, para 56
  • Where the matter was not raised at trial, no reasonable-diligence requirement applies to the party seeking to set the judgment aside. Takhar, para 54 (Lord Kerr)
  • Limitation Act 1980, s.32(1)(b): time does not run where “any fact relevant to the plaintiff's right of action has been deliberately concealed from him by the defendant”, until discovery or until it “could with reasonable diligence have discovered it”. Limitation Act 1980, s.32(1)
  • Section 32(3) protects a purchaser for value who was an innocent third party. Limitation Act 1980, s.32(3)
  • The registry facts behind R1 and R3 surfaced in HM Land Registry letters of 23 March, 25 May and 30 July 2026. HMLR letters, 2026
  • Every limb must reach 4 of 5 before any court step. No ground meets that bar yet. Highlight Report HR-02, reopen-loop section, updated 21 October 2026

Criticism after 1987
  • Law Commission and HM Land Registry, 2001. “There is one decision in the Court of Appeal that appears to hold that a landowner, X, can make a squatter, Y, his licensee unilaterally, simply by writing to Y and informing her that she is his licensee. Provided that Y does not write back to X to say that she is not his licensee, then she is: see BP Properties Ltd v Buckler (1987) 55 P & CR 337, 345 — 347. This decision has been criticised, rightly in our view: see Herbert Wallace “Limitation, Prescription and Unsolicited Permission” [1994] Conv 196. It appears to rest on the assumption (see 55 P & CR at 346), since discredited, that the paper owner’s intentions can be relevant to whether a squatter’s possession is adverse.” Law Com No 271, Land Registration for the Twenty-First Century: A Conveyancing Revolution (2001), para 14.56, footnote 187 (read in full, 21 October 2026)
  • The same footnote finds Buckler “difficult to reconcile” with Mount Carmel Investments Ltd v Peter Thurlow Ltd [1988] 1 WLR 1078. Law Com No 271, para 14.56 n 187
  • Herbert Wallace, 1994. “Limitation, Prescription and Unsolicited Permission” [1994] Conv 196–210. The Law Commission cites it as the criticism it agrees with. The article’s own text has not been read here. Cited in Law Com No 271, n 187; also in Law Express: Land Law (Pearson), as reproduced at lawexplores.com
  • Land law textbook. “The finding in BP Properties v Buckler has been criticised because it suggests that a landowner may defeat an adverse possession claim simply by unilaterally granting a licence to the occupier.” Law Express: Land Law (Pearson), “Aim Higher” note, as reproduced at lawexplores.com. Author and edition not yet checked.
  • House of Lords, 2002. “The suggestion that the sufficiency of the possession can depend on the intention not of the squatter but of the true owner is heretical and wrong.” Pye does not name Buckler. The Law Commission traces Buckler to that same owner-intention assumption. J A Pye (Oxford) Ltd v Graham [2002] UKHL 30, para 45 (Lord Browne-Wilkinson); full text checked for “Buckler”: no mention
  • O. Radley-Gardner, 2017. “Foisted permission and adverse possession” (2017) 133 LQR 214. R. Hickey, 2017. “The effect of supervening permission on adverse possession” [2017] Conv 223–230. Both discuss unilateral permission after Smith v Molyneaux. Their texts have not been read here; their positions are not yet checked. Cited in Anderson (2020) 40 Legal Studies 477, n 23; Hickey listed by Queen’s University Belfast
  • C. Anderson, 2020. “One point on which the two may differ is the effect of a unilateral permission to occupy that is given to someone currently occupying the land without any right to be there.” “Unilateral permission and prescriptive acquisition: a Scottish perspective” (2020) 40(3) Legal Studies 477–493, abstract
  • Against: the Privy Council, 2016. “This case decides that it is sufficient that permission is given unilaterally.” The Board raised Buckler itself; the parties accepted it, and the Board held that permission need not be acknowledged (paras 28–30). A Privy Council decision persuades but does not bind courts in England and Wales. Smith v Molyneaux [2016] UKPC 35, para 28
  • Simon Brown LJ assumed Buckler correct in Markfield Investments Ltd v Evans; the Law Commission notes the assumption “was, however, unnecessary for the decision in the case”. Markfield Investments Ltd v Evans [2001] 2 All ER 238, [12], as noted in Law Com No 271, n 187
