Llandough Community CouncilThursday 22 October 2026, 7.00pm

Preserving our identity.

That is the Council's own mission statement. On 22 October it meets Tŷ Mawr, the Great House of Llandough: home to one Welsh family for 320 years, demolished at four in the morning on 6 December 1988, and missing from the public record ever since.

Item 10 is too small for this.

The agenda reduces it to a plaque and a mailing list. These are the eight things to put before the Council, in priority order. Open any one for the draft resolution.

01Put it on the recordReceive the family's statement and this pack, and minute them.

Nothing about Great House is on the Council's record. This is the first step and costs nothing.

Draft resolutionThat the Council receives the statement and meeting pack of the Williams–Buckler family, records them in the minutes, and publishes them with the papers for this meeting.

02Make it a standing itemName a councillor. Bring it back every meeting until it is resolved.

This is the first meeting, not the last. Detail belongs to later meetings, in public, with papers in advance.

Draft resolutionThat “Great House Farm / Tŷ Mawr” is placed on the agenda of each ordinary meeting until the Council resolves otherwise, and that Councillor ______ acts as the Council's liaison with the family.

03Correct the Council's own historyThe village history page says 800 burials and nothing about Great House.

The page gives “over 800 burials” (the published report counts 1,026), dates the Roman villa to the 1990s (it was excavated in 1978–79), and does not mention Great House, the Williams family or the demolition.

Draft resolutionThat the Council's published village history is revised, with heritage advice and the family's input, to record Great House, the manorial courts held there, its demolition in 1988, and the 1,026 burials published in the excavation report.

04Mark it on the groundA board or memorial near the green: monastery, the 1,026 dead, the manor, Marconi, Peerless Jim.

There is nothing on the ground that records any of it. The family has offered a free talk to the Council or residents.

Draft resolutionThat a working group is set up to scope an interpretation board or memorial near the green, covering St Dochdwy's monastery, the 1,026 early medieval burials, Great House and the manor, the 1897 wireless trials and Peerless Jim Driscoll, with wording checked against sources and agreed with the family; and to explore a Llandough companion to the Marconi artwork on the Cardiff Barrage.

05Find the witnessesInvite people who remember the village from the 1950s to the 1990s to come forward.

This is the request already listed as Item 10(a). Forwarding an invitation discloses nobody's data.

Draft resolutionThat the Council forwards the family's invitation to former members, former clerks and long-standing residents, leaving it to them whether to make contact; no personal details to be disclosed without consent.

06Open the Council's own recordsAn independent review, separate answers, and a hold on the files.

The refusal priced the request at 224 sets of minutes and gave figures that do not reconcile. Land and heritage records can fall under the Environmental Information Regulations, which have no automatic cost cap.

Draft resolutionThat the internal review of the 11 October refusal is conducted by a person other than the officer who made it; that the environmental-information request and the subject access request of 12 October are each answered under their own law and time limit; and that all Council records concerning Great House, Church View Close and village green VG41 are preserved.

07Speak up for disclosureWrite to the bodies holding the records, and note the call for an inquiry.

A letter from the community's own council carries weight no family letter can. It asks for openness, not a verdict.

Draft resolutionThat the Clerk writes on the Council's behalf to Cadw, the Vale of Glamorgan Council, HM Land Registry, Amgueddfa Cymru and the National Library of Wales, and to the Ministry of Justice about its outstanding reply to Stephen Doughty MP, copied to Mr Doughty and the area's Members of the Senedd, supporting full and prompt disclosure to the family and the preservation of all records meanwhile; and that the Council notes the family's call for an independent public inquiry.

08Carry it into tonight's other businessItems 12 and 14 are about identity and culture. Tŷ Mawr belongs in both.

The agenda already asks the Council to think about Welsh culture, sport and local identity. Here is Llandough's strongest example.

Draft resolutionThat Tŷ Mawr's heritage, including Peerless Jim Driscoll and the 1897 wireless trials, is considered in the Council's response to the Welsh Government call for evidence on culture and sport (Item 14, closing 18 December 2026) and in the heritage and identity of the proposed Llandough and Cwrtyrala Community Council (Item 12).

Not asked: for the Council to decide ownership, fraud or whether any court was right. Those belong to courts and regulators. The Council is asked to put the matter on record and use its own powers.

What the agenda leaves out.

Eight things the published agenda does not mention. Each is a headline. Open it for the record.

MonasteryNever formally found.St Dochdwy's monastery is attested from the 7th century. Its site has never been located.Read more

Heneb's record says the exact location of the monastic centre has not been established. The Council's own page says Llandough was “almost certainly” its site. The ground beside the church where Great House stood was demolished in 1988 before its fabric could be fully examined; medieval pottery had been found on the slope beside the house in 1962–63.

On record
1,026The dead are in storage.The largest early medieval burial population recovered in Wales, excavated in 1994.Read more

Excavated March–July 1994 by the Cotswold Archaeological Trust ahead of the houses. In 1990 the area had been marked “low potential”. The Home Office licence lapsed for part of the dig. On 28 July 1994 the developer, as landowner, gave the remains and finds to the National Museum of Wales. Nothing on the ground marks them.

The manorThe courts sat here.The manorial courts of Llandough and Leckwith were held at Great House.Read more

Recorded in the official Historic Environment Record (GGAT02038s), which also notes building details suggesting an older core, a 1974 survey limited by the ownership dispute, and demolition on 6 December 1988 amid considerable local controversy.

The trials of May 1897 are documented by Amgueddfa Cymru. Family tradition records that Thomas Williams of Great House carted the equipment, and that the house lodged visitors connected with the work. That family part is still to be corroborated, which is a reason to research it, not to leave it out.

On record

The story they told.

Contemporary coverage framed the dispute as an eviction. These articles show what was reported at the time; they do not prove every claim in print.

  • Squatters.Born, married and died in the house for 320 years. Family account
  • An eviction.A demolition at 4am, the morning after the last protection fell. On record
  • A farmer's feud.Against a landlord the Commons heard called “the most rapacious” in South Wales, by then owned by the BP pension fund. On record
  • An old farmhouse.The house where the manorial courts sat. On record
  • Nobody cared.About a thousand signed in 1974. The village protested in 1988. On record
  • The courts settled it.They settled who could stay. No court ever ruled on who owned the house. Family account
The judgments—and the title questions still being tested.What the courts decided, what the records show, and what still needs checking.Read more
  • The courts decided possession. BP never asked a court to declare it owned the house, and the 1974 action in which Mary Williams pleaded ownership was adjourned and never relisted.
  • The possession clock was started in 1955, from an order about the fields. The family's case is that the house was a separate parcel and theirs from 1928.
  • Mary Williams's statement records the family papers being taken from the house in 1950–52. They were never produced in court.
  • BP registered the farmhouse as a separate title in 1983, then merged it into the fields title in February 1987, while the appeal was pending.
  • No deed conveying the house to Western Ground Rents or BP, earlier than a 1975 conveyance between two BP companies, has been produced.
  • HM Land Registry's schedule of 43 first-registration documents does not include the 1969 conveyance on which the title rests.

The High Court (1986), Court of Appeal (1987), House of Lords (1988) and European Commission of Human Rights (1989) all ruled against the family. These are the family's grounds for saying the outcome was unfair and wrong. Testing them is for the courts, the Land Registry and regulators. This Council is not asked to decide them.

Family account

The record: facts and open questions.

Research has only started. Nothing yet meets the legal threshold to reopen the case on fraud, concealment or government involvement, and the family does not claim it does. The records that would test those questions are the ones not yet disclosed. What is already on the public record is enough to show the official story is wrong.

01The root deed is not on file.The Land Registry's own list of 43 registration documents leaves out the deed BP's title rests on.

HM Land Registry's schedule of 30 July 2026 lists 43 documents on the 1982–83 first-registration files. The 31 December 1969 conveyance, recited as the root of BP's title, and a 1971 abstract are not among them. 02Two titles, merged during the appeal.BP registered the farmhouse as a separate title in 1983, then folded it into the fields title in 1987.

Title WA240304 (farmhouse and garden) was applied for on 23 February 1983, four weeks before Mary Williams died. On 2 February 1987, with the appeal pending, it was amalgamated into WA231076. The case was argued as one farm while the register held two.

On record Wiki: Anomalies A-025, A-026

03Registered over a woman living in the house.Mary Williams was in occupation in 1982. Nothing on the file shows she was asked what she claimed.

Linklaters & Paines applied to register BP on 30 November 1982. On 23 February 1983 BP's solicitors applied for the farmhouse title, certifying they knew of no question or doubt affecting it. Mary had pleaded ownership in 1974 and told the Daily Telegraph her solicitors said she had a valid claim. BP's 1974 action was adjourned and never relisted. The family's account is that notice of registration reached them only after her death; the original notice is still being sought.

Family account Wiki: Anomalies A-011, A-012; M-038

04Cadw kept no file.Two photographs survive. No notes, no decision record, no decision-maker, no record of destruction.

Cadw photographed the house on 29 July 1988 and coded it “YYY”, which it has explained two different ways. It refused to list on 5 December 1988. It says its file was “quite likely”, then “most likely”, destroyed, and holds no destruction record. The Wales Office searched only its digital records and says paper files “would have been” transferred.

On record Wiki: Anomalies A-037–A-041; M-018–M-020, M-052

05The plan said keep it.The Local Plan said the buildings should be retained. They were demolished before the application was decided.

The officer's report on BP's 1989 outline application records that the buildings the Local Plan said should be retained had been demolished. GGAT's 1989 report had described the farmhouse as a “county treasure”. Permission was granted on 13 March 1990. No environmental screening decision has been found for a 3.4-acre site above the threshold.

06The pages that are missing.Appendices B–E of the planning report are gone, including a letter from a councillor who sat on GGAT.

The officer's report says further GGAT comments, two public representations and BP's agents' response were attached as Appendices B–E. None is in the file disclosed in 2026. One representation came from a County Councillor described as a member of GGAT. The Vale's internal review has been unanswered since July.

07Warned in 1982. Called “low potential” in 1990.Burials were then found in that ground, some a few centimetres below the topsoil.

In October 1982 GGAT told the planners occupation might continue north of the church and asked for investigation before development. In 1989 GGAT advised, specified and priced the assessment, then took BP's commission to do it. Its 1990 trenches could not investigate the eastern ground but zoned it low potential. The 1994 excavation found the burials there. GGAT disclosed its dual role; the question is independence, not secrecy.

08Never properly recorded.The Royal Commission says the house was only partly recorded because of the ownership dispute.

RCAHMW records its correspondence with Mrs Buckler as lost since 1986–87, holds no record of being told of the demolition, and made additions to its plan on 12 December 1988, six days after the house came down.

09The Lords papers were there all along.Earlier searches missed them. The National Archives found them on 23 September 2026.

The House of Lords judicial papers in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler (YHL/PO/JO/10/11/2536) were catalogued in May 2026 and identified on 23 September after earlier keyword searches failed. The Strasbourg case file was destroyed in 2000.

10Five bailiffs, fifteen police, a chainsaw.Two evictions in 1988. The second put William Buckler in hospital and then in the dock.

On 29 April 1988 bailiffs broke the doors with poleaxes while his children were behind them. In November he was injured, taken to Llandough Hospital, and charged from his hospital bed. The house came down from 4am on 6 December. The family's papers, photographs and Mary's journal were lost.

Three rights, not one.Freedom of information, environmental information and your own data are separate laws.Read more
FOIFreedom of Information Act 2000

General recorded information. Twenty working days. A council may refuse if compliance would cost more than £450 (18 hours at £25), but must estimate reasonably and offer advice to narrow the request.

EIREnvironmental Information Regulations 2004

Information on land, development and the historic environment. Twenty working days. No automatic cost cap: refusal only if “manifestly unreasonable”, and then only if the public interest favours it.

SARUK GDPR, Article 15

Information about the person asking. One month, free. Covers what the Council holds about the living family members; it does not cover records about people who have died.

The three-minute statementRead it

Chair, councillors, thank you.

My grandmother was Mary Williams of Great House Farm, Tŷ Mawr, beside St Dochdwy's Church. Our family account is that we lived in that house for 320 consecutive years, from 1667 until it was demolished at about four in the morning on 6 December 1988.

Thank you for listing it. Item 10 describes a plaque and a mailing list. I want you to see the size of what sits under Church View Close.

It is the likely ground of St Dochdwy's monastery, one of the great early Christian centres of Wales, whose site has never been formally located. It is where 1,026 people were buried in the early medieval period, the largest such population yet recovered in Wales; their remains are in museum storage. It is the house where, according to the official Historic Environment Record, the manorial courts of Llandough and Leckwith were held. Our family tradition ties it to Marconi's 1897 wireless trials at Lavernock, and published sources record Peerless Jim Driscoll's first fight in its barn.

None of that is on the Council's own history page. Great House is not mentioned at all.

In 1988 the story was told as the eviction of squatters. We were not squatters. The courts decided who could live in the house. No court has ever decided who owned it. The house came down the morning after Cadw declined to list it, while our application to the European Commission of Human Rights was still pending.

Our research has only started, and nothing yet meets the legal threshold to reopen the case. That is because the records are still being kept from us. For eighteen months we have asked more than twenty organisations. The Ministry of Justice has not answered our MP. The Ministry of Defence will neither confirm nor deny. Cadw says its 1988 file was probably destroyed but holds no record of destroying it. The Land Registry's own list of 43 documents leaves out the deed its title rests on. This Council refused our request on cost, with figures that do not add up.

What is already public is enough to show the official story is wrong. BP registered the farmhouse as a separate title, then merged it into the fields while the appeal was running. The Local Plan said the buildings should be kept. Ground marked low potential in 1990 turned out to hold burials.

I am not asking this Council to try the case. I am asking you to do three things. Put our statement and pack on the public record. Make Great House Farm a standing item, with a named councillor. And write to the bodies holding the records, asking them to disclose them fully. The detail can follow, meeting by meeting, in the open.

Your mission is “Serving our community, preserving our identity”. Tŷ Mawr is part of that identity. For thirty-eight years it has had no voice. Tonight, please give it one.

Thank you.
