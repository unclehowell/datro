Rights of access are not one single process

FOI, EIR and personal data

Some requests concern general recorded information and fall under FOIA; environmental information may fall under the Environmental Information Regulations 2004; personal data about the living requester is a separate subject-access matter under the UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018. The ICO says a mixed request should be handled as separate access tracks. This is not a claim that GDPR gives a right to every record, or an automatic right to a deceased relative’s data. Prepared statement to read aloud

Designed for Agenda item 4, Public Participation, in relation to item 10. Approximately 6–7 minutes at a measured pace. Ask the Chair before beginning and follow any reasonable time limit.

Open plain-text script

Opening

Chair, councillors, thank you for allowing me to speak about agenda item 10. I am grateful that my request has finally reached a Council meeting. I want to explain why this is more than a request for a plaque. It is about Llandough’s history, my family’s place in it, and a documentary record that we have spent around eighteen months trying to establish with public bodies.

My family is the Williams–Buckler family of Great House Farm, also known as Tŷ Mawr, beside St Dochdwy’s Church. Our family account is that we occupied the house continuously from 1667 until it was demolished in December 1988: 320 consecutive years. I recognise that the exact early genealogy and the title history still need documentary testing. I am not asking this Council to decide ownership. I am asking that our history is heard and treated seriously instead of being reduced to a headline.

The house and the story that was lost

Great House was not an interchangeable modern farmhouse. The official Historic Environment Record describes an historic site beside the parish church, notes that the manorial courts for Llandough and Leckwith were held at Great House, and records building details suggesting that the core may have been medieval. The same record says the house and farm buildings were completely demolished by BP Properties on 6 December 1988, amid considerable local controversy. The building was demolished before its full fabric could be properly examined; the record also says the 1974 survey was incomplete because access was affected by the ownership dispute.

That history barely exists on the ground today. Some media coverage presented the family through an eviction or “squatters” narrative. Our family disputes that characterisation as misleading because it strips away the long occupation, the contested ownership history and the unresolved questions surrounding the records. I ask the Council not to repeat that label as if it settled the history or the law.

Marconi and Llandough’s wider heritage

There is also a story connecting the house to Marconi’s 1897 wireless experiments between Lavernock Point and Flat Holm. The experiments are well documented by Museum Wales and other historical sources. The family’s more specific account—that Marconi stayed at Tŷ Mawr and that our family had a direct connection to the work—is not yet proved by contemporary evidence in the public archive. I want that story researched properly, not exaggerated and not forgotten.

The archaeological story is equally important. The published excavation report describes 1,026 burials in an early-medieval cemetery, beside the remains of a Roman villa and evidence of an Iron Age feature. Heneb’s current record says the Llandough monastery’s precise location has not been established with certainty. It also records postholes for a possible medieval timber building, but not a conclusively identified monastery complex. There is a genuine historical question here: the cemetery is real, the monastic centre is supported by historical and archaeological evidence, and the exact location of the monastery remains unresolved. That is a reason for careful interpretation and further study.

Why we are asking for help now

Since 2025, we have asked multiple public bodies for the records needed to understand the land history, the court proceedings, land registration, planning, heritage assessments and the 1988 demolition. The responses have often involved redirects, gaps in searches, refusals or requests to narrow what we ask for. We consider the overall pattern obstructive and resistant. I understand that public bodies must apply lawful exemptions, cost limits, retention rules and data-protection safeguards. I am not asking councillors to assume bad faith. I am asking that the correspondence is examined as a whole and that access to our own personal data is not confused with the separate public rights to non-personal or environmental information.

The distinction matters. FOI is not a universal answer to every records request. Environmental information may be governed by the Environmental Information Regulations. Requests for my own personal data are subject to the relevant access rights under data-protection law. The Information Commissioner’s Office says a mixed request should be separated and handled under the appropriate regimes. Those routes have to be followed correctly, with clear reasons for any refusal and a meaningful explanation of what was searched.

What I ask the Council to do

First, please help us reach former councillors, former clerks and long-standing residents who remember Llandough from the 1950s to the 1990s. Please do this by passing on our contact details only where lawful and appropriate, or by forwarding an invitation so that residents can choose for themselves whether to contact us.

Second, please consider an interpretation board or plaque near the green, with wording prepared against reliable sources. It should acknowledge the demolished farmhouse and its manorial context, the 1897 Marconi story with its evidence status clearly marked, and the Roman, Iron Age and early-medieval archaeological landscape. It should also explain that the monastery’s exact footprint remains uncertain. This is Llandough’s heritage as well as my family’s.

Third, please appoint a councillor or other contact to help us develop the heritage proposal, and ask the relevant heritage bodies what a responsible interpretation could say. Fourth, please place a properly described follow-up item on a future agenda about Great House Farm / Tŷ Mawr, the community’s historical record and the unresolved documentary questions. Please include this statement with the meeting record, and record the actions, the responsible person and a date for follow-up.

I would be glad to give a short, no-fee talk to the Council or residents. I am not asking you to find that fraud occurred, that a public authority acted unlawfully, or that a court judgment must be reversed. Those are matters for evidence, review and the proper legal bodies. I am asking for fair scrutiny, a route to witnesses, and recognition that this was a real family home with a long history—not just a property dispute and not just a demolition.

Our village does not yet know the full story of who we are. Please help us put the evidence, the family’s account and Llandough’s heritage back into the public record.

Thank you.

Family statement for circulation and the minutes

This is the fuller statement to send to the clerk before the meeting. It is intentionally personal, while distinguishing family testimony from the independent record.

Statement of the Williams–Buckler family · 22 October 2026

Great House Farm / Tŷ Mawr: a home, a family and a missing public history

We are the Williams–Buckler family associated with Great House Farm, known to our family as Tŷ Mawr, beside St Dochdwy’s Church in Llandough-juxta-Penarth. Our family history is that we occupied the house continuously from 1667 until it was demolished in December 1988. We describe that as 320 consecutive years. The earliest part of this chronology remains a family claim that we are testing against estate, parish, land and family records; we state it openly as our family history, not as a conclusion already established by a court or a complete chain of surviving documents.

For our family, the house was not merely an address or an asset. It was home across generations. Our elders maintained their claim to the house and land during their lifetimes. That claim did not disappear when they died. We continue to seek a reliable account of the documentary history, the division of the land, the legal proceedings, the registration records and the circumstances that ended in demolition.

The surviving heritage record confirms that Great House stood on an historic site next to the parish church; it records that the manorial courts for Llandough and Leckwith were held there, and that internal features suggested a possible medieval core. The house was surveyed in 1974, but the survey was incomplete because access was affected by the ownership dispute. Heneb’s Historic Environment Record says the house and farm buildings were suddenly and completely demolished by BP Properties on 6 December 1988, amid considerable local controversy. This is not a building whose full physical history can now be inspected: demolition removed the evidence that future generations might otherwise have examined.

The public narrative has not, in our view, told this whole story. We believe that some media coverage reduced us to an eviction story and used or repeated the “squatters” framing. We dispute that description as a fair summary of a longstanding family occupation and a contested ownership dispute. We ask that it is not treated as a settled historical or legal conclusion without reading the judgment, the underlying title documents and the family account alongside one another. The Council need not decide who owned the property in order to recognise that the family’s experience and claim deserve accurate description.

Our family account also connects Tŷ Mawr with Marconi’s wireless experiments in 1897 between Lavernock Point and Flat Holm. Those experiments are independently established and form part of Wales’s communications history. The more specific family tradition—that Marconi stayed or slept at the house and that the Williams family had a direct role or connection—still needs contemporary corroboration. We do not ask the Council to repeat it as proven fact. We ask the Council to recognise it as a serious historical lead worthy of checking against household papers, local recollections, contemporary press, Post Office records and museum or archive material.

The land is also associated with an archaeological landscape of national significance. The published account of the 1994 excavation describes an early-medieval cemetery of 1,026 burials, a Roman villa nearby and material evidence that may relate to an Iron Age settlement. Heneb’s record states that historical sources place an important monastic centre at Llandough by at least the mid-7th century, but that the monastery’s precise location has not been fixed with certainty. Postholes for a possible medieval timber building were identified, although the surviving ground plan was incomplete and its function could not be determined. These distinctions matter: the cemetery and archaeological finds are evidence; the monastery is historically attested but not precisely located; and further work is needed to explain the relationships between the house, church, cemetery and wider settlement.

For around eighteen months, we have pursued documentary disclosure from public bodies about the property history, court records, land registration, planning, heritage consideration and the demolition. Some replies have redirected us elsewhere; some have disclosed only part of the requested record; some have said records could not be located; others have invoked cost limits or asked that requests be narrowed. We consider the combined pattern obstructive and resistant. That is our assessment of the correspondence, not a substitute for a regulator’s finding. We are maintaining a chronology of requests, replies, internal reviews and complaints so that each institution’s actions can be checked against the actual record.

We also want the legal routes kept distinct. General information may be obtainable under the Freedom of Information Act 2000. Information about land, development, buildings or archaeological/environmental matters may fall under the Environmental Information Regulations 2004. Personal data about a living requester may be accessible under the UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018. A mixed request may need to be split and answered under more than one regime. We do not claim data-protection legislation automatically entitles us to all records about deceased relatives; we ask that our own personal data and the separate request for public or environmental information each receive the correct consideration.

We ask Llandough Community Council for modest, practical help. Please create a voluntary path to former councillors, former clerks and residents with memories from the 1950s to the 1990s. Please consider a heritage interpretation board or plaque, developed with relevant heritage professionals and the landowner, whose text identifies what is known, what comes from family testimony and what remains open. Please nominate a Council contact, consider a short talk we would give without charge, and place a properly described follow-up item on a future agenda so that the wider historical and records questions can be discussed without being forced into an item that currently lists only two requests.

We are not asking this Council to determine land title, declare that fraud occurred, criticise any individual as a proven wrongdoer or promise a legal remedy. Those questions require evidence and the appropriate legal or regulatory process. We are asking for recognition, fair process, local memory and a public record that does not erase the people who lived there.

Our family’s position is straightforward: Tŷ Mawr was our home, its loss remains disputed in our family’s history, and Llandough deserves a complete and carefully evidenced account of the place. We ask the Council to help us recover that account for the community.

Signed: Sion Buckler (Ret’d Sergeant), on behalf of the Williams–Buckler family.

Access-to-records concerns: the right route for each question

This is the legal framing for the meeting—not a finding that a particular authority has acted unlawfully. Each request and response must be assessed on its wording, dates, scope and records.

Route A

Freedom of Information Act 2000

For recorded information held by a public authority that is not environmental information or the requester’s own personal data. It can include meeting minutes, general correspondence, policies, internal decision records and other institutional records.

A section 12 cost-limit refusal is possible where the authority reasonably estimates that complying with the relevant duties would exceed the applicable limit. For a community council the appropriate limit is generally £450 at £25/hour (18 hours), but the estimate must be supported and the duty to advise and assist still matters. Ask for the calculation and whether a narrower search can be done.

ICO: FOI and EIR guidance

Route B

Environmental Information Regulations 2004

Potentially relevant to information about land, buildings, development, planning, environmental condition, archaeological heritage and measures affecting the environment. A request does not need to name the EIR for the duty to arise.

Do not automatically copy FOIA section 12 reasoning across to EIR. EIR has its own refusal provisions, including “manifestly unreasonable”; most exceptions require consideration of the public interest. Whether specific material is environmental information is a question of scope and content.

ICO: what counts as EIR · Receiving a request

Route C

UK GDPR / Data Protection Act

A subject-access request can be valid without saying “SAR” or quoting a law, where it is clear the person is seeking their own personal data. A mixed request should be split: the person’s own data under data-protection law and the remaining non-personal or environmental records under the relevant access law.

Article 15 access is not a general entitlement to all material about the family or to the personal data of deceased relatives. Personal data about the requester can appear in correspondence, case notes, referrals, search logs and decisions, subject to applicable rights and exemptions. Some processing may be subject to different Data Protection Act provisions, including law-enforcement processing.

ICO: recognising a SAR (updated 7 April 2026) · ICO: right of access

Route D

Review, complaint and escalation

Where records are withheld, missing or a refusal seems inadequately explained, the sequence normally begins with the authority’s internal review or complaint process, then the relevant Information Commissioner route for FOI/EIR/data protection, where applicable. Land-registration, court-record and heritage disputes may have their own distinct procedures. Preserve each response and its date.

A public meeting should not be used as a substitute for filing a statutory review or meeting a court time limit. The Council can help with a neutral referral or local-history request but cannot adjudicate a disclosure complaint against another body.

ICO: make a complaint · Welsh statutory governance guidance

The family reconstruction treats the 1877 Bute–Daniel Thomas division as the key to distinguishing the house/building parcel from the eastern agricultural parcel. The underlying deed and its legal effect require original-document verification.

The archived reconstruction records repeated attempts to characterise the occupation as permissive and later possession proceedings. The HER records an incomplete survey of the house, with access problems associated with the ownership dispute. The family points to a public petition for preservation and local interest; the number of signatories and original petition should be cited only from the original record.

The family’s research raises questions about first registration, later amalgamation of title lines and the scope of the BP Properties Ltd v Buckler litigation. It is the family’s contention that root title was not adjudicated in the way later descriptions imply. This is a legal issue for specialist advice, not a conclusion the Council should make.

The family chronology says possession was obtained in late November and the farmhouse demolished on 5–6 December. One rule for every claim

The family’s strongest position is audacious about the importance of the case and exact about the evidence status.

Independent record

Say what the source records

Use the HER wording on the site, manorial-court context, incomplete survey and demolition; cite the published archaeological report for the cemetery and the museum source for Marconi’s local trials.

Family account / contention

Name whose account it is

“Our family’s account is…”, “we contend…”, and “the archive currently indicates…” allow forceful advocacy without presenting an unresolved claim as a judicial finding.

Open question

Ask for a specific next step

Identify the missing document, custodian, response date or expert view. A focused request for a search or explanation is more likely to produce an auditable answer than a general accusation.
