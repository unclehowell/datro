02 / Narrative justice

We were not
a headline.

We were a family. Tŷ Mawr was a home. Llandough’s history is bigger than the property dispute used to describe it.

What we mean by a boundary against erasure

The family sees this as more than mourning a demolished home. It is a question of Welsh cultural dignity and the relationship between local communities, land administration, corporate power and public institutions. The moral contrast we want acknowledged is between belonging, peace and transparency on one side, and secrecy, coercive power and erasure on the other. That is our political and moral interpretation; it is not a finding that every institution acted together or that fraud has been proved.

The family says the 1988 eviction-focused narrative omitted essential context: its long account of occupation, a contested ownership history, the house’s historic setting and the archaeological story. A newspaper headline establishes what was printed; it does not establish that every claim in the report was true.

03 / The history to bring into view

Five stories.
One place.

Every card has a short top-level summary. Open only the evidence detail you need.

The home

Tŷ Mawr / Great House Farm

320 years of occupation in the family account. A historic manorial site. Demolished in 1988.

Read the evidence

Heneb’s Historic Environment Record says the farmhouse and barn formed a historic group beside the parish church, identifies the manorial courts for Llandough and Leckwith as having been held at Great House, records architectural details suggesting an older core, and notes that the house’s fabric could not be fully examined before demolition. The record dates demolition to 6 December 1988 amid local controversy.

Open Heneb HER GGAT02038s ↗

The continuous occupation from 1667 to 1988 is the family’s account, which remains under documentary investigation. It should be stated as family history, not as a finding already reached by a court.

The sacred landscape

1,026 burials. One open question.

A major early-medieval cemetery, a Roman villa nearby, Iron Age evidence—and the monastery’s exact location still uncertain.

Read the evidence

Holbrook and Thomas’s published excavation report describes 1,026 inhumation burials, the largest early-medieval burial population then recovered in Wales. Heneb records the monastic centre as historically attested from at least the mid-7th century, but says its exact location has not been fixed with certainty. A possible medieval timber building is not a conclusively identified monastery.

The family interprets the surrounding landscape as an ancient Christian place whose wider story was lost in redevelopment. Keep the distinction clear: the cemetery is excavated; the monastery is historically attested; its precise footprint remains unresolved.

Communications history

Marconi: 1897

Wales was part of wireless history. The Tŷ Mawr connection is a family lead still to be corroborated.

Read the evidence

Museum Wales records the General Post Office trials of Marconi’s wireless apparatus in May 1897, linking Lavernock Point, Flat Holm and Brean Down. The family’s more specific claim—that Marconi stayed at Tŷ Mawr or that the Williams family had a direct connection—is not independently proved in the public evidence currently identified. It belongs in the research agenda, not as an established fact and not as a story to omit.

A village connection

Peerless Jim Driscoll

The Welsh boxing legend’s first fight is attributed to a barn at Great House Farm, Llandough.

Read the evidence

A secondary biographical account attributes Driscoll’s first fight to a barn at Great House Farm and cites Peter Stead and Gareth Williams (eds.), Wales and its Boxers: The Fighting Tradition (University of Wales Press, 2008), p. 20. The book’s existence and subject are independently discoverable; the underlying National Monuments Record reference should be obtained and checked directly before this is stated as settled fact on a plaque.

This is a strong local-history lead: a celebrated Welsh boxer, his first fight reportedly in Llandough, and a story that could form part of the community interpretation.

A proposal

Radio Flatholm — why not Llandough?

Should Llandough have a companion artwork—or a future conversation about the current sculpture’s siting?

Read the idea

Radio Flatholm is a lottery-funded artwork commemorating the 1897 wireless story, made from recycled railway sleepers and located on the Cardiff Barrage. The designer’s record says it was unveiled in 2024. Llandough is part of the same historical landscape. The immediate request is feasibility and consultation—not a presumption that an existing artwork will be moved. Options include a companion work, an interpretation marker, a community-designed piece, or a future dialogue with the artist, commissioning/funding body, landowner and community about possible siting.

04 / The unresolved record

Question hard.
Claim carefully.

Strong scrutiny needs clear labels. These are the lines to keep separate at this first meeting.

Independent record

HER description and demolition date; published excavation report; Museum Wales account of Marconi’s trials; published court judgment and archived press items.

Family account / case

320 years of continuous occupation; eviction-only portrayal incomplete; concerns about the title chain, registration and how evidence was treated.

Not yet established

Fraud, concealment or coordinated government involvement; whether any registration was legally mistaken; the precise monastery footprint; Marconi’s specific Tŷ Mawr connection.

Land / court / registration

The file is not closed for the family.

The family says earlier appeals and the ECHR route did not provide a remedy, while newly recovered material raises procedural and evidential questions worth testing.

What can responsibly be said

The 1987 Court of Appeal judgment addressed adverse possession of the farmhouse and garden and the effect of 1974 licence letters. The family contends that the registration/title history and scope of what was decided warrant specialist review. The family also reports that correspondence with its MP has not resulted in an acknowledgement of error from Land Registry. Its working concern is whether later registration may have represented distinct parcels as a synthetic whole over earlier claimed rights. This remains an unresolved legal/evidence question, not a proven finding of fraud or invalid registration.

The European Commission of Human Rights declared the 1988 application inadmissible in April 1989. That outcome did not provide the family a remedy; it should not be misdescribed as a modern re-examination of records discovered decades later. Any attempt to reopen a case or pursue a human-rights remedy requires specialist advice on current time limits, admissibility and new-evidence thresholds.

18 months / public bodies

Access is not one single law.

The family describes redirects, incomplete searches, refusals and narrowing requests across several institutions. Its assessment is that the cumulative effect has been obstructive.

FOI · EIR · personal data

FOIA covers general recorded information; EIR can apply to environmental information, including land, development and archaeology; UK GDPR / Data Protection Act access concerns the requester’s own personal data. Mixed requests should be separated and handled under the correct regime. This is not a blanket entitlement to every record or all personal data about deceased relatives.

The ICO says the usual FOIA limit for most public authorities is £450 at £25/hour, and provides separate guidance for mixed requests and environmental information. Each refusal should be assessed against its actual wording, search, estimate and review route.

05 / Say this first

A short script.
No improvisation needed.

Lead with the place, the public record, and what the Council can do. Keep technical title issues for the later meetings and evidence review.

“This is not only about mourning a demolished home. It is about narrative justice and Welsh cultural dignity: whose history is kept, whose evidence is heard, and whether a family can challenge the combined power of law, institutions and corporate interests.”

Open the full read-aloud statement

Chair and councillors, thank you. I am asking you to see Item 10 as more than a plaque. It concerns Llandough’s public history, my family’s place within it, and records we have pursued for around eighteen months.

Our family account is that we occupied Great House Farm / Tŷ Mawr continuously from 1667 until its demolition in December 1988: 320 years. The early genealogy and complete title history still need documentary testing. I am not asking the Council to decide ownership; I am asking that our history is heard rather than reduced to a headline.

The official Historic Environment Record says the manorial courts for Llandough and Leckwith were held at Great House, that the survey was incomplete before demolition, and that the house was demolished on 6 December 1988 amid local controversy. The published excavation report describes 1,026 early-medieval burials, a nearby Roman villa and wider Iron Age evidence. The monastic centre is historically attested, but its exact location remains uncertain.

Two further local histories deserve checking. Museum Wales documents Marconi’s 1897 wireless trials between Lavernock Point and Flat Holm; our more specific Tŷ Mawr connection remains family tradition awaiting corroboration. A published secondary account, citing Stead and Williams’s 2008 book, attributes Peerless Jim Driscoll’s first fight to a barn at Great House Farm. The underlying National Monuments Record should be checked directly.

The family disputes the fairness and completeness of the eviction-focused media narrative. Headlines prove what newspapers printed, not that every statement was true. Our family also questions the title and registration history, the legal route that ended in demolition, and whether evidence and procedural issues deserve fresh examination. Earlier appeals and the 1988–89 ECHR route did not provide a remedy. Our working concern about synthetic parcels or registration error is not a finding of fraud, and this Council is not being asked to decide it.

For around eighteen months we have pursued records from several public bodies. We consider the overall experience obstructive, but that is our assessment rather than a regulator’s finding. FOI, environmental-information rights and access to our own personal data are separate legal routes and should not be conflated.

Please circulate this statement, help us invite former councillors and residents to contact us voluntarily, explore a sourced board or plaque, and appoint a Council contact with a follow-up date. Please also consider a Llandough companion to the Radio Flatholm artwork, or a future discussion of its siting. That is a proposal for consultation, not an immediate demand to relocate it.

This is not only about mourning a demolished home. It is about narrative justice and Welsh cultural dignity: whose history is kept, whose evidence is heard, and whether a family can challenge the combined power of law, institutions and corporate interests. We ask Llandough to begin a clear public record of what is known, what our family says, what remains disputed, and what happens next. Thank you.
