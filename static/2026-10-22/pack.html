3 Three rules at the microphone

  1. Name the source. Say where a fact comes from: the court, the heritage record, the newspaper, the archive.
  2. Say whose account it is. “Our family account is…” is accurate, and accuracy is hard to dismiss.
  3. Ask for a decision and a date. Every ask ends with who does it and when it comes back.

4 Statement to the Council

About three minutes. For the public participation session (Item 4), and for the record.

Chair, councillors, thank you.

My grandmother was Mary Williams of Great House Farm, Tŷ Mawr, beside St Dochdwy's Church. Our family account is that we lived in that house for 320 consecutive years, from 1667 until it was demolished at about four in the morning on 6 December 1988.

I am grateful this is on the agenda. But Item 10 describes it as a plaque and a mailing list, and I want you to see the size of what sits under Church View Close.

It is the likely ground of St Dochdwy's monastery, one of the great early Christian centres of Wales, whose site has never been formally located. It is where 1,026 people were buried in the early medieval period, the largest such population yet recovered in Wales; their remains are in museum storage. It is the house where, according to the official Historic Environment Record, the manorial courts of Llandough and Leckwith were held. Our family tradition ties it to Marconi's 1897 wireless trials at Lavernock, and published sources record Peerless Jim Driscoll's first fight in its barn.

None of that is on the Council's own history page. Great House is not mentioned at all.

In 1988 the story was told as the eviction of squatters. We were not squatters. The courts decided who could live in the house. No court has ever decided who owned it. The house came down the morning after Cadw declined to list it, while our application to the European Commission of Human Rights was still pending.

For eighteen months my family has asked twenty organisations for the records. Cadw says its 1988 file was probably destroyed but holds no record of destroying it. The Land Registry's own schedule of 43 documents does not include the deed its title rests on. This Council refused our request on cost, with figures that do not add up. Freedom of information, environmental information and our own personal data are three separate rights, and each should be answered on its own terms.

I am not asking this Council to try the case. I am asking you to do three things. Put our statement and pack on the public record. Make Great House Farm a standing item, with a named councillor. And write to the bodies holding the records, asking them to disclose them fully. The detail can follow, meeting by meeting, in the open.

Your mission is “Serving our community, preserving our identity”. Tŷ Mawr is part of that identity. For thirty-eight years it has had no voice. Tonight, please give it one.

Thank you.

5 Why it matters: scope, politics, culture

Scope: Bigger than a plaque.

A sacred site, a manorial seat, a Welsh family, and a public record that left all three out.

  • St Dochdwy's monastery, one of the great early Christian centres of Wales, whose site has never been formally located.
  • 1,026 early medieval burials, the largest such population yet recovered in Wales, now in museum storage.
  • Great House, where the manorial courts of Llandough and Leckwith sat.
  • A family's 320 years, ended by demolition and recorded only as an eviction.
  • Eighteen months of records requests to twenty organisations, including this Council.

Politics: Who writes the record?

A family and a village, or closed files and corporate lawyers.

  • The family puts it plainly: force, stealth and licence on one side; peace, transparency and freedom on the other. A Welsh home and a Welsh Christian site were decided by distant institutions and a pension fund's property company, and the Welsh family was left without a voice.
  • The ask of this Council is not partisan. It is the Council's own four commitments: open, accountable, responsive, participatory.
  • Fraud, concealment and government involvement are not established. The family is seeking the records that would test them. Openness is how that question gets answered, whichever way it falls.

Culture: Llandough's story, not only ours.

Saints, a manor, the first wireless message over open sea, and a world boxing champion.

  • The monastery and its dead belong to every Christian and every Welsh person, not to a housing estate.
  • Marconi's 1897 trials at Lavernock made this coast part of world history. Family tradition puts Great House in that story.
  • Peerless Jim Driscoll's first fight is recorded in a barn at Great House Farm.
  • None of this is marked anywhere in Llandough.

“Serving our community, preserving our identity.”

The Council's mission statement. Its commitments: be open and accessible in all our decision making; be accountable in all our council activities; be responsive to the needs of the community; encourage community participation.

6 What the agenda leaves out

MonasteryNever formally found. St Dochdwy's monastery is attested from the 7th century. Its site has never been located. Heneb's record says the exact location of the monastic centre has not been established. The Council's own page says Llandough was “almost certainly” its site. The ground beside the church where Great House stood was demolished in 1988 before its fabric could be fully examined; medieval pottery had been found on the slope beside the house in 1962–63. On record
1,026The dead are in storage. The largest early medieval burial population recovered in Wales, excavated in 1994. Excavated March–July 1994 by the Cotswold Archaeological Trust ahead of the houses. In 1990 the area had been marked “low potential”. The Home Office licence lapsed for part of the dig. On 28 July 1994 the developer, as landowner, gave the remains and finds to the National Museum of Wales. Nothing on the ground marks them. On record
The manorThe courts sat here. The manorial courts of Llandough and Leckwith were held at Great House. Recorded in the official Historic Environment Record (GGAT02038s), which also notes building details suggesting an older core, a 1974 survey limited by the ownership dispute, and demolition on 6 December 1988 amid considerable local controversy. On record
1897Marconi. The first wireless message over open sea crossed from Lavernock to Flat Holm. The trials of May 1897 are documented by Amgueddfa Cymru. Family tradition records that Thomas Williams of Great House carted the equipment, and that the house lodged visitors connected with the work. That family part is still to be corroborated, which is a reason to research it, not to leave it out. Family account
Peerless JimFirst fight, in the barn. Jim Driscoll, Cardiff's featherweight legend, is recorded as first fighting at Great House Farm. Published sources cite the National Monuments Record for Wales for Driscoll's first fight in a barn at Great House Farm, Llandough, and Stead and Williams, Wales and its Boxers (2008). The barn was demolished with the house. On record
04:00Demolished in the dark. The morning after listing was refused, with Strasbourg still pending. On 5 December 1988 the court refused to continue the injunction pending the European Commission of Human Rights, and Cadw said the house was not of sufficient merit to list. Demolition began at about 4am the next day. An MP had asked the Lord Chancellor to review the case. The house was never fully recorded. On record
Our pageSays 800. Says nothing. The Council's village history omits Great House altogether. The Council's About Llandough page mentions the villa, “over 800 burials”, the church and the cross. It does not mention Great House, the manorial courts, the family or the demolition. On record
18 monthsClosed doors. Twenty organisations asked. Files destroyed without record, deeds not on file, refusals on cost. On record

Lost on possession. Never tried on ownership.

  • The courts decided possession. BP never asked a court to declare it owned the house, and the 1974 action in which Mary Williams pleaded ownership was adjourned and never relisted.
  • The possession clock was started in 1955, from an order about the fields. The family's case is that the house was a separate parcel and theirs from 1928.
  • Mary Williams's statement records the family papers being taken from the house in 1950–52. They were never produced in court.
  • BP registered the farmhouse as a separate title in 1983, then merged it into the fields title in February 1987, while the appeal was pending.
  • No deed conveying the house to Western Ground Rents or BP, earlier than a 1975 conveyance between two BP companies, has been produced.
  • HM Land Registry's schedule of 43 first-registration documents does not include the 1969 conveyance on which the title rests.

The High Court (1986), Court of Appeal (1987), House of Lords (1988) and European Commission of Human Rights (1989) all ruled against the family. These are the family's grounds for saying the outcome was unfair and wrong. Testing them is for the courts, the Land Registry and regulators. This Council is not asked to decide them.

9 Three rights, not one

FOIFreedom of Information Act 2000. General recorded information. Twenty working days. A council may refuse if compliance would cost more than £450 (18 hours at £25), but must estimate reasonably and offer advice to narrow the request.
EIREnvironmental Information Regulations 2004. Information on land, development and the historic environment. Twenty working days. No automatic cost cap: refusal only if “manifestly unreasonable”, and then only if the public interest favours it.
SARUK GDPR, Article 15. Information about the person asking. One month, free. Covers what the Council holds about the living family members; it does not cover records about people who have died.
