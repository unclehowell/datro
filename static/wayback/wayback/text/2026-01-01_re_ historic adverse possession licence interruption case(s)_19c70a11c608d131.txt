I write on behalf of my family in connection with the case BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2, which concerned a claim to adverse possession of property at Great House Farm, Llandough (a historic site). The Court of Appeal upheld the effectiveness of a unilateral licence granted in 1974, finding that it ended the adverse nature of possession.

The concerns I wish to raise relate to the conduct of the barrister(s) representing BP Properties Ltd in the 1987 proceedings, specifically:

1. Whether there was adequate consideration or challenge to the identity of the addressee in the 1974 licence letters (addressed to "Mrs Buckler"), given that the occupier was known as Mary Williams and did not formally adopt the surname Buckler. She retained her maiden name Williams throughout her life, as evidenced by her marriage records (married as Williams in 1936), court proceedings and dealings prior to 1974 (all referring to her as Williams), and her 1983 death record (registered as Mary Williams). The licence letters appear to be the first documents to use "Mrs Buckler," a name which arguably carried no equivalent claim to the land as that held by Mary Williams. This relates to whether the basis for the licence's effectiveness was fully addressed in terms of offer/acceptance or meeting of minds under relevant principles (e.g., as referenced in cases like Cundy v Lindsay on consensus ad idem). The court's use of Latin phrases in the judgment may not have fully reflected Mary Williams's actual superior land claim.

2. Whether any relevant information concerning the property's heritage and archaeological significance, or potential future demolition plans, was known or should have been disclosed during the proceedings. The property was a 13th-century monastic grange, and a Roman soldier in full armour was discovered and reported by the family in 1870 beneath the farmhouse floor, as emphasized in a 1978 newspaper article where Mary Williams stated, "This is my house." The judgment was delivered on 31 July 1987, and the property was demolished in December 1988 despite Cadw's active consideration of emergency spot-listing. Subsequent archaeological excavations in 1994 confirmed the site as a major early-medieval monastic cemetery of national importance, with over 800 burials.

3. Whether all material facts relating to the circumstances and addressing of the 1974 licence letters were fully and properly presented to the court, including any potential concealment or suppression of evidence regarding the occupier's true identity, the 1667 family tradition of acquisition, or the alleged 1984 removal of a key index card from Cardiff Central Library that may have supported the family's ownership claim.

4. Whether the question of the family's potential superior title or ownership — derived from centuries of occupation, the 1916 tenancy context, and accumulated adverse possession — was adequately addressed or challenged, given that possession proceedings may have deferred or avoided a full determination of ultimate title.

5. Whether any assessments, surveys, or heritage-impact reports conducted before the 1987 proceedings were fully disclosed, and if they addressed or omitted historic reports of Roman and monastic burials on the site.
