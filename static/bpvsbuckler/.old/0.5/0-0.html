1667

Documented Williams Family Occupation

The Williams family occupied Great House Farm continuously from at least 1667. A 1987 newspaper article quotes Mrs Williams stating the property was 'acquired' by the Williams family in 1667.

Legal Context:
Under the common law doctrine of adverse possession as it existed in the 17th century, continuous occupation and use of land could establish possessory rights. In the absence of formal land registration systems (not established until the Land Registration Act 1862, and not mandatory in Wales until much later), occupation constituted de facto possession.

The Williams family maintained de facto possession and a continuous indigenous presence on the land from at least 1667.

While corporate records often prioritize the transition of "paper title," the Williams family asserts that they acquired the farm in 1667, as stated by Mary Williams in a 1978 newspaper article report, implies a purchase or a definitive transfer of rights rather than a mere tenancy. This distinction is vital because it shifts the family's status from "occupiers" to "owners," positioning them as the legitimate successors to the post-Dissolution gentry.

1888

Williams Family Discover Roman Soldier

Evidence: A Roman soldier in full armor was discovered under the living room floor of Tŷ Mawr (Great House Farm) in 1888. This discovery occurred while the Williams family was replacing old stone flagstones with a timber floor. The soldier was reportedly found in a stone-lined grave (cist) wearing what appeared to be bronze or iron armor.
Legal Context:
Under the Ancient Monuments Protection Act 1882, archaeological discoveries of significant historical value could be designated as protected monuments. Section 2 of the Act gave the Commissioners of Works power to take into guardianship any monument the owner was willing to surrender. However, the Act did not mandate reporting of discoveries on private land, nor did it require cessation of private works upon discovery.

The Treasure Trove common law (later codified in the Treasure Act 1996) required reporting of gold and silver objects, but did not explicitly cover human remains or non-precious metal artifacts in the 1880s.

This private find happened exactly one hundred years before the house was demolished in 1988 and over a century before the "official" 1994 excavations that claimed to first reveal the site's true historical depth.

Archaeological Significance: The burial was likely part of the Llandough Roman Villa estate, which was later used as a sacred monastic burial ground. Academic reports from the 1978 and 1994 excavations documented high-status Roman Villa remains and an early medieval cemetery on the same grounds.

Physical evidence found by residents was dismissed or "lost" until a developer's project forced a formal, taxpayer-funded excavation. It is therefore well-documented in local lore and early antiquarian records that Llandough was a Roman and early Christian hub. The discovery of a "Roman soldier in full armor" should have legally triggered an immediate halt to any development.

1897

Marconi stays at Great House Farm

Guglielmo Marconi slept at Great House Farm while working on wireless telegraphy experiments, including transmission tests between Lavernock Point and Flat Holm.

In a suppressed article a Williams was also mounting a pole during the successful famous experiment at Lavernock Point. The Williams in question was cut from the narrative or dismissed as another post office worker. But what are the odds that the man cut from the successful Marconi experiment, called Williams, isn't the Williams from Great House Farm that was carting Marconi to and from his experiments and the farm house, including that day - but another Williams. What are the odds if it was two men not one, cut from the headlines, they were both Williams. The Williams farmer was also allegedly not the engineer erecting the mast, as the farming family allegedly couldn't have been engineers. Yet a cousin called Williams, of Barry, died in Brussels as a Royal Engineers Soldier in 1918.
