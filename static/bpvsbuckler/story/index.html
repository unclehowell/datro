The Root

c. AD 650 · St Dochdwy's, Llandough

The church beside the house

Long before there is a farm, there is a church. St Dochdwy's at Llandough is one of the oldest Christian sites in Wales, the seat of an early monastery. Beside it, on the slope above the river, stands the place that will become Tŷ Mawr: the Great House. This is a story about who owns that ground, and how the answer was buried.

I.4 11

1877 · Bute Estate Office

The split

1877. The move that everything turns on. The Bute Estate sells the farmhouse, its buildings and about ten acres to the quarryman Daniel Thomas. Call it Parcel A. Act II · 1916 – 1948

Two Words

"The whole farm." A tenancy describes two parcels as one, just as the house becomes the family's own.

II.1 16

February 1916 · Great House Farm

"The whole farm"

February 1916. Bute lets John Williams "the whole farm" on a yearly tenancy. Read those words with what we now know. Bute has not owned the house since 1877. It owns the fields, Parcel B, and nothing else here. So "the whole farm" is a landlord describing land it does not own as if it did. The words are Bute's, not the family's. The tenancy itself has never been found; only those three words survive, quoted seventy years later by the Court of Appeal as the start of the story. From here on there are two Great House Farms: the real one, in two parcels, and a mythical one, a single estate that exists only on paper. Call it the synthetic farm. Every case against the family will be fought over the synthetic farm.

The words: "the whole farm". Both parcels, described as one by a landlord (Bute) that by then owned only Parcel B. The birth of the synthetic farm.

Act III · 1949 – 1968

The Mimic

The deeds vanish. Then, one move at a time, a second Parcel A is built on paper out of tenancies and court orders.

III.1 24

2 February 1949 · Great House Farm

Nothing in writing

February 1949. John Williams gives up his tenancy of the fields, and Frederick takes them on from Western Ground Rents. Nothing is written down: no lease, no plan. And now the first attempt to make Mary's occupation look permissive: the landlord begins to speak of Frederick's fields tenancy as a tenancy of "the farm", house and all, as if the house were his and she lived there by his tenancy. Mary rejects it. The house is hers, it was never in any tenancy of the fields, and she has the paper title in the blanket box to prove it. Note who blurs the two parcels here. Not the family. The landlord's own 1939 plan shows it knew exactly where the line was.

The words: "the farm" (Frederick's tenancy). Never written down. On the facts, the fields: Parcel B. Speaking of it as the farm, house and all, is the first attempt to make Mary look permitted.

III.4 27

2 February 1955 · High Court / Great House Farm

The first order

February 1955. Western Ground Rents sues Frederick, its tenant of the fields, and gets a High Court order for possession of "the whole of the farm". Now unpack it. The tenancy that ended was a tenancy of the fields. The landlord was the owner of the fields. An order giving a landlord back what it let can only reach Parcel B. "The whole of the farm" is the synthetic farm again: borrowed words that reach over the house without the landlord ever proving a right to it. Mary, who owns the house, is not a party and is never heard. On 4 July the order is enforced. The fields are taken. The family's account is that the woodland on the north side of Parcel A goes too, to a couple in Llandough, and later to a charity that has never touched it. The house is left: Mary, just home from hospital after losing a leg, stands her ground. Years later this order will be read as the day a twelve-year clock started running on her house. It wasn't. Whatever clock started in 1955 was a clock on the fields. The second attempt to make Mary's occupation look permissive is an insinuation, and it will later be mistaken for a fact.

The words: "the whole of the farm". As a matter of law, only what the landlord let and owned: Parcel B. As enforced on 4 July 1955: the fields (and, on the family's account, the woodland). Not the house.

The words: adverse possession "from 1955". The clock the 1987 court counted. It can only have been a clock on the fields; Parcel A was never Western Ground Rents's.

III.5 28

1959 · Great House Farm

Mary says no

The third attempt. In 1959 Western Ground Rents offers Mary a tenancy of "the farmhouse and garden", on their own. Notice the words. Not "the farm" this time. The landlord names the house parcel by itself: Parcel A, less the woodland to the north and the land to the south already being taken from around it. It knows exactly which piece is separate, and exactly which piece it has no deed to. Why else offer a tenancy of a house you say you own? If she signs, she becomes a tenant, and a tenant's occupation is permissive by definition. She refuses. The house is hers, she tells them, through her grandfather, and there are documents to prove it. The documents are in someone else's hands. The court will one day record that they were "never produced".

The words: "the farmhouse and garden". Parcel A (the farmhouse, yard and garden), less the woodland to the north and the land to the south already taken. The landlord now names the house parcel on its own.

III.6 29

11 December 1962 · Cardiff County Court

The 1962 order

The fourth attempt, and the one that will matter most. 11 December 1962: in Cardiff County Court, Judge Temple Morris QC orders Frederick and Mary out of "the farmhouse and garden", and to pay for every year back to 1955. V.5 52

31 July 1987 · Court of Appeal, London

The Court of Appeal

31 July 1987, the Court of Appeal. Here is the twist. The judges find that Frederick and Mary were in adverse possession of the house from 1955. The clock was running against the landlord. So why does BP win? Because of two of the attempts. The 1962 order, never enforced, was brought within twelve years and so stopped the clock. And the 1974 letters made Mary's possession permissive, the court holds, whether she accepted them or not. Now look at what is in front of the judges. A conveyance they themselves call "the actual conveyance of the farmhouse and garden": a separate deed for the house. Yet the case is decided as one farm, with the clock started in 1955 by an order about the fields. Count from 1928, as the house's own history requires, and the twelve years were up in 1940, fifteen years before any order. Mary's title documents, the judgment notes, were "never produced". Nobody asks where they went. BP is recorded as having "the paper title to the farm": paper to the fields, and for the house only a deed between two BP companies. BP Properties Ltd v Buckler goes into the law books as a leading case on adverse possession. It decided possession. It never decided ownership.

Epilogue · Now

How it was done, who holds what, and what was never decided.

E.1 98

Today · The two titles

How it was done

So here is how it was done. Not with one forged deed, but with a sequence. First, two words in 1916 made two parcels one farm, in a landlord's document, never the family's. Then six attempts to make Mary's occupation of her own house look permissive: her house spoken of as part of her husband's fields tenancy; an order against the fields tenant worded for the whole farm; a tenancy offered, and refused; a possession order made and left in a drawer; another tenancy offered, and refused; and a licence she never asked for and never accepted. Alongside them, the woodland and the land to the south were taken from around her. Then the paper: BP took the fields by deed and the case against the house with them; one BP company conveyed the house to another; the state registered it, then folded it into the fields. The family, for their part, were led into claiming adverse possession of the mythical farm, counted from an order about the fields, when Parcel A had been theirs since 1928 and by possession since 1940. And the courts, asked only who had the right to possession, never had to ask who owned the house. In English law, possession can be awarded without ownership ever being tried, and a title can be registered without any court deciding a rival claim to the same land. Neither step, on its own, decides the older title. Every move went through one of those two doors.

E.2 99

Today · The two parcels

Who knew

So who knew the farm was two? Mary did, in her way. Her 1974 statement tells of the house and ten acres sold to Daniel Thomas, and the nine acres Bute kept. But her deeds were gone, and no lawyer of hers ever turned that history into a case about two parcels. If the family had grasped what it meant, they would have shouted it from the rooftops; it was their whole case. The other side never needed it explained. Western Ground Rents offered tenancies of "the farmhouse and garden" alone, and sued for it alone. BP conveyed it alone, between its own companies, registered it alone, and then merged it. The Land Registry issued two titles and joined them into one. The Court of Appeal read a conveyance of "the farmhouse and garden". Everyone made a mess of this case: the drafting, the lawyers, the courts, the Registry. A haze over the two parcels might be forgiven once. But the people who knew best treated the two parcels as one whenever that helped, and as two whenever that helped more. The family call it what it looks like: a conspiracy against them.
