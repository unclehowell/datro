Late Iron Age (approx. 2,200 years ago; exact date uncertain) · Llandough Roman Villa site (separate site)

Iron Age farmstead at Llandough

Excavations in 1978–79 at the separate Llandough Roman Villa site identified earlier Iron Age occupation. A curvilinear feature and postholes may represent a round hut; associated pottery is in a late Iron Age tradition. This is evidence for early occupation at Llandough, not evidence that a round hut stood on the Great House Farm parcel.

What we now know. Heneb's record for the separate Llandough Roman Villa site describes an Iron Age farmstead and a possible round hut, represented by a curvilinear feature and postholes. The pottery indicates a pre-Conquest tradition, but the record does not establish an exact date of c. 200 BC or show that the hut stood on Great House Farm itself.

P.4 4

1536 – 1793 · Manor of Llandough

The lordship of the manor

Llandough belongs to Tewkesbury Abbey until the Dissolution of 1536–39. Cardiff Records states that in 1543 Henry VIII granted the manor to Lord Clynton and Say and Robert Turwitt, and that by 1545 it was held by Sir George Herbert. The Western Mail letter states that the Herberts held the lordship until the Earl of Bute bought it in 1793; Cardiff Records places William Hurst and others in the account from 1767. The same 1974 newspaper record identifies Mary (Nancy) Williams's family as occupying the manorial home since 1667. That occupation and the lordship are separate from the farmhouse freehold. The family's case is that the Williamses were virtual freeholders under the 1877 bargain while quarrying continued, and that Parcel A's freehold passed to them in 1928 when quarrying ended.

The received account. The lord of the manor owned the farm.

Wording: "since 1667" The 1974 newspaper records the Williams family’s occupation of the manorial home; occupation is a separate matter from the lordship and the freehold.

What we now know. Lordship of a manor and ownership of the farmhouse parcel are separate interests. The 1974 newspaper records the Williams family’s occupation of the manorial home since 1667. The family’s title case is not occupation alone: it is that the 1877 bargain entitled the Williamses to Parcel A’s freehold when quarrying ended, identified by Mary as 1928, when the final rent was paid. On that account, the Williamses were virtual freeholders while the quarrying arrangement ran and became freeholders in 1928. The 1877 deed and final receipt have not been located, so the 1928 transfer remains the family’s asserted title history rather than a fact determined by the 1974 newspaper or a court. The manorial sources also differ over 1767–1793; the Herbert estate papers have not yet been checked.

20 June 1547 · Manor of Llandough — Cydwin

A 1,000-year lease of Cydwin

The National Library of Wales catalogue describes a lease for 1,000 years dated 20 June 1547 from Sir George Herbert of Swansea to Edmund Vaughan of a farm called Cydwin in the manor of Llandough; the record names fields. This long leasehold interest predates the later Bute estate narrative. The affirmative Cedwyn thesis asks whether those named fields belong to the older Cydfin/Ty Mawr holding later catalogued as a 107-acre farm, and how that interest passed from the Herbert/Vaughan line into subsequent estate records. The instrument and field boundaries must be examined before the lease can be equated with the whole Great House site.

The received account. The Bute estate is the beginning of the land's title.

What we now know. The 1547 NLW catalogue entry names a Cydwin farm in the manor of Llandough and a 1,000-year lease from Sir George Herbert to Edmond Vaughan. That is a specific pre-Bute interest and an archival route to follow. It does not yet identify every field with the later Great House boundaries; the lease and its named fields must be compared with D 219 and historic plans.

Mid 16th – late 18th century · Great House Farm

The Vaughans

From the mid-16th to the late 18th century the Vaughan family hold Great House as the chief freehold farm of the parish. The lordship of the manor is a separate interest, held over the same period by the Herberts.

The received account. One chain of owners: the Herberts, then the Vaughans, then Bute.

What we now know. Two separate lines. The lordship of the manor: Tewkesbury Abbey, the Crown, the Herberts from 1545, Bute from 1793. The ownership of the farm: a freehold within the manor, held by the Vaughans until the late 18th century and by Bute from the early 19th. How the farm passed from the Vaughans to Bute is not yet documented. Parcel A's title descends from the farm freehold, not from the lordship.

1667 · Great House Farm

The Williams family at Great House

Mary (Nancy) Williams's c.1974 statement records that her family had lived and farmed at Great House since 1667. A 1974 newspaper report also reports her statement that her family first acquired Tŷ Mawr around that date. The affirmative Cedwyn thesis treats 1667 as the opening of the family's own estate history and tests that statement against the Cydwin/Cydfin lease and farm-name records. The surviving statement and press report support the Buckler case; a 1667 conveyance has not yet been identified.

The received account. Tenant farmers who lived on someone else's land.

Wording: "tenants" The estate's description of the family, repeated in every later account.

What we now know. Mary's statement and the 1974 press report support the Buckler case for a connection with Great House from 1667 and treat it as a lineage claim, not a passing tenancy. The affirmative reconstruction now connects that claim to the older Cydwin/Cydfin/Ty Mawr lease records and asks whether later labels of tenancy concealed a longer-standing family interest. The surviving items do not themselves constitute a 1667 conveyance; the deed chain must be reconstructed from the leases, estate accounts, plans and the 1877/1928 evidence.

P.10 10

1793 – early 19th century · Great House

The Bute Estate

The historical account places Bute's acquisition of the lordship of Llandough in 1793 and its acquisition of the Great House freehold in the early 19th century; the manorial courts of Llandough and Leckwith were held at the house. The missing control point is the instrument that carried the farm freehold from the Vaughan line into Bute's estate. The 1547 Cydwin lease, its 1739/40 assignment, and the D 219 record of Cydfin or Ty Mawr leases give the affirmative reconstruction a named chain to investigate alongside the manor's later ownership. A manorial interest, a long lease, and a farm freehold must be mapped separately before the later corporate titles can be treated as the originating chain for every parcel.

The received account. Bute owned the farm, and everything after descends from Bute.

What we now know. The historical account places the manorial lordship in Bute's hands from 1793 and the Great House farm freehold in the early 19th century. The deed that moved the farm freehold from the Vaughan line is the key missing control point. The L 11 lease assignment and D 219 farm records provide older property and tenure references against which Bute's acquisition, rentals and subsequent 1877 division must be tested. The house was also a centre for the Llandough and Leckwith manorial courts; that administrative role must not be confused with ownership of each parcel.

E.1 158

Today · The two titles

The sequence

1916 to 1987: a tenancy, two possession orders, three tenancy offers, licence letters, a conveyance, two registrations and an amalgamation.

The received account. A landlord and its successors recovered their farm from tenants who stayed on without paying, and the courts confirmed it.

Wording: "the whole farm" 1916.

Wording: "the whole of the farm" 1955.

Wording: "licence" 1974.

Wording: "the paper title to the farm" 1987.

Wording: "registered correctly" 2026.

What we now know. Parcel A was the family's from 1928, and by possession from 1940. It was never shown to belong to Western Ground Rents or BP. A synthetic single farm was described in 1916; six attempts (1949, 1955, 1959, 1962, 1965, 1974) treated Mary's occupation as permissive; the woodland and the land to the south were taken; the 1975 conveyance and 1983 registration gave the synthetic Parcel A a deed and a title; the 1987 amalgamation folded it into Parcel B. The courts decided possession and never decided ownership. Possession can be awarded without ownership being tried, and land can be registered without a rival claim being decided. Neither decides the older title.

The two parcels in the record

Records of 1877–1987 describe the property both as one farm and as separate parts.
