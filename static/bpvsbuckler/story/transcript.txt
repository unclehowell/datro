TŶ MAWR — THE GREAT HOUSE FARM STORY
Narration script: 99 scenes in date order. Release v0.0.0.52, 2026-10-06. Source: https://greathousefarmwiki.wordpress.com/

PROLOGUE: THE GREAT HOUSE (c. 650 – 1857)

P.1  C. AD 650 — The church beside the house (St Dochdwy's, Llandough)

NARRATOR: Long before there is a farm, there is a church. St Dochdwy's at Llandough is one of the oldest Christian sites in Wales, the seat of an early monastery. Beside it, on the slope above the river, stands the place that will become Tŷ Mawr: the Great House.

P.2  12TH – 14TH CENTURY — An old house (Great House — north-east slope)

NARRATOR: In the Middle Ages people live and work on this slope; their pottery is still in the ground eight hundred years later. The house that grows here is old, and it is grand: a long stone house the Royal Commission will judge sub-medieval, probably of the seventeenth century, perhaps built around something older still.

P.3  1552 – 1829 — Cydfin, or Tŷ Mawr (Llandough manorial leases)

NARRATOR: In the manor's leases it goes by an older name: Cydfin, or Tŷ Mawr, a farm of a hundred and seven acres. For nearly three centuries the leases are kept, signed and filed.

P.4  MID 16TH – LATE 18TH CENTURY — The Vaughans of Great House (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Great House is the chief freehold farm of the parish. For two hundred years the Vaughans, a minor gentry family, hold it. Their memorials are still in the church next door.

P.5  1667 — The Williamses arrive (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: In 1667 the Williams family come to Great House. Mary Williams, born there two and a half centuries later, will say her ancestors bought it. From now on, for more than three hundred years, there is a Williams in the house.

P.6  EARLY 19TH CENTURY — The Marquesses of Bute (Great House)

NARRATOR: Early in the nineteenth century the Bute Estate, the greatest landowner in South Wales, takes the freehold. The manor courts of Llandough and Leckwith sit in the house itself. By 1818 Llandough's rents are in Bute's books, and in 1824 the estate's surveyor walks the farm and writes it down.

P.7  C. 1840 — The Great House shrinks (Census and tithe records)

NARRATOR: Then, on paper, the Great House begins to shrink. In the census and the tithe returns it stops being 'Great House' and becomes 'Great House Farm': no longer the court house of a manor, just a farmhouse. It is the first time the records make the place smaller than it is. It will not be the last.

ACT I: THE BARGAIN (1858 – 1915)

I.1  C. 1858 — Bones by the church (Church Farm, Llandough)

NARRATOR: Workmen digging foundations beside the church find six skeletons, a medieval spearhead and a spur. The bones are put back in the ground by the churchyard stile. Llandough's dead lie close under the surface. Nobody yet asks how many there are.

I.2  C. 1870 — The soldier under the floor (Great House — dining room)

NARRATOR: Under the dining-room floor of the Great House, the family will always say, lies a soldier in armour, with his horse, his lance and a small shield. The story is told for a hundred years. It reaches the Daily Telegraph and the Royal Commission's files. Nobody ever lifts the floor to look.

I.3  1876 – C. 1912 — The quarrymen (Llandough Limeworks)

NARRATOR: Limestone brings the quarrymen. The Bute Estate lets about thirty-three acres of the farm to the Llandough Limeworks, and for the next thirty-five years the quarry eats into the land.

I.4  1877 — The farm is cut in two (Bute Estate Office)

NARRATOR: In 1877 the estate divides the farm. It sells the farmhouse, its buildings and about ten acres to the quarryman Daniel Thomas, and keeps nine acres of fields on the top. The Williamses stay on as tenants of both, and, as Mary will tell it, with a promise: when the quarrying ends, the house comes to them. From this year Great House is two parcels with two owners. Everything that follows turns on that line, and on the paper that drew it. That paper is now missing.

I.5  11 NOVEMBER 1891 — Three geese (Llandough — court report)

NARRATOR: Fourteen years later three geese are stolen from "Mr. John Williams, Great House Farm, Llandough". The thief goes to court, and the newspaper records, in passing, who lives at the house.

I.6  MAY 1897 — Marconi (Lavernock Point)

NARRATOR: In May 1897 Marconi sends the first wireless signals across open sea, from Lavernock Point to Flat Holm. In the family's memory it is Thomas Williams of Great House who carts his equipment down to the point.

I.7  1908 — John Williams takes over (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Mary's grandfather dies, and her father, John Williams, takes over: the house under the Thomases, the fields under Bute. Two parcels, two landlords, one family.

I.8  10 AUGUST 1913 — Mary is born (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 10 August 1913 Mary Williams is born in the farmhouse. She will live in it for the rest of her life, and she will die in it.

ACT II: THE HOUSE IS THEIRS (1916 – 1948)

II.1  FEBRUARY 1916 — "The whole farm" (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: In 1916 Bute lets John Williams "the whole farm" on a yearly tenancy. Bute no longer owns the house; it sold it in 1877. But those two words, "whole farm", will be read for the next seventy years as if it did. The tenancy itself does not survive. Only the words do, quoted in a judgment seventy years later.

II.2  1928 — The last rent (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: In 1928 the last of the quarry machinery leaves. John Williams pays a final rent of about four pounds to Alfred Thomas, and the bargain of 1877 comes due. From now on the house is the family's own. Mary sees the receipt. It will not survive.

II.3  1936 — Mary marries Frederick (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Mary marries Frederick Buckler, a farmer, and he comes to live with her in the house. They will have seven children; the first, a son, is born within a few years.

II.4  1938 — Sold to Western Ground Rents (Bute Estate Office)

NARRATOR: In 1938 Bute sells out to a property company, Western Ground Rents. It sells what it still has at Llandough: the fields, and its interest as landlord. It cannot sell the house. It no longer owns it.

II.5  MAY 1939 — The plan (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: A year later Western Ground Rents gives John Williams a new tenancy, with a plan. The plan shows the fields east of the house. The house is not on it.

II.6  C. 1940 — Twelve years (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: By 1940 John Williams has lived in the house for twelve years without paying anyone a penny for it. In law, twelve years is enough. If the bargain of 1877 had not already made the house his, time has.

II.7  1944 — War on the land (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: The war is on, the farm is struggling, and the War Agricultural Committee wants it run better. Frederick takes over the fields as manager for his father-in-law.

II.8  C. 1948 – 49 — Billy is born (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: William Buckler, Billy, is born at the farm. He will be the last of the family to live there, and the one they evict.

ACT III: THE PAPERS VANISH (1949 – 1968)

III.1  2 FEBRUARY 1949 — Nothing in writing (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: In February 1949 John Williams gives up his tenancy of the fields, and Frederick takes them on from Western Ground Rents. Nothing is written down. Mary, whose house it is, is not part of it. But from now on the landlord will talk of Frederick's tenancy as a tenancy of "the farm", house and all.

III.2  JUNE 1950 – C. 1952 — The blanket box (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: In 1950 an estate agent from Penarth places a lodger in the farmhouse. He stays two years. Before he goes, he tells Mary what he has done: he has taken the papers from the family's blanket box, 'including the agreement relating to the farm', and handed them to the agent. The family's proof of title leaves the house in a lodger's hands. It is never seen again.

III.3  1952 – 53 — The last rent on the fields (Landlords' agents)

NARRATOR: In October 1952 the landlord's agents write that they will not go on letting the fields to Frederick. He pays his last rent the next year. After that the family pay no one.

III.4  2 FEBRUARY 1955 — The first order (High Court / Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: In February 1955 the High Court orders Frederick to give up possession of "the whole farm". Mary, who owns the house, is not in the case and is never heard. On 4 July the landlord comes to take it all. Mary has just come home from hospital. She stands her ground, and they take the fields and leave the house.

III.5  1959 — Mary says no (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: In 1959 Western Ground Rents offers Mary a tenancy of her own farmhouse and garden. She refuses. The house is hers, she tells them, and there are documents to prove it. The documents are already gone.

III.6  11 DECEMBER 1962 — The second order (Cardiff County Court)

NARRATOR: On 11 December 1962, in Cardiff County Court, Judge Temple Morris QC orders Frederick and Mary out of the farmhouse and garden, and makes them pay for every year back to 1955. The order is made. It is never carried out. Mary stays.

III.7  JUNE 1963 — Committal (Cardiff County Court)

NARRATOR: The landlord goes after Frederick for the money, and committal proceedings follow. The threat of prison now hangs over the family for staying in their own home.

III.8  JANUARY – MARCH 1965 — Two pounds a week (Landlords' agents)

NARRATOR: In March 1965 the agents try again: a weekly tenancy of the farmhouse and garden for 'Mrs Williams', at two pounds a week. Mary does not sign it, does not send it back, and does not pay. From now on even the landlord counts her as living there in her own right, not theirs.

III.9  1964 – 68 — A rumoured sale (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Somewhere in these years, a cousin will later say, the eldest son sells "our land parcel" through a solicitor, behind his parents' backs. No document has ever come to light. In 1968 he and his household leave the farm.

III.10  1967 — Frederick dies (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Frederick Buckler dies at fifty-six. Mary takes back her maiden name, Williams, and stays on in the house she was born in.

ACT IV: BP (1969 – 1983)

IV.1  31 DECEMBER 1969 — BP buys in (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On the last day of 1969 Western Ground Rents sells Great House Farm to the BP Pension Trust, the pension fund of British Petroleum. That conveyance will become the root of BP's title. Today it cannot be found in the Land Registry's files.

IV.2  1970 – 72 — Plans drawn around her (Planning office)

NARRATOR: Around Mary the plans are already being drawn. In 1970 land by Leckwith Road is registered as a village green. In July 1972 planning permission is granted for houses on the site, with Mary still living in the middle of it.

IV.3  1974 — Half a survey (Great House — interior)

NARRATOR: The Royal Commission sends a surveyor to record the old house. He gets as far as the ground floor. The rest, he notes, cannot be examined 'because of problems of access created by an ownership dispute'. The house will never be fully recorded.

IV.4  15 APRIL 1974 — Open day (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 15 April 1974 Mary, now sixty, opens her home to the public 'to show what the nation is losing'. Hundreds come, and a thousand sign a petition. The Daily Telegraph reports that BP's pension fund has permission for thirteen houses, that Mary will not move, and that her solicitors say she has a valid claim to the house.

IV.5  3 JULY 1974 — Mary pleads ownership (Court)

NARRATOR: BP Pension Trust sues for possession. In her defence Mary pleads, plainly, that the house is hers, and the Limitation Act besides. The case is part heard and adjourned. It is never brought back. Her claim to own the house is never tried.

IV.6  SEPTEMBER – OCTOBER 1974 — The licence letters (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: In September BP gets leave to enforce the old 1962 order; Mary is given no notice. Then, on 31 October, BP withdraws the warrant and writes to her instead, 'licensing' her to live in the house rent-free for life. Mary never asked for a licence, and never accepts one. She does not believe BP owns her house. But BP keeps the letters.

IV.7  19 NOVEMBER, 1970S (YEAR UNCLEAR) — "Grievous loss" (Western Mail — letters)

NARRATOR: A letter to the Western Mail warns that pulling down Great House would wipe out the site of St Dochdwy's Celtic monastery, and traces the land back through Tewkesbury Abbey to the Crown. The letter is printed. Nothing changes.

IV.8  1975 – 1980 — "The most rapacious ground landlord" (House of Commons)

NARRATOR: In 1975 the pension fund passes its interest to BP Properties. In May 1980 Ted Rowlands MP tells the House of Commons that Western Ground Rents, 'the most rapacious ground landlord' in South Wales, has been bought by the BP pension fund. The landlord that first came for the farm in 1955 now belongs to BP.

IV.9  1 OCTOBER 1982 — A warning about the ground (GGAT)

NARRATOR: In October 1982 GGAT, the regional archaeological trust, warns the planners that the ancient settlement may run north of the church, under the farm, and asks for thorough investigation before anything is built.

IV.10  19 – 30 NOVEMBER 1982 — First registration (HM Land Registry — WA231076)

NARRATOR: In November 1982 BP's solicitors apply to register the land at HM Land Registry for the first time. Mary is seventy and living in the farmhouse. Nothing on the file shows anyone asking her what she claims.

IV.11  23 FEBRUARY 1983 — The farmhouse title (HM Land Registry — WA240304)

NARRATOR: In February 1983 they apply again, for the farmhouse and garden on their own: a separate title, WA240304. The application certifies that BP knows of no question or doubt about its title. The woman who told a court nine years earlier that the house is hers is living in it.

IV.12  26 MARCH 1983 — Mary dies (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 26 March 1983, a month later, Mary Williams dies in the farmhouse where she was born. Her son Billy inherits her claim, and her fight.

IV.13  8 APRIL – 12 MAY 1983 — Letters after her death (HM Land Registry — WA240304)

NARRATOR: Two weeks after Mary's death, BP's solicitors and the Land Registry are writing to each other about the farmhouse file. Whatever the Registry asked about the family in the house, and whatever it was told, is in those letters.

ACT V: POSSESSION, NOT OWNERSHIP (1984 – March 1988)

V.1  1984 — BP's writ (BP Properties)

NARRATOR: In January 1984 the register is reissued. In May BP Properties issues its writ for possession of the house. Its case rests on the licence letters of 1974, the letters Mary refused. That same year, the family learn, the library's copy of the 1877 deed between Bute and Daniel Thomas goes missing.

V.2  10 JULY 1986 — Judgment for BP (High Court, Cardiff)

NARRATOR: On 10 July 1986, in the High Court at Cardiff, Mr Justice Hollis finds for BP. The 1974 letters, he holds, gave Mary a licence, so she was never in possession against BP. The deed of 1877, the receipt of 1928 and the plan of 1939 are not before him. The question of who owns the house is never put.

V.3  1986 – 87 — A file goes missing (Royal Commission (RCAHMW))

NARRATOR: While the case goes on, the Royal Commission's correspondence with Mrs Buckler goes missing from its files: letters in which she may have told an independent public body, in her own words, why the house was hers.

V.4  2 FEBRUARY 1987 — Two titles become one (HM Land Registry)

NARRATOR: On 2 February 1987, with the appeal still to be heard, the Land Registry is asked to fold the farmhouse title into BP's main title. The separate record of the family's house disappears into the whole.

V.5  31 JULY 1987 — The Court of Appeal (Court of Appeal, London)

NARRATOR: On 31 July 1987 three judges in the Court of Appeal dismiss Billy's appeal. The 1962 action, they hold, stopped the clock, and BP's letters made Mary's possession permissive whether she accepted them or not. The documents she spoke of are not produced. Nor is the newspaper that reported her claiming the house in 1974. BP Properties Ltd v Buckler goes into the law books as a leading case on adverse possession. The family's story does not.

V.6  3 MARCH 1988 — Twenty-eight days (House of Lords)

NARRATOR: On 3 March 1988 the House of Lords refuses leave to appeal. The family are given twenty-eight days to leave a house the Williamses have lived in for three hundred years.

ACT VI: BULLDOZED BEFORE BREAKFAST (April – December 1988)

VI.1  29 APRIL 1988, MORNING — The bailiffs come (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: 29 April 1988. Five bailiffs and fifteen police officers come up the lane. Billy blocks the narrow drive with a tractor, locks the doors and bars the windows.

VI.2  29 APRIL 1988 — The chainsaw (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: The bailiffs smash the gate and take poleaxes to the doors. When an axe comes through a door with his three small children behind it, Billy starts a chainsaw and pushes it through. The bailiffs fall back. After four hours they leave. Branwen takes the children away. BP tells the press it let Mary stay out of kindness. Billy tells them the farm is theirs.

VI.3  MAY 1988 — "Someone will be killed" (Alun Michael MP's office)

NARRATOR: Billy takes his case to the European Commission of Human Rights in Strasbourg. On 12 May the Cardiff MP Alun Michael asks the Lord Chancellor to look at it again. Billy warns that someone is going to be killed.

VI.4  29 JULY 1988 — Cadw comes to look (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 29 July Cadw, the Welsh heritage body, comes to look at the house for listing, and photographs the farmhouse and the barn. Those two photographs are all that is left of the visit. No notes, no file, no record of who decided.

VI.5  29 – 30 NOVEMBER 1988 — Forced entry (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: 29 November. The bailiffs come back, and this time they force their way in. Within hours, the family say, the demolition has begun: half the roof is already off an outbuilding. Billy is taken to Llandough Hospital. Eight other people living at the farm lose their homes with the family.

VI.6  1 DECEMBER 1988 — Everything in the road (Llandough Hospital)

NARRATOR: Billy lies injured in hospital. Branwen and the children have nowhere to go. Mary's whole life, her photographs, the family heirlooms, the legal papers, is left in the house or thrown into the road. Billy has the clothes he stands in.

VI.7  2 DECEMBER 1988 — Do not touch the house (High Court)

NARRATOR: On 2 December a High Court judge orders that the farmhouse and everything in it must not be touched. Branwen may go in for her belongings, if BP's solicitors agree. BP says it means to demolish and build new homes on the ten acres.

VI.8  3 DECEMBER 1988 — From hospital bed to the dock (Llandough Hospital / magistrates)

NARRATOR: Police take Billy from his hospital bed and charge him with assaulting two bailiffs, criminal damage, and furious driving.

VI.9  3 – 5 DECEMBER 1988 — One weekend (Cadw)

NARRATOR: Over one weekend, without setting foot inside, Cadw considers emergency listing. In public it says it is looking at the house. In July it had already marked it just below the bar.

VI.10  MONDAY 5 DECEMBER 1988 — Nothing more we can do (Cardiff)

NARRATOR: Monday 5 December. Judge Norman Francis refuses to keep the injunction in place until Strasbourg can hear the family. Their solicitor says there is nothing more they can do. The same day Cadw announces that the house is not of sufficient merit to list. Nothing now protects it.

VI.11  6 DECEMBER 1988, 4AM — Bulldozed before breakfast (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: 6 December 1988. Just after four in the morning, a twenty-strong team arrives with bulldozers. By breakfast no building is left standing. Branwen and the children watch their home go. Three centuries of the Williams family, the old court house of the manor, the soldier under the floor: gone before the day has begun.

VI.12  7 – 9 DECEMBER 1988 — Like a battlefield (Llandough)

NARRATOR: Llandough is furious. A county councillor, himself a BP pensioner, calls it 'disgusting'. Villagers confront their councillors. The council's planning chief, the family remember, says the site looks like a battlefield. On 9 December magistrates bail Billy on condition that he stays away from the land he was born on.

VI.13  DECEMBER 1988 — In the rubble (Great House Farm — rubble)

NARRATOR: In the rubble a Royal Commission investigator finds what the bulldozers left: a carved fireplace jamb, and a moulded beam of a kind rare in Glamorgan. And he records what is lost: a carved stone capital that stood by the front door, probably taken long ago from the old church.

ACT VII: BUILT OVER (1989 – 2019)

VII.1  20 MARCH 1989 — Clearing the site (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 20 March 1989, at half past seven in the morning, the lorries move in to carry away what is left.

VII.2  JANUARY – MARCH 1989 — "I will fight on" (Court)

NARRATOR: In January one charge is dropped as out of time, and Billy is told not to come within half a mile of the site. On 23 March he pleads guilty to the rest and walks free. He will fight on, he says, for his home and for more than seventy acres of land.

VII.3  1989 — From farm to a bus (Penarth)

NARRATOR: The family who lived in the Great House now live with Billy's sister in Penarth. Billy plans to make a home in an old bus. Thirty thousand pounds' worth of his belongings, he says, were taken from the site after the demolition.

VII.4  14 APRIL 1989 — Strasbourg says no (European Commission of Human Rights)

NARRATOR: In April 1989 Strasbourg rules. The European Commission accepts that the eviction interfered with Billy's home, but finds it lawful, and closes the case.

VII.5  OCTOBER – NOVEMBER 1989 — BP applies to build (Vale of Glamorgan planning)

NARRATOR: In November 1989 BP applies for permission to build houses on the farm. GGAT advises that nothing should be decided without an archaeological assessment. Then BP hires GGAT to do it. Their report calls the lost farmhouse 'a county treasure'.

VII.6  FEBRUARY – MARCH 1990 — "Unfortunately demolished" (Vale of Glamorgan planning)

NARRATOR: In March 1990 outline permission is granted. The planning officer notes that the Local Plan said the farm buildings should be kept, 'however they were unfortunately demolished a little while ago'. No preservation notice was ever served. Part of the archaeologists' advice is missing from the file.

VII.7  AUGUST – OCTOBER 1990 — Eight trenches (Former farm site)

NARRATOR: In August 1990 GGAT cuts eight trenches for BP. They find early medieval burials. A large area they could not investigate is marked as low potential, and in October the council signs the archaeology off.

VII.8  21 AUGUST 1991 — The obituary (Historic Environment Record)

NARRATOR: In 1991 the official heritage record writes the house's obituary. It 'was suddenly and completely demolished by B.P. Properties Ltd. on 6th December 1988 amid considerable local controversy'.

VII.9  14 MAY 1992 — Twenty houses (Vale of Glamorgan planning)

NARRATOR: In May 1992 Ideal Homes applies to build twenty detached houses on the farm.

VII.10  15 MAY 1992 — Billy dies (Llandough)

NARRATOR: The next day, 15 May 1992, Billy Buckler dies of a heart attack in Llandough Hospital, in the village where he was born. He is buried at Michaelston-le-Pit. He never gets his home back.

VII.11  27 JULY 1992 — A warning, half missing (GGAT)

NARRATOR: In July 1992 GGAT warns the council that the ground holds significant remains, medieval and perhaps older, and human bone. Page two of the letter is missing from the file.

VII.12  3 SEPTEMBER 1992 — Approved anyway (Vale of Glamorgan planning)

NARRATOR: Thirty-eight days later the twenty houses are approved, with no new archaeological condition.

VII.13  DECEMBER 1993 — The diggers move in (Former farm site)

NARRATOR: In December 1993 BP transfers the land to Ideal Homes, and the diggers move in before the archaeology has been dealt with. GGAT asks the council to stop the work. The developer produces a barrister's opinion that the condition is met. The council accepts it: 'a difference of opinion', it later tells the MP.

VII.14  MARCH – JULY 1994 — A thousand graves (Former farm site)

NARRATOR: In the spring of 1994 the excavators find what the trenches missed. Burial after burial lies just below the topsoil, in the ground the 1990 report called low potential. By the end there are more than a thousand: the largest early medieval burial ground ever found in Wales. For at least two weeks of the dig, the Home Office licence to remove the dead has run out. The work goes on.

VII.15  28 JULY 1994 — Who owns the dead (National Museum of Wales)

NARRATOR: In July 1994 Ideal Homes, as owner of the land, gives the human remains and finds to the National Museum of Wales. The dead of St Dochdwy's pass from a developer to the state.

VII.16  1994 – 96 — Church View Close (Woodland — WA735527)

NARRATOR: In November 1994 the woodland above the house, part of the family's parcel, passes to the Forest of Cardiff. The power lines go in. The houses go up. Church View Close is built over the farm.

VII.17  2005 — The cemetery in print (Medieval Archaeology)

NARRATOR: In 2005 the cemetery is published in a learned journal: 1,026 burials. It tells the story of the dead. It does not tell the story of the family whose home stood over them, or of how that home came down.

VII.18  20 MARCH 2019 — Nearly thirty years late (RCAHMW national archive)

NARRATOR: In 2019 GGAT's 1990 report on Great House Farm is finally added to the national record, nearly thirty years after it was written, and thirty years after the house was gone.

ACT VIII: THE SILENCE (2025 – 2026)

VIII.1  NOVEMBER 2025 – JANUARY 2026 — The grandchildren (Royal Commission and others)

NARRATOR: In November 2025 Mary's grandchildren start asking for the records. The community council says it cannot help. Cadw's email addresses bounce, the first of many. In January 2026 they ask the Land Registry to put back on the record the claim that was left off it in 1983.

VIII.2  FEBRUARY – MARCH 2026 — "No evidence of a mistake" (HM Land Registry)

NARRATOR: The Land Registry retrieves its old paper files. In March it replies: there is 'no evidence of a mistake in the register'. It treats the farm as one holding. It does not say how the house got into BP's title.

VIII.3  19 APRIL – 25 MAY 2026 — "Registered correctly" (HM Land Registry)

NARRATOR: The family complain of fraud and maladministration. The Registry will not look behind the 1987 judgment. In May it rules that the title is 'registered correctly', on the strength of a case about possession and not a single deed to the house.

VIII.4  21 MAY 2026 — Cadw's two photographs (Cadw)

NARRATOR: Cadw releases its two photographs from July 1988, marked 'YYY': 'marginally below the bar', it says. Everything else is gone. The file is 'quite likely' destroyed, it says; by July, 'most likely'. There is no record of anyone destroying it.

VIII.5  JUNE – JULY 2026 — The missing root (HM Land Registry)

NARRATOR: In June the family ask to see the Land Registry's original files, and ask that nothing be destroyed. Six months after their first request, the Registry lists the forty-three documents behind BP's first registration. The 1969 conveyance, the root of BP's title, is not among them.

VIII.6  25 AUGUST 2026 — "Not held" (Museum Wales)

NARRATOR: Museum Wales refuses the family's request: the information is 'not held'. In the same letter it says it has found records of other discoveries at Great House Farm, and withholds them as 'not in scope'.

VIII.7  8 SEPTEMBER 2026 — A lead to 1877 (Glamorgan Archives)

NARRATOR: Glamorgan Archives hold no deed for the 1877 sale. But they point to the Bute trustees' book of payments for that year, now in Cardiff. If Daniel Thomas paid for the house, it will be written there.

VIII.8  17 – 21 SEPTEMBER 2026 — Complaints (Cadw / Stephen Doughty MP)

NARRATOR: In September the family lodge a formal complaint against Cadw, whose own address blocks it. They write to their MP, Stephen Doughty, asking for the Parliamentary Ombudsman. They ask Heneb, the successor to GGAT, for its files. A Land Registry agent tells them the title was 'closed in 2005', which the Registry's own history contradicts.

VIII.9  23 SEPTEMBER 2026 — The Lords papers (The National Archives)

NARRATOR: The National Archives finds the House of Lords papers in BP Properties v Buckler, added to its catalogue only in May 2026. For thirty-eight years, searches had found nothing.

VIII.10  25 – 27 SEPTEMBER 2026 — An offer on the woodland (Forest of Cardiff)

NARRATOR: The Forest of Cardiff, which holds the woodland, offers the family a route to owning it, on its own terms. They turn it down.

VIII.11  27 – 29 SEPTEMBER 2026 — Keep everything (Heneb / HM Land Registry)

NARRATOR: The family take the Land Registry to its Independent Complaints Reviewer, and complain to Heneb about GGAT's part from 1982 to 1995. A preservation notice goes to eleven public bodies: keep everything.

VIII.12  29 SEPTEMBER – 5 OCTOBER 2026 — On record all along (Cadw / RCAHMW / Museum Wales)

NARRATOR: The Royal Commission opens its archive: the 1974 newspaper report, and an old note of the soldier in armour under the dining-room floor. Museum Wales, after two refusals, releases its file on the bones found by the church in 1858. The story was on record all along.

VIII.13  SEPTEMBER – OCTOBER 2026 — The question (Great House Farm Wiki)

NARRATOR: The family put everything they have found in public: every document and every cutting, from the geese of 1891 to the bus of 1989. And they ask the question no court, no registry and no minister has answered. By what document did the house ever become BP's? No such document has ever been produced.

EPILOGUE: TODAY (Now)

E.1  TODAY — Who holds it now (Church View Close, Llandough)

NARRATOR: Today twenty houses stand on Great House Farm. BP has sold up and gone. The Land Registry says the title is 'registered correctly'. A thousand of Llandough's dead lie in the stores of the National Museum. The woodland belongs to a charity. The house is gone, and so is the heritage file that might have saved it. BP built the title. The courts upheld it, the Registry recorded it, Cadw declined to protect the house, and the state now holds its dead. Not one of them has said how it happened.

E.2  TODAY — Not in the history (Llandough Community Council)

NARRATOR: The village's own history page tells of the Roman villa, the lost monastery, and the hundreds of burials dug up in 1994. It does not mention the Great House, the family who lived in it for three hundred years, or the morning it was bulldozed. There has been no public inquiry. No apology. No reparation.

E.3  TODAY — What the family ask for (The family)

NARRATOR: The family ask for what was taken. A ruling that the house was theirs. The woodland and the green back, or reparation where that cannot be done. Reparation for the houses built on their land. The estate's artefacts returned. A true record of the Great House in the nation's heritage. And a public inquiry into how all of it was allowed to happen. Every body involved is being given the chance to put it right, before the family go back to court.
