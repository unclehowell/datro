TŶ MAWR — THE GREAT HOUSE FARM STORY
Narration script: 114 scenes in date order. Release v0.0.0.59, 2026-10-06. Source: https://greathousefarmwiki.wordpress.com/

PROLOGUE: TO 1857 (c. 650 – 1857)

P.1  C. AD 650 — St Dochdwy's church (St Dochdwy's, Llandough)

NARRATOR: St Dochdwy's church stands at Llandough, on the site of an early monastery. Great House stands beside it.
NARRATOR (the received account): A village church with a farm next to it.
NARRATOR (what we now know): One of the oldest Christian sites in Wales, and the ground beside it the site of what its excavators call "by far the largest Early-medieval burial population so far recovered from Wales". The heritage record states that the manorial courts of Llandough and Leckwith were held in the house.

P.2  12TH – 14TH CENTURY — Medieval occupation (Great House — north-east slope)

NARRATOR: In 1962–63 E. J. Beare reports pottery of the 12th to 14th centuries on the slope north-east of the house. The Royal Commission classes the house as sub-medieval, probably 17th century, possibly with a medieval core.
NARRATOR (the received account): An old farmhouse of no special note.
NARRATOR (what we now know): A house of the 17th century or earlier, on a site occupied since the Middle Ages, possibly with a medieval core.

P.3  1536 – 1793 — The lordship of the manor (Manor of Llandough)

NARRATOR: Llandough belongs to Tewkesbury Abbey until the Dissolution of 1536–39. Cardiff Records states that in 1543 Henry VIII granted the manor to Lord Clynton and Say and Robert Turwitt, and that by 1545 it was held by Sir George Herbert. The Western Mail letter says the Herberts held it until the Earl of Bute bought it in 1793; Cardiff Records places it with William Hurst and others from 1767, and says Llandough's history followed that of Cogan, bought by Bute in 1793.
NARRATOR (the received account): The lord of the manor owned the farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Lordship of a manor and ownership of a farm within it are separate interests. Over this period the Herberts held the lordship while the Vaughans held Great House as a freehold. The sources for the lordship are secondary (the Western Mail letter and Cardiff Records) and differ on 1767–1793; the Herbert estate papers have not yet been checked.

P.4  1552 – 1824 — Manorial leases (Llandough manorial leases)

NARRATOR: The National Library of Wales holds a Bute Estate bundle of Llandough manorial leases and agreements (D 219), dated 1552–1829 as a whole. Its catalogue identifies leases of "Cydfin Farm or Ty Mawr Farm, (107 a.)", dated 1552–1824.
NARRATOR (the received account): A farm let on ordinary leases.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The farm is on written record from 1552. The 1829 end date belongs to the bundle as a whole, not to the farm, and no event in 1829 is recorded. The individual deeds have not yet been inspected; they should show who granted the leases and on what terms.

P.5  MID 16TH – LATE 18TH CENTURY — The Vaughans (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: From the mid-16th to the late 18th century the Vaughan family hold Great House as the chief freehold farm of the parish. The lordship of the manor is a separate interest, held over the same period by the Herberts.
NARRATOR (the received account): One chain of owners: the Herberts, then the Vaughans, then Bute.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Two separate lines. The lordship of the manor: Tewkesbury Abbey, the Crown, the Herberts from 1545, Bute from 1793. The ownership of the farm: a freehold within the manor, held by the Vaughans until the late 18th century and by Bute from the early 19th. How the farm passed from the Vaughans to Bute is not yet documented. Parcel A's title descends from the farm freehold, not from the lordship.

P.6  1667 — The Williams family at Great House (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Mary Williams's statement (c. 1974) records her family at Great House from 1667.
NARRATOR (the received account): Tenant farmers who lived on someone else's land.
NARRATOR (what we now know): For more than three hundred years one family lived in the house where the manor's courts sat, beside one of the oldest churches in Wales. Generation after generation was born there, married there and died there. Mary's account is that her ancestors bought it. Whatever the estate's books called them, the Williamses were the people of the Great House. Not squatters. Not passing tenants. The family of the house.

P.7  1793 – EARLY 19TH CENTURY — The Bute Estate (Great House)

NARRATOR: In 1793 the Earl of Bute buys the lordship of the manor of Llandough. Early in the 19th century the Bute Estate also acquires the freehold of Great House. From then the lordship and the farm are held by the same owner. The manorial courts of Llandough and Leckwith are held in the house.
NARRATOR (the received account): Bute owned the farm, and everything after descends from Bute.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Bute held the lordship from 1793 and the farm freehold from the early 19th century: two interests, now in one hand. Bute owned the whole farm only until 1877. The house was the manor's court house.

P.8  C. 1840 — Great House Farm (Census and tithe records)

NARRATOR: Census and tithe records begin to describe the house as "Great House Farm".
NARRATOR (the received account): It was always just a farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The first change in how the place was described. Later descriptions, especially "the whole farm", decide the case.

ACT I: DIVISION (1858 – 1915)

I.1  C. 1858 — Burials at Church Farm (Church Farm, Llandough)

NARRATOR: Workmen at Llandough Church Farm find about six skeletons, a medieval spearhead and a spur. The bones are reburied by the church stile.
NARRATOR (the received account): A chance find, of no consequence.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The first record that the ground beside the church held burials. The 1994 excavation found more than a thousand.

I.2  C. 1870 — The armour under the floor (Great House — dining room)

NARRATOR: Family tradition records a soldier in armour, with his horse, a lance and a shield, found beneath the dining-room floor. The account is in a Royal Commission record (NMR 6048942) and the Daily Telegraph of 16 April 1974. No primary record of a find has been located.
NARRATOR (the received account): Family folklore.
NARRATOR (what we now know): An account on public record since 1974, never investigated before the house was demolished.

I.3  1876 – C. 1912 — The limeworks (Llandough Limeworks)

NARRATOR: The Bute Estate lets about 33 acres of the farm to the Llandough Limeworks, which work it until about 1912.
NARRATOR (the received account): Part of the farm let for quarrying.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The quarrying is the reason for the 1877 division, and its end in 1928 is the date the house passes to the family.

I.4  1877 — Division of the farm (Bute Estate Office)

NARRATOR: Mary Williams's statement records that in 1877 the Bute Estate sold the farmhouse, buildings and about 10 acres to Daniel Thomas, and kept about 9 acres. The Williamses remained as tenants of both. The deed is missing.
NARRATOR (the received account): Nothing changed: Bute went on owning the farm, and the Williamses went on renting it.
NARRATOR (what we now know): On Mary's statement, from 1877 there are two parcels with two owners: Parcel A, the house, owned by Thomas; Parcel B, the fields, kept by Bute. Anything Bute sells after 1877 can only be Parcel B. Her statement says the freehold of the house was to pass to the Williamses when quarrying ended. The deed is missing; the wiki notes that whether the 1877 deal was a sale or a quarry lease is still to be tested against the Bute papers.

I.5  11 NOVEMBER 1891 — Theft of geese (Llandough — court report)

NARRATOR: Three geese are stolen from "Mr. John Williams, Great House Farm, Llandough". The theft is reported from court.
NARRATOR (the received account): A minor court report.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Contemporary proof that John Williams lived at the house in 1891.

I.6  MAY 1897 — Marconi at Lavernock (Lavernock Point)

NARRATOR: Marconi's wireless trials at Lavernock Point, May 1897. Family tradition records that Thomas Williams of Great House carted his equipment.
NARRATOR (the received account): An unproven family story.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The trials are documented; the family's part is their own account.

I.7  1908 — John Williams (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Mary's grandfather dies. John Williams takes over the house from the Thomases and the fields from Bute.
NARRATOR (the received account): John Williams became the tenant of the farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Two tenancies from two landlords: Parcel A from the Thomases, Parcel B from Bute.

I.8  10 AUGUST 1913 — Birth of Mary Williams (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Mary Williams is born in the farmhouse on 10 August 1913.
NARRATOR (the received account): A tenant's daughter.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The owner of Parcel A from 1928 by her family's title. She lived in the house until her death in 1983.

ACT II: TENANCIES (1916 – 1948)

II.1  FEBRUARY 1916 — The 1916 tenancy (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Bute grants John Williams a yearly tenancy described as "the whole farm". The document has not been found.
NARRATOR (the received account): The Williamses were yearly tenants of the whole farm from 1916, and every later right flows from that tenancy.
NARRATOR (what we now know): In 1916 Bute owned only Parcel B. It could not let the house, which Thomas owned. "The whole farm" is a landlord's description of land it did not own: the birth of a single, synthetic farm that existed only on paper. Every later case was fought over that synthetic farm.

II.2  1928 — Last rent on the house (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Mary Williams's statement records that in 1928 the last quarry machinery left, and John Williams paid a final rent of about £4 to Alfred Thomas. She saw the receipt. It is missing.
NARRATOR (the received account): Nothing happened in 1928. The tenancy of the whole farm carried on.
NARRATOR (what we now know): On Mary's statement, the bargain of 1877 came due: the quarrying ended and the freehold of the house passed to the Williamses, and they never paid rent on it again. The wiki treats 1928 as the strongest evidence-based date for the Williams freehold. The freehold line of the house: the Vaughans, then Bute (by the early 19th century), then Daniel Thomas (1877), then the Williamses (1928).

II.3  1936 — Marriage (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Mary Williams marries Frederick Buckler, who comes to live at the farmhouse. They have seven children.
NARRATOR (the received account): Frederick joined the tenant family on the farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Frederick married into the house, which was Mary's family's. His later tenancy was of the fields only.

II.4  1938 — Sale to Western Ground Rents (Bute Estate Office)

NARRATOR: The Bute Estate conveys its reversion on the 1916 tenancy to Western Ground Rents Ltd.
NARRATOR (the received account): Western Ground Rents bought the farm, and became the Williamses' landlord for all of it.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Bute could only convey what it owned: Parcel B and its landlord's interest in the fields. Western Ground Rents acquired no deed to Parcel A, then or ever.

II.5  MAY 1939 — The 1939 tenancy (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Western Ground Rents grants John Williams a revised tenancy, with a plan.
NARRATOR (the received account): A renewal of the farm tenancy.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The plan shows the fields east of the house only: Parcel B. The landlord's own document leaves the house out. It is the last written tenancy.

II.6  C. 1940 — Twelve years (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: By 1940, twelve years after 1928, no rent has been paid on the house.
NARRATOR (the received account): Not an event. The family were tenants throughout.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Twelve years' rent-free possession from 1928 gives the family title to Parcel A by possession by 1940, in addition to the 1877–1928 title. The wiki notes this depends on what title the Thomases held. On either footing, from 1940 there was no basis for treating the family's occupation of the house as permission from Western Ground Rents, which never owned it.

II.7  1944 — Farm management (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: The War Agricultural Executive Committee is dissatisfied with the farm. Frederick Buckler takes over running it as manager for John Williams.
NARRATOR (the received account): The tenant farm was failing.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Frederick managed the fields, Parcel B.

II.8  C. 1948 – 49 — Birth of William Buckler (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: William (Billy) Buckler is born at the farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Mary's son and heir, who defended the house in court and was evicted in 1988.

ACT III: POSSESSION PROCEEDINGS (1949 – 1968)

III.1  2 FEBRUARY 1949 — The 1949 tenancy (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: John Williams surrenders his tenancy. Frederick Buckler's yearly tenancy from Western Ground Rents begins on 2 February 1949. It is not put in writing.
NARRATOR (the received account): Frederick became the tenant of the farm, and the family lived in the farmhouse under his tenancy.
NARRATOR (what we now know): An unwritten tenancy of the fields, Parcel B. Mary was not a party and rejected any suggestion that the house was in it; she then held the paper title to Parcel A. Describing it as a tenancy of "the farm" was the first attempt to make her occupation of her own house look permissive.

III.2  JUNE 1950 – C. 1952 — The blanket box (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Mary Williams's statement records that in June 1950 Mr Knapp, a Penarth estate agent, placed a lodger, Bruce Sutherland, at the farmhouse. He stayed about two years, and later told her he had taken "the papers which were in the blanket box including the agreement relating to the farm" and given them to Mr Knapp.
NARRATOR (the received account): If the family ever had documents, they could not produce them.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The papers that proved the family's title, kept in a blanket box, were carried out of the house by a lodger an estate agent had placed under their roof, and handed to that agent. It happened a year after Mary refused a tenancy of the farmhouse and asserted ownership, and within three years of the landlord's first court case; Mary believed the two were connected. For the rest of her life she was told to produce documents that had been taken from her. The title was never extinguished. Only its proof was taken.

III.3  1952 – 53 — Last rent on the fields (Landlords' agents)

NARRATOR: On 10 October 1952 the landlord's agents write that they will not proceed with a letting to Frederick. He pays his last rent in 1953.
NARRATOR (the received account): The tenant stopped paying rent on the farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Rent stopped on the fields, Parcel B. No rent had been paid on Parcel A since 1928.

III.4  2 FEBRUARY 1955 — The 1955 possession order (High Court / Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 2 February 1955 Western Ground Rents obtains a High Court order for possession against Frederick Buckler, by summary judgment, described as "the whole of the farm". On 4 July 1955 possession is taken of everything except the farmhouse and garden; the 1987 judgment attributes the exception to Mary's recent return from hospital and her strong objection.
NARRATOR (the received account): The landlord recovered the farm in 1955; the family stayed on in the house only through the landlord's forbearance, and from 1955 their occupation was adverse to the landlord.
NARRATOR (what we now know): An order against a tenant of the fields can only give back the fields, and that is what was taken. Mary was not a party and was never heard. She had just come home from hospital and stood her ground for her house. Any clock that started in 1955 was a clock on the fields. That the order reached the house was an insinuation, later read as fact. The family's account is that the woodland of Parcel A was taken in 1955 too, passing to a couple in Llandough and later to the Forest of Cardiff. (Family account, not yet recorded on the wiki.)

III.5  1959 — 1959 tenancy offer (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Western Ground Rents offers Mary a tenancy of the farmhouse and garden. She refuses, stating that the house is hers through her grandfather and that documents prove it.
NARRATOR (the received account): The landlord offered the occupier a tenancy, and she refused it.
NARRATOR (what we now know): "The farmhouse and garden" is Parcel A (farmhouse, buildings, yard and garden), less the woodland to the north and the land to the south already taken. The landlord named the house parcel on its own, which shows it knew it was separate. An owner does not need its occupier to accept a tenancy. Third attempt to make her occupation look permissive.

III.6  11 DECEMBER 1962 — The 1962 possession order (Cardiff County Court)

NARRATOR: On 11 December 1962 Cardiff County Court (Judge Temple Morris QC) orders possession of the farmhouse and garden, with mesne profits from 1955, against Frederick and Mary, on Western Ground Rents' claim. The order is not enforced.
NARRATOR (the received account): The court confirmed the landlord's right to the house in 1962, and that order kept the landlord's title alive.
NARRATOR (what we now know): A possession order, not a decision on title. It treated Mary as holding over from a tenancy she was never in. No deed to the house was proved, because Western Ground Rents had none. It was left unenforced, and in 1987 it was used to stop the clock. Fourth attempt.

III.7  JUNE 1963 — Committal proceedings (Cardiff County Court)

NARRATOR: The mesne profits under the 1962 order are partly enforced against Frederick, and committal proceedings follow.
NARRATOR (the received account): A tenant in arrears was pursued for the money.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The money was enforced; possession of the house was not.

III.8  JANUARY – MARCH 1965 — 1965 tenancy offer (Landlords' agents)

NARRATOR: On 2 March 1965 the landlord's agents offer "Mrs Williams" a weekly tenancy of the farmhouse and garden at £2 a week. It is not signed or returned and no rent is paid.
NARRATOR (the received account): Another offer refused by an occupier with no title.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The same offer as 1959. The estate's own records treat her occupation as adverse from this date. Fifth attempt. An owner holding a deed would sue on it; Western Ground Rents never did.

III.9  1964 – 68 — Reported sale by the eldest son (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: A cousin's account says the eldest son sold "our land parcel" through a solicitor without his parents' knowledge. No document has been found. In 1968 his household leaves the farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): What was sold, if anything, is unknown.

III.10  1967 — Death of Frederick Buckler (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Frederick Buckler dies, aged 56. Mary takes back the name Williams and remains in the farmhouse.
NARRATOR (the received account): The tenant died; his widow stayed on.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The only tenant there had ever been, of the fields, died. Mary remained the owner in occupation of Parcel A.

ACT IV: BP (1969 – 1983)

IV.1  31 DECEMBER 1969 — Conveyance to BP Pension Trust (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 31 December 1969 Western Ground Rents conveys its interest at Great House Farm to BP Pension Trust Ltd. The conveyance is not on the Land Registry files.
NARRATOR (the received account): BP bought Great House Farm. This deed is the root of BP's title.
NARRATOR (what we now know): By deed, Parcel B passed (Bute, Western Ground Rents, BP). For Parcel A there was no deed to pass; what passed was the 1962 order and the case against Mary. The root deed is now missing.

IV.2  1970 – 72 — Village green and planning permission (Planning office)

NARRATOR: On 16 June 1970 land beside Leckwith Road is registered as village green VG41. On 25 July 1972 planning permission is granted for housing on the site, while Mary lives in the farmhouse.
NARRATOR (the received account): Ordinary planning of an under-used farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Permission was granted over a house whose ownership was disputed, while its owner lived in it. In 1990 a condition of the outline permission provided for a 0.25-acre village green beside the church. The family's account is that the land south of the farmhouse, part of Parcel A, was taken by the council and laid out as a green in the 1980s. (Family account, not yet recorded on the wiki.)

IV.3  1974 — Royal Commission survey (Great House — interior)

NARRATOR: H. J. Thomas surveys the ground floor for the Royal Commission. The house "remained partially recorded because of problems of access created by an ownership dispute".
NARRATOR (the received account): A routine, incomplete survey.
NARRATOR (what we now know): A public body recorded in 1974 that the ownership of the house was disputed.

IV.4  15 APRIL 1974 — Open day (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 15 April 1974 Mary opens the farmhouse to the public. The Daily Telegraph reports her saying ownership has been disputed for 25 years and her solicitors tell her she has a valid claim.
NARRATOR (the received account): A campaign to save an old building.
NARRATOR (what we now know): A woman of sixty, who had lost a leg, opened her home to the nation. Hundreds came. A thousand signed. She told a national newspaper the house was hers. The public stood with Mary. It changed nothing.

IV.5  3 JULY 1974 — 1974 possession action (County court)

NARRATOR: BP Pension Trust brings a county court action for possession. On 3 July 1974 Mary pleads ownership and the Limitation Act 1939. Her statement describes the 1877 sale. The case is part heard and adjourned, and not relisted.
NARRATOR (the received account): An action that went nowhere.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The only time Mary's title to Parcel A was put before a court. It was never decided, and so never extinguished. Her statement set out the two parcels; no lawyer of hers ever built a case on it.

IV.6  SEPTEMBER – OCTOBER 1974 — The 1974 letters (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 19 September 1974 BP is given leave to enforce the 1962 order; Mary had not been given notice. On 31 October the warrant is withdrawn, and BP writes to "Mrs Buckler (Mrs Williams)" that she may remain in the farmhouse under licence. She does not accept.
NARRATOR (the received account): BP kindly let an elderly, ill woman stay in the house rent free.
NARRATOR (what we now know): A woman in a wheelchair, who had lost a leg, was told by an oil company's pension fund that she could stay in her own house under licence. She never asked for it. She never accepted it. It arrived while her claim to own the house stood undecided before the county court. Thirteen years later the Court of Appeal relied on it to give BP possession.

IV.7  19 NOVEMBER, 1970S (YEAR UNCLEAR) — Letter to the Western Mail (Western Mail — letters)

NARRATOR: A letter to the Western Mail, dated 19 November (year unclear), warns that demolition would erase the site of St Dochdwy's monastery and traces the land's descent.
NARRATOR (the received account): A local antiquarian's objection.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The chain of title and the site's importance were in print before the demolition.

IV.8  23 MAY 1975 — The 1975 conveyance (BP Pension Trust / BP Properties)

NARRATOR: On 23 May 1975 BP Pension Trust conveys property at Great House Farm, with a plan, to BP Properties Ltd. The Court of Appeal later calls it "the actual conveyance of the farmhouse and garden".
NARRATOR (the received account): An internal transfer within the BP group.
NARRATOR (what we now know): A conveyance of Parcel A between two BP companies, while Mary lived in it and claimed it, with no earlier deed to Parcel A behind it. It shows BP treated the house as a separate parcel. From here the synthetic Parcel A has a deed.

IV.9  22 MAY 1980 — Hansard (House of Commons)

NARRATOR: On 22 May 1980 Ted Rowlands MP tells the House of Commons that Western Ground Rents, "the most rapacious ground landlord" in South Wales, has been bought by the BP pension fund.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Western Ground Rents, which brought the 1955 and 1962 actions, and BP, which relied on them, were by 1980 the same group.

IV.10  1 OCTOBER 1982 — GGAT advice (GGAT)

NARRATOR: On 1 October 1982 GGAT advises the planners that occupation may continue north of the church and asks for investigation before development.
NARRATOR (the received account): Routine archaeological advice.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The warning that came true in 1994.

IV.11  19 – 30 NOVEMBER 1982 — First registration (HM Land Registry)

NARRATOR: On 30 November 1982 Linklaters & Paines apply to register BP Properties as owner of the land, title WA231076. Mary is living in the farmhouse.
NARRATOR (the received account): BP registered the land it owned.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Nothing on the file shows the occupier being asked what she claimed.

IV.12  23 FEBRUARY 1983 — Farmhouse title (HM Land Registry)

NARRATOR: On 23 February 1983 BP's solicitors apply to register the farmhouse and garden as a separate title, WA240304, on the 1975 conveyance, certifying they know of no question or doubt affecting the title.
NARRATOR (the received account): A routine second registration.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The synthetic Parcel A became a registered title. The certificate was given while the owner who had pleaded ownership in 1974 lived in the house. The Land Registry now held the fields and the house as two titles: it knew they were two.

IV.13  26 MARCH 1983 — Death of Mary Williams (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: Mary Williams dies in the farmhouse on 26 March 1983. Her son William inherits her estate.
NARRATOR (the received account): The licensee died and the licence ended.
NARRATOR (what we now know): She died in the house she was born in, aged sixty-nine, still saying it was hers. She never signed a tenancy, never accepted a licence, and was never once heard on whether the house was her own.

IV.14  8 APRIL – 12 MAY 1983 — Registration correspondence (HM Land Registry)

NARRATOR: On 8 and 12 April and 12 May 1983 Linklaters and the Land Registry exchange letters on the farmhouse file, including form D23.
NARRATOR (the received account): Routine registration correspondence.
NARRATOR (what we now know): What the Registry asked about the occupiers, and what it was told, is in these letters. The family have asked for them.

ACT V: BP PROPERTIES LTD V BUCKLER (1984 – March 1988)

V.1  1984 — The writ (BP Properties)

NARRATOR: On 22 May 1984 BP Properties issues a writ for possession of Great House Farm and adjoining land against William Buckler. The defence relies on adverse possession from 1955.
NARRATOR (the received account): BP, the owner, sued to recover its land from a squatter.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The house was wrapped back into "the farm" a year after BP registered it separately. BP asked for possession, not a declaration of ownership. The defence fought on BP's map: adverse possession of a synthetic farm whose real owner's title was to Parcel B only, with the clock counted from 1955, when the family's title to Parcel A dated from 1928 and by possession from 1940. In the same year the library copy of the 1877 deed was reported missing.

V.2  10 JULY 1986 — High Court judgment (High Court, Cardiff)

NARRATOR: On 10 July 1986 Mr Justice Hollis gives judgment for BP, holding that the 1974 letters gave Mary a licence.
NARRATOR (the received account): The court found that BP owned the house and the family were there by BP's permission.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The court decided possession. The question of who owned Parcel A was not put to it, and the 1877, 1928 and 1939 documents were not before it.

V.3  1986 – 87 — Royal Commission correspondence (Royal Commission (RCAHMW))

NARRATOR: During the trial and appeal, the Royal Commission's correspondence with Mrs Buckler goes missing.
NARRATOR (what we now know): A record of Mary's own account to a public body was lost while the case was live.

V.4  2 FEBRUARY 1987 — Amalgamation of titles (HM Land Registry)

NARRATOR: On 2 February 1987, with the appeal pending, an application is made to amalgamate WA240304 (farmhouse and garden) into WA231076.
NARRATOR (the received account): An administrative tidying of the register.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Two titles cannot be merged unless there are two. BP and the Land Registry held the house and the fields separately from 1983 while the case was argued as one farm. The merger folded the synthetic Parcel A into Parcel B during the appeal.

V.5  31 JULY 1987 — Court of Appeal (Court of Appeal, London)

NARRATOR: On 31 July 1987 the Court of Appeal (Dillon LJ, Mustill LJ, Sir Edward Eveleigh) dismisses the appeal in BP Properties Ltd v Buckler. It finds adverse possession from 1955, but holds that the 1962 action stopped time and the 1974 letters made the possession permissive.
NARRATOR (the received account): The courts finally settled that BP owned Great House Farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The court decided possession, not ownership. It counted the clock from 1955, from an order about the fields; counted from 1928, it had run out in 1940. BP's paper title was to the fields; for the house it had only a deed between two BP companies, which the court itself called the conveyance of the farmhouse and garden. Mary's documents were not produced because they had been taken from her. The family lost in the High Court, lost on appeal, and were refused by the Lords. Every defeat was on who may live in the house. Nobody ever asked who owned it.

V.6  3 MARCH 1988 — House of Lords (House of Lords)

NARRATOR: On 3 March 1988 the House of Lords refuses leave to appeal. The family are given 28 days to leave.
NARRATOR (the received account): The end of the legal road.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The end of the possession case. Ownership of Parcel A had still never been decided.

ACT VI: EVICTION AND DEMOLITION (April – December 1988)

VI.1  29 APRIL 1988, MORNING — First eviction attempt (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: On 29 April 1988 five bailiffs and about fifteen police arrive. William Buckler blocks the drive with a tractor and secures the house.
NARRATOR (the received account): An occupier resisting a lawful order.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Possession was being enforced over a house whose ownership no court had decided.

VI.2  29 APRIL 1988 — The chainsaw (Great House Farm)

NARRATOR: The bailiffs break the gate and doors. William Buckler pushes a running chainsaw through a door. After four hours the bailiffs withdraw. BP tells the press Mary had been allowed to stay rent-free because of ill health.
NARRATOR (the received account): The "chainsaw farmer": a violent squatter.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Billy was a father with his three small children behind a door an axe had just come through. The papers called him the chainsaw farmer. Nobody called the men with the axes anything at all. He was defending his home, on land his family had owned since 1928 and lived on since 1667.

NARRATOR: On 3 May 1988 William Buckler applies to the European Commission of Human Rights. On 12 May Alun Michael MP asks the Lord Chancellor to review the case.

NARRATOR (the received account): The lawful owner recovered possession.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Billy was hurt and taken to hospital. Eight people lost their homes with the family that day. A family that had lived in the house for three hundred and twenty-one years was put out in an afternoon, by an order that never said whose house it was.

NARRATOR: The family's belongings, including Mary's papers and photographs, are left in the house or put in the road.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Mary's photographs, the family heirlooms, the papers, everything left of three centuries, in the house or in the road. Whatever remained of the family's own records went with it.

NARRATOR: On 2 December 1988 a High Court judge orders that the farmhouse and its contents must not be touched. BP states it plans to demolish and build homes.

NARRATOR: William Buckler is taken from hospital and charged with assaulting two bailiffs, criminal damage, and wanton or furious driving.
NARRATOR (the received account): The violent squatter was charged.
NARRATOR (what we now know): He was taken from his hospital bed and charged. With Billy in hospital, then in the dock, then bailed away from the site, there was no one left standing between BP and the house. Within three days it was gone.

NARRATOR: From about 4am on 6 December 1988 the farmhouse and buildings are demolished.
NARRATOR (the received account): An old farmhouse of no special merit was cleared for housing.

A thousand people had signed to save it. An MP had asked for the case to be looked at again. Strasbourg had not yet heard it. None of it mattered by breakfast. The house was destroyed before it was ever fully recorded. The title to Parcel A was not destroyed with it.

NARRATOR (what we now know): Evidence of a house of quality, recorded only after it was destroyed.

NARRATOR (the received account): Strasbourg confirmed the eviction was lawful and the property was BP's.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Strasbourg relied on the British courts, which had decided possession, not ownership. It never saw a title deed. Another loss, on a question nobody had ever asked.

NARRATOR: BP applies for outline planning permission for housing (89/01396/OUT, received 8 November 1989). GGAT's 1989 planning report describes the farmhouse as having been listed as a "county treasure"; Cadw states it was never listed.
NARRATOR (the received account): Routine planning with archaeological advice.

NARRATOR: On 13 March 1990 outline permission is granted with 18 conditions. The officer's report notes the farm buildings "were unfortunately demolished a little while ago". GGAT's further comments of 8 February 1990 are missing from the file.

VII.7  AUGUST – OCTOBER 1990 — Evaluation trenches (Former farm site)

VII.10  15 MAY 1992 — Death of William Buckler (Llandough)

NARRATOR: William Buckler dies on 15 May 1992 in Llandough Hospital and is buried at Michaelston-le-Pit.
NARRATOR (what we now know): He was in his forties. He died in the village where he was born, without his home, without ever having had the one question that mattered answered. Mary's claim to the house passed, undecided, to his children.

STATING THE OBVIOUS: STATING THE OBVIOUS (Plainly)

O.1  PLAINLY — They were not squatters

NARRATOR: The Williams family lived at Great House from 1667 to 1988.
NARRATOR (the received account): A squatter was evicted after losing in court.
NARRATOR (what we now know): A squatter moves into a place that is not theirs. The Williamses were born in this house, married in it and died in it for three hundred and twenty-one years. They owned it from 1928, and by possession from 1940. Nobody who lives somewhere for three centuries is a squatter.

O.2  PLAINLY — Mary never asked for permission

NARRATOR: Mary Williams refused the tenancies offered in 1959 and 1965 and did not accept the 1974 letters.
NARRATOR (the received account): She lived there by the landlord's permission.
NARRATOR (what we now know): You cannot be given permission you refuse. Every offer of a tenancy, and the licence, was an attempt to turn an owner into a guest in her own house. She said no every time.

O.3  PLAINLY — Nobody produced a deed to the house

NARRATOR: No conveyance of the farmhouse parcel to Western Ground Rents or to BP, earlier than the 1975 conveyance between two BP companies, has been produced.
NARRATOR (the received account): BP owned the farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): If Western Ground Rents or BP had a deed to the house, they would have produced it at the first hearing and the case would have been over in a day. They used tenancy offers, orders, a licence and their own conveyance instead. People with deeds don't need tricks.

O.4  PLAINLY — They knew it was two parcels

NARRATOR: In 1983 BP registered the farmhouse and garden as a separate title. In 1987 it was amalgamated with the main title.
NARRATOR (the received account): It was always one farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): You cannot merge two titles unless there are two. BP knew. The Land Registry knew. The court read a separate conveyance of the farmhouse and garden. The only people never told what that meant were the family.

O.5  PLAINLY — Losing on possession is not losing on ownership

NARRATOR: The High Court (1986), the Court of Appeal (1987), the House of Lords (1988) and the European Commission of Human Rights (1989) ruled against the family on possession.
NARRATOR (the received account): They lost every case, so they must have been wrong.
NARRATOR (what we now know): They lost every round of an argument about who could live in the house. Nobody ever asked who owned it. You can lose a hundred times on the wrong question and still be right about the real one.

O.6  PLAINLY — William Buckler was the last obstacle

NARRATOR: William Buckler was injured during the eviction of 29–30 November 1988, charged from hospital on 3 December, and bailed on 9 December on condition he stayed away from the site. The house was demolished on 6 December.
NARRATOR (the received account): A violent man was arrested and charged.
NARRATOR (what we now know): He physically stood in their way, so he was removed: to hospital, to the dock, then barred from the site by his bail. With him out of the way there was nobody left between BP and the house. It came down within the week.

O.7  PLAINLY — Four in the morning was chosen

NARRATOR: Listing was refused on 5 December 1988. Demolition began at about 4am on 6 December. The European Commission application was pending.
NARRATOR (the received account): An old building was cleared for housing.
NARRATOR (what we now know): You don't start bulldozers at four in the morning by accident. It was done in the dark, the morning after the last protection fell and before Strasbourg could be heard, so that it could not be stopped and could never be undone.

O.8  PLAINLY — The public was on their side

NARRATOR: In 1974 about 1,000 people signed a petition to save the house. In 1988 villagers protested the demolition and an MP asked for the case to be reviewed.
NARRATOR (the received account): Nobody much cared about an old farmhouse.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The public stood with the family. A thousand signatures, an MP, a furious village. It made no difference. Where the public could not be persuaded, force and stealth were used instead.

O.9  PLAINLY — The papers did not leave on their own

NARRATOR: Mary Williams's statement records that the family papers were taken from the blanket box in 1950–52 by a lodger placed in the house by an estate agent, and given to the agent.
NARRATOR (the received account): If they ever had papers, they couldn't produce them.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Title deeds don't walk out of a blanket box. They were taken, by a man an estate agent put in the house, and handed to that agent. Thirty-five years later a court noted that they had never been produced.

O.10  PLAINLY — A decision with no file

NARRATOR: Cadw holds two photographs of 29 July 1988 and no other record of its 1988 listing consideration.
NARRATOR (the received account): Cadw assessed the house properly and it fell just short.
NARRATOR (what we now know): A public body decided the fate of a three-hundred-year-old house and kept no file, no notes and no name. A decision nobody can examine is a decision nobody can challenge.

O.11  PLAINLY — They never asked a court who owned the house

NARRATOR: BP's 1974 action, in which Mary pleaded ownership, was adjourned and not relisted. The 1984 writ sought possession, not a declaration of ownership.
NARRATOR (the received account): The courts settled who owned Great House Farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): In 1974 the question of ownership was in front of a judge, and BP walked away from it. In 1984 it asked only for possession. If you were sure you owned the house, you would ask a court to say so. They never did.

EPILOGUE: TODAY (Now)

E.1  TODAY — The sequence (The two titles)

NARRATOR: 1916 to 1987: a tenancy, two possession orders, three tenancy offers, licence letters, a conveyance, two registrations and an amalgamation.
NARRATOR (the received account): A landlord and its successors recovered their farm from tenants who stayed on without paying, and the courts confirmed it.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Parcel A was the family's from 1928, and by possession from 1940. It was never shown to belong to Western Ground Rents or BP. A synthetic single farm was described in 1916; six attempts (1949, 1955, 1959, 1962, 1965, 1974) treated Mary's occupation as permissive; the woodland and the land to the south were taken; the 1975 conveyance and 1983 registration gave the synthetic Parcel A a deed and a title; the 1987 amalgamation folded it into Parcel B. The courts decided possession and never decided ownership. Possession can be awarded without ownership being tried, and land can be registered without a rival claim being decided. Neither decides the older title.

E.2  TODAY — The two parcels in the record (The two parcels)

NARRATOR: Records of 1877–1987 describe the property both as one farm and as separate parts.
NARRATOR (the received account): It was always one farm, and nobody suggested otherwise.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Mary's 1974 statement describes the 1877 split; no lawyer of hers built a case on it. Western Ground Rents offered tenancies of, and sued for, the farmhouse and garden alone. BP conveyed it alone in 1975, registered it alone in 1983, and merged it in 1987. The Land Registry held two titles and joined them. The Court of Appeal read a conveyance of the farmhouse and garden. Those who knew the parcels were separate treated them as one where that helped and as two where that helped. The family's case is that this was a conspiracy against them.

E.3  TODAY — The site today (Church View Close, Llandough)

NARRATOR: Church View Close, twenty houses, stands on the site. The woodland is registered to the Forest of Cardiff. The human remains are held by the National Museum of Wales.
NARRATOR (the received account): A modern estate on a former farm.
NARRATOR (what we now know): The title to Parcel A has never been decided and never extinguished.

E.4  TODAY — Village history (Llandough Community Council)

NARRATOR: Llandough Community Council's page on the village's history describes the Roman villa, the monastery and the 1994 burials. It does not mention Great House, the Williams family or the demolition.
NARRATOR (what we now know): There has been no public inquiry, apology or reparation.

E.5  TODAY — The family's requests (The family)

NARRATOR: The family ask for: a ruling on the ownership of Parcel A; the return of the woodland and the green, or reparation; reparation for the houses built on the land; the return of the estate's artefacts; a true heritage record; and a public inquiry.
NARRATOR (what we now know): Every body involved is being given the opportunity to put it right before the family return to court.
