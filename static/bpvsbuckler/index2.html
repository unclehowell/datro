BP vs Buckler

This is to become the home of a chronology of the timeline of legal battle between BP and the Buckler Family of Great House Farm, Cardiff, Wales.

Introduction

Alongside St Dochdwy's Church, Llandough once stood Great House Farm, the site of a Celtic monastery and missionary centre, overlaying one of the major early-medieval monasteries of Glamorgan.

1st Century: N/A
2nd Century: A Roman villa was constructed.
3rd Century: Considerable development at the Roman villa. Occupied until the early 4th century.
370–640 AD: Burials commenced at the site.
4th Century: Christian villa chapels, though archaeologically elusive, are referenced in late Roman sources (e.g. Ausonius of Bordeaux, and Oratory at Lullingstone, Kent). Roman tombstones and cemeteries indicate early Christian presence.
5th Century: Traditionally, Saint Dochdwy associated with Cadvan is said to have come into Wales early this century. Parish church dedicated to this Saint, possibly original monastery site (some say founded by St Cyngarus for twelve monks).
525 AD: Council of Clermont (535) divided diocesan clergy: those of the city; those of rural parishes (later minsters); and those in villas (estate chapels). New churches required endowment; key feast days to be observed in city churches.
6th Century: Gregory of Tours describes rural churches founded by landowners in Gaul; possible monastic, Christian and villa continuity at Llandough. Archaeological ceramics indicate settlement with nearby Dinas Powys; Dochdwy is probable founder.
8th Century: Archaeologically evidenced by 30+ burials. c.1000–1284: Occasional burials found in villa ruins.
11th Century: Llandough, including the monastery & village, granted to Tewkesbury Abbey; a grange was established. Community and cemetery fell into decline, coinciding with rise of Llandaff.
12th–13th Century: Dovecote and barn overlay villa. Medieval houses use Roman stone. Notable finds include 12th-13th cent. house remains, and 13th cent. burial tile.
13th Century: The Williams family occupied the house; under a replaced timber floor, the remains of a soldier and his horse were discovered, reburied in the churchyard. Local traditions of cheese, pork, and monastic hospitality.
15th Century: Grange wealthy but by Dissolution (1536-41) lands confiscated and sold.
17th Century: Great House Farm largely rebuilt. Norman chancel arch in church. Churchyard walls comparable with church masonry.
18th Century: In 1897, Guglielmo Marconi stayed while conducting wireless experiments. Williams family helped Marconi reach Lavernock Point for first radio trans-sea transmission.
19th Century: Daniel Thomas (quarryman) involved in land swaps; Williams family assert ownership after his passing. Landownership and water rights disputed into the 20th C.
Archaeology:
  • Late Iron Age/Roman roundhouse, villa foundations, baths and barn found in excavations (1960s-79).
  • 12th/13th-century barn reuses villa walls; curvilinear and row cemeteries found, one focused on church site.
  • Post-Roman/early Christian cemetery: over 1,000 inhumations, diverse burial practices. See digital archive (ADS Llandough, Cadw 2004).
  • Detailed analysis: age, sex, burial positions, and body orientation. Rare finds: double burials, Roman artifacts, imported amphora sherds.
References:
  • Principal cemetery report: Holbrook & Thomas, An Early Medieval Monastic Cemetery at Llandough, Glamorgan, Medieval Archaeology XLIX (2005)
  • Palaeopathology: L. K. Loe, Health and Socio-Economic Status in Early Medieval Wales, BAR (forthcoming).
  • Archaeological records held at the National Museums and Galleries of Wales, Cardiff.
