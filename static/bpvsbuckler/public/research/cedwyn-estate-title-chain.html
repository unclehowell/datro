The Cedwyn Estate hypothesis

An affirmative, source-linked reconstruction of Cydwin / Cydfin / Cedfin / Tŷ Mawr and the Williams–Buckler claim to Great House Farm.

Working thesis · v0.1.0 · 17 October 2026

The proposition under investigation

Great House Farm may derive from an older named Cydwin/Cydfin/Tŷ Mawr holding, rather than a title history beginning with the later Bute estate. The Williams family's reported connection from at least 1667, the old lease records, the 1877 quarrying arrangement and the later corporate-title chain should be reconstructed together, parcel by parcel.

“Original Cedwyn Estate” is a research hypothesis about historical identity and continuity. “Synthetic corporate estate” is the family's analytical term for the later register picture assembled from distinct title strands; it is not a statutory category or a finding of fraud.

1547 — L 11

The NLW catalogue for L 11 describes a 1,000-year lease dated 20 June 1547 from Sir George Herbert of Swansea to Edmund Vaughan for a farm called Cydwin in Llandough, with named fields.

1739/40 — assignment

The same catalogue describes Edmond Vaughan's assignment to Thomas Mathews of Llandaf on 13 February 1739/40 for £1,066 13s. 4d. It provides a real transaction point in the older lease chain.

1552–1824 — D 219

The NLW Bute Estate Records D 219 catalogue identifies leases of “Cydfin Farm or Ty Mawr Farm, (107 a.)”. The individual instruments and field boundaries must still be inspected.

The lease is a leasehold interest, not a freehold deed. The names offer a strong archival path, but the exact identity must be established by comparing field names, acreage, adjoining tenants, plans, roads and watercourses. See GHF-E173, GHF-E178 and GHF-E179.

2. The 1667 family claim

Mary Williams's c.1974 statement says the family had lived and farmed at Great House since 1667, and a 1974 newspaper report attributes to her a claim that the family first acquired Tŷ Mawr around that date. These are identifiable family and press sources for the claim. The affirmative thesis asks what interest was acquired, inherited or recognised—not merely what later estate accounts called the family.

Primary starting points: Mary Williams's statement and the Llandough pre-1877 title study.

3. The Bute acquisition gap

The historical account places Bute's acquisition of Llandough's manorial lordship in 1793 and the Great House farm freehold in the early nineteenth century, with the Llandough and Leckwith manorial courts held at the house. The crucial missing control point is the instrument that transferred the Vaughan farm freehold into Bute's estate. A manor's lordship, a farm freehold, a long lease, quarrying rights and the title to an individual field are different interests.

The affirmative reconstruction asks the records to show precisely what Bute acquired, from whom, and on what terms. It does not treat a later estate name as a complete chain for every parcel inside a manorial area. The inquiry is to follow each parcel or interest through the D 219 leases, estate rentals, the 1824 map and the Bute acquisition documents.

4. William Evans Williams, Pwllypant and the 33-acre limeworks

The NLW Pwllypant title papers catalogue includes probate of William Evans Williams's 1870 will, described as leaving his real and personal estate to the Marquis of Bute. The separate D 258 catalogue covers papers concerning his real and personal estates.

The positive research question is whether any property or beneficial interest held by William Evans Williams connects to the 33-acre Llandough Limeworks block, and whether records show Bute renting, administering, acquiring or inheriting those same acres. The will catalogue alone does not identify the limeworks land. The family-reported Clock Tower dispute and newspaper coverage should be linked to exact dates, pages and words before being used to establish Williams's attitude toward Bute.

The Llandough Limeworks page identifies Glamorgan Archives Bute papers D153/4–8 as the retrieval lead for the 33-acre block from 1876 to about 1912. The block's boundaries must be mapped; it should not be assumed to be the same land as Mary's roughly 10-acre house parcel or the roughly 9 acres she says Bute retained.

5. The 1877 division and the 1928 claim

Mary Williams's account says the Bute Estate sold the farmhouse, buildings and about 10 acres to Daniel Thomas in 1877 and retained about 9 acres. She describes a quarrying arrangement under which the freehold would pass to her grandfather when quarrying ceased. The full operative instrument remains untranscribed; the related GHF-E115 plan and source-status page identifies the plan as partial evidence.

The family case is that quarrying ended and the final rent to Alfred Thomas was paid in 1928, bringing the arrangement into effect. That yields two title strands to test: Parcel A, the house/quarrying parcel, and Parcel B, the eastern fields retained by Bute. Compare the 1877 plan, the 6 November counterpart lease, Bute accounts D153/4–8 and X.6(12), the 1916 and 1939 tenancy plans, and the 1938 and 1969 conveyances.

The title-register history records WA231076 and WA240304 separately in 1982–83, followed by their amalgamation on 2 February 1987 while the appeal was pending. The affirmative “synthetic estate” question is whether the company register picture joined the Bute/WGR field chain to a separately registered house title without resolving the older Cydwin/Cydfin and Williams/Thomas provenance. The proper test is the operative deeds, registration files and plans read side by side.

Related pages: The Bute Estate, The 1877 Agreement, GHF-E121 — 1938 Bute–WGR conveyance, and the pre-1877 manorial evidence page.

Conclusion

The affirmative case is that the older Cydwin/Cydfin/Tŷ Mawr holding and the Williams family's reported connection from 1667 must be brought into the same documentary reconstruction as the 1877 quarry arrangement and the later WGR/BP register chain. The question is not settled by a farm name, a long lease, a will or a registration entry in isolation. It will be settled as far as the surviving records allow by reconstructing the parties, interests, parcels, plans and transfers in sequence.

Source-status rule: archival catalogue description, original instrument inspected, family statement, press report, inference and unresolved point must remain visibly distinct. The case advances as documents are recovered.
