6 December 1988 — Demolition of Great House Farm

As recorded in the Glamorgan-Gwent Historic Environment Record (GGAT02038s)

HER compiled 21 August 1991 · Last updated 13 October 2023

Primary Reference Number (PRN): 02038s
NGR: ST16707335  |  Community: Llandough  |  Site Type: POST MEDIEVAL Farm

On 6th December 1988, Great House Farmhouse and its buildings were "suddenly and completely demolished by B.P. Properties Ltd. amid considerable local controversy." This official entry in the Glamorgan-Gwent Historic Environment Record — the definitive archaeological and historical record for the region — independently corroborates the family's account of the demolition.

"The house and farm buildings were suddenly and completely demolished by B.P. Properties Ltd. on 6th December 1988 amid considerable local controversy."

"The plan of Great House was not published in the Inventory of Farmhouses & Cottages because of the incompleteness of the available information but it was classified as a sub-medieval house of direct-entry type."

— GGAT HER Record 02038s

Medieval Origins Confirmed

The HER record reveals that the core of Great House may have been medieval. The clearly-defined cross-passage end-chimney plan with masonry partitions and external wall batter had affinities with Brynwell, a medieval farmhouse in neighbouring Leckwith.

Pottery sherds of C12th, C13th, and early C14th date were found on the steep slope immediately NE of the house, implying occupation of the site from that period.

Medieval Ironwork at the National Museum

'Armour' found under the floor of the rear wing at Great House c.1880 — described in the HER as medieval ironwork found at Llandough — is now held at the National Museum of Wales. This artefact directly links the site to its medieval past.

The Vaughan Family: Chief Freehold Farmers

Great House was the chief freehold farm of the parish, occupied by the Vaughan family, a minor gentry family, from the mid C16th until the latter part of the C18th. Several memorial inscriptions relating to the family are preserved in the church and churchyard. In the early C19th the freehold was acquired by the Bute Estate.

Historical Timeline

  • mid C16th–late C18th: Vaughan family tenure (chief freehold farm)
  • Early C19th: Freehold acquired by the Bute Estate
  • C.1880: Medieval 'armour' found under floor, now at National Museum
  • 1974: Ground floor surveyed by H.J.T. in unusual circumstances due to ownership dispute
  • 6 December 1988: House demolished by BP Properties Ltd
  • 21 August 1991: HER record compiled
  • 13 October 2023: HER record last updated

Significance for the BP vs Buckler Case

  1. Official confirmation: The HER independently verifies the demolition by BP Properties Ltd on 6 December 1988, using the phrase "suddenly and completely demolished" — matching the family's account of a strategic, pre-emptive destruction.
  2. Medieval origins: The core of the house may have been medieval; pottery suggests occupation from C12th-C14th. This supports the family's narrative of ancient tenure predating the Bute Estate.
  3. Ownership dispute referenced: The 1974 survey was conducted "in unusual circumstances" due to "problems of access created by an ownership dispute" — a direct reference to the BP/Buckler conflict.
  4. Artefacts exist: Medieval ironwork from the site is preserved at the National Museum of Wales, providing material evidence of the site's significance.
  5. Documentary gap acknowledged: The house was not published in the Inventory "because of the incompleteness of the available information" — a further admission of the archival gaps.
