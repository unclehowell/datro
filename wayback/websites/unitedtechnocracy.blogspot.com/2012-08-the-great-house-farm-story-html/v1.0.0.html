The Great House Farm Story

=========================================================
Great House Farm (Ty Mawr), Llandough
A Historical, Archaeological and Family Record
=========================================================

.. contents::
   :depth: 3


Introduction
============

Alongside St Dochdwy's Church in Llandough, near Penarth, once stood
Great House Farm (Ty Mawr). The site was not simply a historic farmhouse;
it occupied a landscape recognised by archaeologists as having exceptional
continuity of human activity extending from the prehistoric period through
the Roman era, early Christianity, medieval occupation and later farming
history.

The history of Great House Farm is closely connected with the wider history
of Llandough. Archaeological investigations have identified evidence of:

* Late Iron Age and Romano-British activity;
* a substantial Roman villa complex;
* post-Roman occupation;
* an early medieval Christian cemetery;
* possible monastic activity associated with St Dochdwy;
* medieval occupation linked to Tewkesbury Abbey;
* later agricultural and domestic use.

The site therefore represents an important example of continuity of
occupation in south Wales, where religious, agricultural and domestic
landscapes developed over many centuries.


The Earliest Landscape
======================

Late Iron Age and Romano-British Period
---------------------------------------

Archaeological investigations identified activity predating the Roman villa,
including evidence interpreted as a possible Late Iron Age roundhouse or
farmstead.

During the Roman period, a substantial villa was constructed at Llandough.

The earliest villa phase consisted of a significant stone-built structure
containing multiple rooms, including evidence of a hypocaust heating system.
Later development included additional buildings, including a bath complex.

The size and quality of the villa suggest that it was a high-status Roman
settlement comparable with other important Romano-British sites in south
Wales.

Archaeological evidence indicates occupation from approximately the 2nd
century AD until the early 4th century AD.


The Post-Roman and Early Christian Period
=========================================

Following the Roman period, Llandough appears to have remained an important
place.

Archaeological evidence indicates that a substantial early medieval cemetery
developed at the site. The cemetery is associated with the early Christian
tradition of St Dochdwy.

Historical sources have long associated Llandough with an early Christian
community or monastery. While the precise organisation of the community
cannot be established with absolute certainty, archaeological and historical
evidence demonstrates that the area was a significant religious centre during
the early medieval period.

The excavations at Llandough revealed one of the largest early Christian
cemeteries excavated in Wales.


The Early Medieval Cemetery
===========================

Excavations undertaken at Llandough revealed an extensive burial ground.

The cemetery contained:

* over 1,000 recorded burials and disarticulated bone groups;
* evidence of long-term use and development;
* burial practices consistent with early Christian communities in western
  Britain.

The archaeological investigations demonstrated that burial activity continued
over several centuries.

The cemetery provides important evidence for understanding:

* population;
* health;
* social organisation;
* Christianity in early medieval Wales.


St Dochdwy and Monastic Tradition
=================================

The church of St Dochdwy has traditionally been associated with the early
Christian figure Dochdwy.

Some historical accounts identify Llandough as the location of an early
monastic foundation.

Archaeological interpretation suggests that a religious community may have
developed on or near the former Roman villa landscape, potentially using the
surviving structures and resources of the earlier settlement.

The site therefore represents an important example of continuity between the
Roman, post-Roman and medieval worlds.


Medieval Llandough
==================

Following the Norman period, Llandough became associated with Tewkesbury
Abbey.

The manor of Llandough formed part of Tewkesbury Abbey's important
possessions in Glamorgan.

A medieval grange was established, and archaeological remains identified:

* a barn;
* a dovecote;
* medieval structures incorporating earlier Roman stonework.

Some Roman villa walls remained standing and were reused within later
medieval buildings, demonstrating the continued importance of the location
over many centuries.


Great House Farm
================

The farmhouse known as Great House Farm (Ty Mawr) was largely a 17th-century
building, although archaeological evidence indicates that the wider site had
much earlier origins.

The farm occupied a location where layers of Welsh history existed together:

* Roman settlement;
* early Christianity;
* medieval monastic activity;
* agricultural history;
* living family occupation.

This unusual continuity is central to the historical importance of the site.

=========================================================
Part 2 — The Williams Family, Occupation and Property Dispute
=========================================================


The Williams Family at Great House Farm
=======================================

For many decades Great House Farm was occupied by the Williams family.

Mary Williams and her family maintained that their connection with the
property extended back through generations and that they held a lawful claim
to the land.

The family position has always been that their occupation was not as tenants
or temporary occupiers, but as successors to a longstanding family interest in
the estate.

The precise legal status of that historic claim became the subject of dispute
during the twentieth century.


Family Tradition and Documentary Claims
=======================================

The Williams family has maintained that their interest derived from earlier
transactions involving the land.

The family relies upon historical documents, including deeds and conveyancing
records, which they say demonstrate that their predecessors obtained an
equitable interest in the property.

The family position is that these documents were not properly reflected in
later dealings concerning the land.

The family acknowledges that historical claims require examination against
the formal legal record and seeks an independent review of all available
documents.


Early Twentieth-Century Dispute
===============================

During the early twentieth century, the family became involved in disputes
concerning ownership and possession of the estate.

The family maintains that earlier challenges to their occupation were
resolved after they produced documentary evidence supporting their claim.

According to the family's account, the dispute demonstrated that questions
surrounding title and occupation had existed for many years before the later
conflict involving Western Ground Rents and BP-related interests.


Western Ground Rents and the 1950s Dispute
==========================================

During the mid-twentieth century, ownership interests associated with the
estate changed.

In the 1950s, Western Ground Rents sought possession of the property.

The Williams family disputes the basis on which possession was sought and
maintains that Mary Williams produced documents supporting the family's
position.

The family states that possession orders were obtained but not enforced,
and that the family remained in occupation.

The dispute placed considerable pressure on the household and became a
long-running conflict between the occupying family and the parties claiming
paper title.


Mary Williams' Occupation
=========================

Mary Williams continued to occupy Great House Farm throughout the dispute.

The family maintains that she:

* openly occupied the property;
* publicly asserted her claim;
* did not voluntarily surrender her interest;
* continued to treat the property as belonging to her family.

The family further maintains that Mary Williams' disability and personal
circumstances created additional difficulties during the dispute.

These circumstances form part of the family's account of why they believe
the process should now receive independent historical examination.


Community Campaign and Public Attention
=======================================

The dispute attracted significant local attention.

In 1974, a petition supporting the preservation of Great House Farm was
signed by approximately 1,700 people.

Supporters argued that the farm represented an important part of local
heritage and should not be lost.

The campaign reflected both concerns about the family's position and wider
concerns about the potential loss of a historic building located beside
St Dochdwy's Church.


The 1974 Licence Correspondence
===============================

During the dispute, the paper-title holders sent correspondence offering
Mary Williams continued occupation of the property on a rent-free basis for
her lifetime.

Mary Williams rejected the characterisation that she was merely being
allowed to remain by permission.

The family relies upon her response as evidence that she continued to assert
a claim of ownership rather than accepting a licence arrangement.

The later legal significance of this correspondence became a central issue in
the subsequent court proceedings.


BP Pension Trust and Parliamentary Attention
============================================

By 1980, interests connected with Western Ground Rents had become associated
with BP Pension Trust.

The dispute attracted parliamentary attention.

During a House of Commons debate in May 1980, concerns were raised about the
treatment of residents affected by ground rent disputes, including criticism
of the approach being taken in relation to Great House Farm.

The debate demonstrated that the dispute had moved beyond a private property
matter and had become a matter of public discussion.


The Family's Position
=====================

The Williams family position is that:

* Mary Williams maintained a genuine claim to the property;
* the family's occupation continued openly for many years;
* important historical documents existed which required consideration;
* later legal proceedings did not examine the full historical background of
  the family's claimed title.

The family does not seek acceptance of its claims without examination.

Instead, it argues that the complete documentary record should be reviewed,
including:

* historic deeds;
* conveyancing records;
* Land Registry documents;
* court papers;
* correspondence;
* archaeological and heritage records.

=========================================================
Part 3 — Court Proceedings, Registration, Demolition and Archaeological Legacy
=========================================================


BP Properties Ltd v Buckler
===========================

The dispute eventually reached the Court of Appeal in:

    BP Properties Ltd v Buckler (1987) 55 P & CR 337

The case concerned possession, occupation and the legal consequences of
correspondence between the parties.

The Court of Appeal considered whether Mary Williams' continued occupation
could amount to adverse possession.

The court held that the circumstances surrounding the occupation meant that
the limitation period required for an adverse possession claim had not
succeeded.

A central issue was the effect of correspondence offering Mary Williams
continued occupation by way of a licence.

The court concluded that the offer of a licence altered the legal character
of the occupation.

However, the judgment did not determine the wider historical question of
whether the Williams family had a legitimate ancestral or equitable claim
arising from earlier dealings with the land.

The family maintains that the historical foundation of their claim was never
fully examined in court.


The Importance of the Court Decision
====================================

The legal decision established the position under the law as applied by the
Court of Appeal.

However, the Williams family argues that several wider questions remained:

* whether the historical family claim had received adequate investigation;
* whether the correspondence accurately reflected Mary Williams' position;
* whether the legal treatment of occupation fully captured the circumstances
  of the dispute.

The family accepts that the court reached a legal determination, but believes
that the historical record requires separate examination.


Land Registration
=================

During the early 1980s, while the dispute remained contested, registration
of the land was completed.

The family believes that the registration process raises questions about how
long occupation and competing interests were considered.

The family seeks examination of:

* Land Registry files;
* applications for registration;
* supporting evidence submitted at the time;
* correspondence connected with the registration process.


The Death of Mary Williams
==========================

Mary Williams died in 1983.

Her death occurred during the period in which the dispute concerning Great
House Farm remained unresolved from the family's perspective.

The family maintains that her death meant that the person most directly able
to explain the family's history and documents was no longer available to give
evidence in later stages of the dispute.


The Loss of Great House Farm
============================

Following the conclusion of the legal dispute, Great House Farm was
demolished.

The demolition represented the physical loss of a building that had stood
within an exceptionally important historic landscape.

The farmhouse had formed part of a continuous human story at Llandough:

* Roman settlement;
* early Christian activity;
* medieval religious occupation;
* centuries of agricultural use;
* generations of family life.

While the physical building has gone, the archaeological and historical
record demonstrates the exceptional significance of the location.


Archaeological Investigations
=============================

Later archaeological work at Llandough revealed the depth of the site's
history.

Investigations identified:

* remains of a Roman villa;
* evidence of medieval structures;
* early Christian burial activity;
* more than one thousand recorded burials and disarticulated bone groups;
* evidence of continued occupation across many centuries.


The Early Medieval Cemetery
===========================

The Llandough cemetery represents one of the most significant early medieval
Christian burial sites excavated in Wales.

Excavation recorded:

* 814 excavated inhumations;
* 212 disarticulated bone groups;
* a total assemblage of 1,026 recorded burial-related remains.

The cemetery provided evidence relating to:

* early Christianity in Wales;
* population history;
* health and disease;
* burial practice;
* social organisation.

The archaeological archive and human remains were subsequently curated by
the National Museums and Galleries of Wales.


The Relationship Between Great House Farm and Archaeology
========================================================

Archaeological evidence demonstrates that Great House Farm stood within a
landscape of exceptional historical continuity.

Investigations showed:

* Roman walls reused in later medieval structures;
* medieval buildings occupying the earlier settlement landscape;
* evidence of post-Roman activity;
* extensive early medieval burial.

This confirms that the site was historically significant long before the
modern property dispute.


Heritage Significance
=====================

The loss of Great House Farm was therefore not only the loss of a private
home.

It also represented the loss of a building connected with a landscape of
national archaeological importance.

The later discoveries at Llandough demonstrate that the location contained
layers of history extending over more than fifteen centuries.


Outstanding Questions
=====================

The Williams family believes that the following questions remain matters of
public interest:

1. How should the family's historic occupation and claimed interest have
   been considered?

2. Were all relevant historic documents properly examined?

3. Did the legal process fully address the difference between legal title
   and the family's historical claim?

4. How should the loss of a building located within such a significant
   archaeological landscape be understood?


The Family's Request
====================

The family seeks an independent and transparent review of the documentary
record.

Such a review should consider:

* historic deeds;
* conveyancing records;
* court records;
* Land Registry documentation;
* archaeological reports;
* heritage records;
* contemporary press and parliamentary material.

The family's position is that only by considering all evidence together can
the complete history of Great House Farm be properly understood.

=========================================================
Part 4 — Final Press Release
=========================================================


PRESS RELEASE
=============

Family calls for independent review into Great House Farm,
Llandough — a historic site where family history and Welsh heritage meet
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The family of Mary Williams is calling for an independent review into the
history of Great House Farm (Ty Mawr), Llandough, near Penarth — a place
where generations of family life existed alongside one of Wales's most
important archaeological landscapes.

Great House Farm was not simply a farmhouse. It stood beside St Dochdwy's
Church on a site recognised through archaeological investigation as having
exceptional continuity of occupation, extending from prehistoric activity
through the Roman period, early Christianity, medieval occupation and later
agricultural history.


A Site of Exceptional Heritage Importance
------------------------------------------

Archaeological investigations at Llandough have revealed evidence of:

* Late Iron Age and Romano-British activity;
* a substantial Roman villa complex;
* post-Roman occupation;
* an early medieval Christian cemetery;
* possible monastic associations connected with St Dochdwy;
* medieval occupation linked with Tewkesbury Abbey;
* centuries of later farming and domestic use.

The discoveries at Llandough include one of the largest early medieval
Christian cemeteries excavated in Wales, providing important evidence about
the development of Christianity, population, health and society in early
medieval Wales.


The Williams Family and Great House Farm
----------------------------------------

For many decades Mary Williams and her family occupied Great House Farm.

Mary Williams maintained that her family had a longstanding connection with
the property and that she possessed a lawful claim to the land.

Throughout the dispute she publicly rejected the suggestion that she was
merely occupying property belonging to others and maintained that her family's
interest had not been properly recognised.

The family position is that their historic claim, supported by family records
and documents, has never received a complete independent examination alongside
the full historical evidence.


The Legal Dispute
-----------------

The later legal proceedings, including:

    BP Properties Ltd v Buckler (1987) 55 P & CR 337

considered issues relating to possession, occupation and the legal effect of
correspondence between the parties.

The Court of Appeal determined the legal issues before it, but the family
maintains that the wider historical question of the origins of their claimed
interest was not determined.

The family therefore believes that an important distinction remains between:

* the legal outcome reached by the court; and
* the wider historical record concerning generations of occupation and family
  connection with the land.


The Loss of Great House Farm
----------------------------

Following the conclusion of the dispute, Great House Farm was demolished.

The family believes that the loss of the farmhouse represented more than the
loss of a private residence. It was the removal of a building standing within
a landscape later shown to contain internationally significant archaeological
evidence.

The physical building cannot be restored. However, the family believes that
the history surrounding it should be properly recorded and examined.


A Call for Transparency
-----------------------

The Williams family is not asking the public to accept every element of
family tradition without investigation.

Instead, the family is calling for an open review of all relevant evidence,
including:

* Land Registry records;
* historic deeds and conveyancing documents;
* court papers;
* correspondence concerning occupation and title;
* archaeological reports;
* heritage records;
* contemporary public records.

The purpose of such a review would be to establish a complete historical
record.


A Matter of Heritage and Justice
--------------------------------

Great House Farm represented a unique connection between:

* ancient Welsh history;
* early Christianity;
* medieval occupation;
* agricultural tradition;
* generations of family life.

The Williams family believes that examining the full evidence is in the
public interest, not only because of the family's own history, but because
Great House Farm formed part of a landscape of exceptional importance to the
heritage of Wales.

The family's request is simple:

That the complete record should be examined openly, transparently and fairly,
so that the true history of Great House Farm and the people connected with it
can be properly understood.
