    Welshmen everywhere will learn with I surprise and grief of the death of the Hon. D. T. Phillips, United States Consul at Cardiff. Mr. Phillips was in poor health, and the shock of the gas explosion at his residence proved too I much for him. Mr. Phillips was much esteemed throughout a wide circle. A Welsh-American, he thoroughly under- stood the people on both sides the Atlantic, and, whilst he did his duty by his own Government, he was thoroughly at home '.vith every social and religious movement in the Principality. This activity on the part of Mr. Phillips was not as well understood by some Americans as it deserved, and some years ago a New York paper said Mr. Phillips was sent to Wales "as a Consul and not as a mis-, sionary." But the Welsh people under- stood Mr. Phillips better than his New York critic, and they will be deeply grieved that an active and useful career has come to such a sudden and melancholy closet The United States has sent to Cardiff from time to time some excellent men. A generation ago there was the scholarly Wirt Sykes, whose writings on British folklore (illustrated by the facile pencilj of Mr. T. H. Thomas) are to-day a classic. Then there was Major Jones, whose name is a household word in the Principality. After him came Mr. Howard, a Bostonian, and a fine type of the cultured American at his best. Mr. Howard, like Wirt Sykes, had a keen appreciation of Eng- land, and hearty sympathy with our race. Next came Mr. Anthony Howells, a Welsh-American, who won for himself a host of friends, and now we shall soon be having a successor to the genial, hearty Cymro—for Cymro he was—who wan, Stricken down yesterday.     NEW YEAR TEETOTALER I have been a teetotaler all this year, sir," pleaded a man charged with being drunk and disorderly at Marlborough-streot Police-court. London, yesterday. The Magistrate joined in the laughter that greeted this confession. It turned out that it was the man's 268th appearance. He was discharged, and advised to try to show a better record of sobriety.
