    OUR LONDON CORRESPONDENT. The second Court which her Majesty held on Monday at Buckingham Palace cannot but be re- garded as another indication-slight in itself, but fery encouraging in connection with other events- of the Queen's willingness, ere long, to assume that public position from which she has been so long voluntary estranged. The Hag floating over the well- known palace looked cheering, and the carriages of the nobility reminded us of former times, when crowds used to gather in St. James's Park and about the Palace to see some of the Upper Ten pay their respects to the Queen at a levee or a drawing-room. I had not the pleasure of seeing Her Majesty on her arrival, but I am told by friends v/ho saw her that she looks in good health. Your lady readers also Twill doubtless draw their conclusions from the fact that Her Majesty wore a black silk dress, her cap being ornamented with large pearls, and that the Queen moreover" wore four rows of large pearls round the neck, and pearl brooches, the ribband and the star of the Order of the Garter, the Victoria and Albert order, and the order of Louise of Prussia." The ice may thus be said to have been broken, and it is now perhaps not too much to express the hope that her Majesty may soon see fit to take that promi- nent public position which will so much gratify her loyal subjects. There is nothing like a public scandal-or a semi- public, semi-private scanda!-to get up a scene in the House," or excitement out of it. The Edmunds scandal becomes more and more interesting and piquant. As the whole affair is now to be sifted by a committee, I will not further comment on it; but I must take exception in one respect to the constitu- tion of the committee. Among small committee surely it is scarcely fair to have four members of the Cabinet on it, considering that virtually the Cabinet have already sanctioned Lord Westbury's proceedings. Another marriage scandal in high life! This -ime it i? a contrast to the Yelverton case. The honourable Major repudiated an alleged Scotch mar. riage, which would have invalidated his subsequent I regular English marriage. Lord Huntingtower, on the contrary, who was supposed to be duly wedded to a lady by whom he has a family, adduces, as a defence to an action for arrears of annuity settled on another lady, that the plaintiff is his wife by a Scotch marriage. The [complication which this curious plea introduced into the proceedings on the suit would be very amusing, if it were not for the serious consequsnces to be apprehended to some of the parties concerned. The learoed gentlemen of the long robe were fairly puzzled for ooce, professing themselves unable to decide for their respective -lient On the part of the plaintiff, it would scarcely have been politic to have attempted the disproval of a marriage the establishment of which ought to be of more consequence to the hJy than the recovery of £ 15, one quarter's arrear of her annuity. On the other hand the nobleman, by making good his plea, would be setting up an ugly fact against himse:f. It would he rather an inconve- j nient thing," as the L)rd Chief Baron remarked, for a nobleman to be charged with bigamy." Any- how, sufficient, prima. facie evidence of the Scotch marriage was given to obtain a verdict for the noble defendant on that pica, subject to further considera- tion of the point of law by the learned Judge. The scandal of this case is certainly increased by the fact that the defendant represents himself as having separated from the plaintitf in iviS, shortly before his (second ?) marriage, whereas the lady swears that Lord Huntingtower is the father of the youngest child, born in ls«3. Coupling this with the belief expressed by the plaintiff's counsel, that the defen- dant would be very glad to find that the plaintiff was his real wife, in order that he might get rid of some one else," we may expect some curiously com- plicated litigation to spring from the matter, afford- ing a fresh example of the unsatisfactory state of oar marriage laws, and also of the loose morality of -too many of our aristocracy. The rage for company making has found a vent in a new direction. We shall now have probably a number of newspaper companies, limited. One of them was started last week in South London, which has already some eight or nine local organs." The new journal is to be under the patronage of Captain Hodges, the fussy private fireman the King of Mar- gate; the salamander; the haughty distiller of strong waters. Of course a newspaper starting under such powerful auspices as these must succeed Nevertheless, joint-stock company newspapers rarely pay. There was the Dial; of which we have heard nothing since it was so ignominiously incorporated w-Ttii the Star. There was the' joint-stock news- vendors'paper; and there have been many others. All failures. Somehow or other the joint-stock principle does not appear at all to barmonise with newspaper management. Despotism—constitutional despotism, if you will, but still despotism-is the oniy regime that suits a newspaper. It is probable that a bill will be introduced into Parliament this season for the compulsory establish- ment of workmen's trains on all railways entering the metropolis. Several vestries of the metropolis have already petitioned in favour of such a bill, and a hope has been expressed that the example of the London, Chatham, and Dover Railway in the matter will be followed. Consequent on this wish has come the rumour, that the London and North Western Railway intend to reduce their fares for goods. I hope the rumour may prove true; but why not -cheaper rates for passengers also. The fares on railways generally are too high, especially for the poorer cla-s of passengers and for goods. The in- tention of the Legislature from the very first was that there should be a cheap train at least to and from each place twice a day, and the workman's train is "bat a development of this idea. Why should not every line have its workman's train ? That on the London, Chatham, and Dover line is now proving itself a great boon. Some rather influential commmercial men here are talking over a project for establishing a London Chamber of Commerce. They have not yet made their views public but, entre eux, they urge the importance of such a step. They lay great stress, <Md it appears to me justly, on the fact of many pro. vincial towns having their Chamber of Commerce, and the metropolis having none, and point to the continental chambers as further illustrations of the value of the principle. There can be no doubt that these views are correct. There never was a more thoroughly commercially-minded Chancellor of the Exchequer than Mr. Gladstone, and no man of busi- ness can say where he will next lay his grasping and powerful band. With such a man governing the commercial destinies of the country, it behoves the mercantile and financial community to have a com- mon bond of union and to this end nothing could be better, so far as the metropolis is concerned, than a Chamber of Commerce which could make itself heard and felt in the Legislature as other Chambers of Commerce do. Mr. Spurgeon's Tabernacle has not long been li- censed for marriages, but the good folk who listen to his earnest, dogmatic, and somewhat erratic minis- trations are apparantly making up for lost time. There is quite a rage now, among a certain class, to be" married by Mr. Spurgeon." I have not had the honour of assisting at any of these ceremonies, which in truth are somewhat too popular for me, for the crowds which attend these hymeneal sacrifices are something alarming. What special attraction there can be in a Tabernacle marriage more than any other, I am at a loss to conceive, unless it be that the officiating high priest is on such occasions char- acteristically jocular. The position of clubs in London is gradually shifting. I believe I told you last week that Mr. Alfred Smee had been literally turned out of the Re- form, because he ventured to talk Conservative Liberalism to the borough of Rochester. The Old Garrick Club is, however, undergoing a total change. It was once the especial resort of men of letters and celebrated actors. Now this glory has departed. Several instances of blackballing have occurred lately, and have caused great irritation amongst a large class of literati. As I predicted somewhile since, Mr. Hannay has come from Edinburgh to take the control of the Pall Mall Gazette—now both a morning and evening publication. The famed Jacob Omnium is also placed upon the staff; but it is said that he also writes for the London Journal. Ex- tremes meet,and the extreme seems to suitthe public taste.

    CANTON LOCAL GOVERNMENT BOARD. The monthly meeting was held at the Board- room on Monday, Mr. Geake presiding, the other members present being Messrs. Glaves, Toms, French, Francis, Jones, and Yorath. After the minutes of the previous meeting had been read and confirmed, the Clerk stated that he had written to Mr. Waring, in accordance with the resolution of the last meeting, inquiring upon what terms he would undertake to prepare plans and su- perintendent the private improvements throughout the district. Mr. Waring bad replied, offering his services to the Board at five per cent. upon the cut- lay, and had also attended the meeting, to ofler any explanation which might be required upon the plans be had prepared. The Clerk also stated that he had caused the proper notices to be served upon the owners or occupiers of houses in Wellington- street, for the execution of the drainage, pitching, and paving works, according to Act of Parliament. Mr. GLAVES stated that from his own knowledge there were a great many of the ratepayers felt aggrieved at the resolution of the Board to carry out these private improvements at the present time. It was then announced that a deputation wished to wait upon the Board in reference to the subject. The CHAIRMAN suggested that as Mr. Waring was present, they had better decide whether or not they accepted his offer to superintend the works at the rate of five per cent. Mr. To-As thought it would be premature to decide the question of remuneration, until they heard what the objections were against the proposed improve- ments. The deputation were then admitted, consisting of about a dozen owners of property in Wellington- street. Mr. West, confectioner, and two or three other gentlemen, were the principal spokesmen. They urged that in their opinion the proposed pri- vate improvements were premature and uncalled for. For the last four or five years property had been ruinous to the owners, and now that things were getting a little better, and they were enabled to let their prosily at a slight advantage, the Board sought tu throw upon them the additional expense of pitching and pavi' g. They argued that so far as Wellington street is concerned, there was no urgency for these works. It was unusual in Cardiff to order such works until the street was finished, and as the street referred to was not yet finished, and would not be for some time to come, it was inju- dicious to put owners to the expense. Besides, the street, with the exception of that portion belonging to Captain Morgan, was in a very good state of repair, and no complaints were made by the tenants. They (the deputation) did not wish to be understood as being opposed to improvements; on the contrary, when things improved and the street was finished, they would cheerfully pay their respective propor- tions. Another objection was, that many of the landlords were not in a position to pny for these work*, and if executed the consequences would be ruinous. Mr. TOMS replied generally to the observations of the deputation. He said that when he made the motion he did it with the object of benefitting the landlords themselves, as individually he bad no in- terest in the matter. He did not, however, agree with the views of the deputation, for he believed the reason why low rents had so long prevailed in the hamlet of Canton was in consequence of the wretched sanatory condition of the roads and houses. So far from the improvements ruining the owners, he be- lieved they would be the means of increasing the value ot the property, and securing a far more res- pectable class of tenants. By offering objections of this kind they were retarding the prosperity of the district, and at the same time injuring their own property. In answer to a question, the Clerk read a section of the Act, from which it appeared that the Board had the power to borrow money for private improve- ments, and lend it to the owners for a certain num- ber of years at a reasonable interest. After a very long and somewhat irregular discus. [name] between individual members of the Board and the deputation, the latter retired. Mr. TOMS propose that those owners who desired time to pay for their proportion of private improve- ments be allowed three years, to be paid by instal- ments, together with a legal rate of interest. Mr. FUENCH seconded the motion. Mr. YORATH did not like the idea of carrying out these works against the evident wish of the owners. He believed, with them, that it was premature, and that it would be desirable to postpone the question for twelve months. He, therefore, moved as an amendment, that the question of private improve- ments in Wellington-street be deferred for twelve months. It was stated that the resolution to carry out the works had been passed, and therefore Mr. Yoratb could only give notice of motion to rescind it at the next meeting. The amendment was then put in the form of a notice of motion, for which Messrs. Yorath, Jones, and Francis voted; for the original motion, Me-srs. Toms and French. The question will therefore come on at the next meeting for final settlement. A letter was read from Mr. Henry Jones, of Clarendon Villa, complaining of the smell arising from Mr. Whiffen's candle works. He had written to Mr. Whiffen, who bad replied, and Mr. Jones sent the two letters to the Board along with a medical certificate, to the effect that the works were injurious to health, and asked that the nuisance should be abated. The members thought that Mr. Jones bad the remedy in his own hands, as the Act gave him the power to indict the owner if be fslt so disposed. At the same time the members expressed a hope that Mr. Whiffen would do everything in his power to abate the nuisance, which indeed be promised to do in the letter he sent to Mr. Jones. Mr. Henry Francis waited upon the Board to complain of a nuisance in the sewer in Wyndham- terrace. It appeared from what he stated that some of the occupiers were allowing sewage of a most offensive kind to go into an old drain, the outlet of which was closed up, so that at spring tides the effluvia was unbearable. The standing committee and the surveyor were requested to examine the sewer. Mr. YORATH complained of the practice of unau- tborised persons putting horses and other animals into pound. The other day a horse was pounded by some one, and before it could be got out 18s. had to be paid. He thought this a great hardship, as the horse had strayed out of a field. The Clerk said the Act gave the power to any person. Mr. Miller said that the horse referred to bad been pounded by his direction. The expenses were mainly incurred in its keep. Mr. TOMS was very glad to find that it was in the power of any individual to seize straying animals, for donkeys, ponies, etc., had become a perfect nui- sance, and caused much damage to gardens and other property in the neighbourhood. Several cheques were signed, and the meeting separated. ————

    FRIDAY, MARCH 17, 1865. GOVERNMENT ANNUITIES FOR THE PEOPLE. THERE are not wanting men of tolerably decent standing who look with distrust on this and other progressive measures. They say: "See! our representative system is falling into despotism. Power that used to be extended over a large sur- face, and in a vast number of hands, is gradually becoming concentrated. What for instance," say they, is the likeliest mode to begin a despotic rule, but by first getting hold of the people's money ? and this has been done by the Savings' Bank system." The nearest approach to despotism," they say, "is indicated in the Post Office system but gradually other departments will begin working 11 11 in unison gradually the web will be drawn more closely until things will arrive at such a pass that the next monarch will rule as despotically as the Russian Czar." We can all afford to laugh at these whims and crudities of men who look at the wrong end of a question, and invariably see it in its worst light. England will no more become despotic than she will become Roman Catholic. Civil and religious liberty, the institution of the press—the great exposer and corrector of abuses, stand gaunt breakwaters by the shore, and high as the angry waves may be lashed by the storm of ignorance and passion, these breakwaters loom out through the storm, and defy the violence of every wave. Man's destiny is onward-not backward. Just as it is the destiny of the oak to soar above and broaden out into strength and beauty, so it is man's destiny to tread onward—gleaning virtue and wisdom by the way, and at each step be- coming more impressed than ever with the power and majesty of the Supreme. Side by side with these indications, not of des- potism but of the growing liberality of a paternal government, freedom also grows, bigotries flee, 1 11 science levels distinctions, and the rule of phy- sical force is exchanged—slowly—for the rule of moral force and affection. Government annuities may be termed the ex- pression of this growing regard of the Govern- ment for the interests and for the welfare of the people, and it is coming into force just at the time when most needed. The working man is evidently becoming more convinced of the good of saving than in former years. A few years ago there were but few who saved. The old savings' banks of the country did but little business, and the mass of men lived in ignorant disregard of the morrow's requirements. Any observant mind can now see that a great change has taken place in the social arrangement and condition of things. Men are beginning to be more fully impressed with the necessity of saving for age, for the hour when the clear mind or the strong muscles fail, and bread winning becomes a toilsome if not im- possible task. So savings' banks and Govern- ment annuities sprung up, fulfilling the old axiom —where there is a demand there will also be a supply. Government annuities are specially in- tended for the working man. He must be a very poor man who cannot put by a shilling a week, yet this shilling secures his old age from poverty 0 0 or the workhouse. There is also another class who will be largely benefited,-servants, espe- cially servants in respectable families. When the employer dies the well conducted servant is generally remembered in the will, and as gene- rally the little legacy is soon misapplied or lost. C, By the Government system this little sum in- vested secures a pension for life after a certain age. The practice with Insurance offices generally is to direct payment of premiums by annual or half-yearly remittances. Working men would probably disperse their little hoard before they had saved sufficiently. Government steps in and says we take monthly payments. Looking at the question from whatever point C, of view we may, the conclusion derived is that it is eminently an institution for the people, for a class who lack the prudence which other classes have, and are more exposed than others to the attacks of swindlers and the seductions of rogues. And a class, too, who long ago deserved a greater C, regard and consideration than they have had. Our poor workers and diggers are the rank and file of our victorious army of commerce. They wither up before the puddlers fire; they are whirled away like straws before the demon of the mine. They are crushed under mighty monu- ments; are torn asunder, suffocated, and in a thousand ways meet death bravely like men. But the many keep on the steady tramp, and heights are won to which those of Alma pales, and deeds of labour and mind achieved that justly rise in glory and renown over those of any blood sprinkled field. It is for these-these hardy workers, that Government at length begins to exhibit a paternal feeling, and we should justly hail the scheme with gratitude and delight, as it promises not only to do so much good, but to bring about a gentler, more kindly, and more affectionate relation between the powers that are governed and those who rule.

POPULAR ERRORS.—A common error exists amongst the lower orders of the working classes, to the effect that Workhouse Infirmaries are places where poor people are legally murdered so as to save the parish expenses. When the Merthyr workhouse was build- ing, an old man pointed to the Union, the Infirmary, and the Cemetery, as necessary, the three natural steps a poor man or woman would have to take. This week another believer in legal murdering ob- served to a friend respecting a pauper, "He is very ill there. My mother was there also some time ago, but they didn't take long in finishing her-only four days."
