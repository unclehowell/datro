    Ige Cardiff Crates. FRIDAY, DECEMBER 30, 1864. A CHAMBER OF COMMERCE FOR CARDIFF. CIRCUMSTANCES are constantly arising which prove the need of tbeestahlishment ofa Chamber of Commerce in Cardiff. There are very ports in the world, doing so great a trade as ours, which have not some association of the kind. either under this or some other designation. Questions are constantly arising respecting which it is important that the opinion of the traders and merchants of the port should be authoritatively expressed. At present we have no machinery through which that expression can be uttered. This very week the question of a change in the postal arrangements has been brought before the Town Council, in de- fault of the existence of any other body which could be supposed to utter the voice of Cardiff on the subject. The Bristol Chamber of Com- merce had already spoken, and in due time to be heard. As the change was to take place on the 1st proximo, little could be effected towards its prevention, by a meeting held so late as the 26th. Here, then, vie see one instance of the need of a Chamber of Cornmekee-a body' which would be prompt to take notice of such matters as affected the trade of the port, which could meet, unlike a public body, at a day or two's notice, and whose recommenda- tions on commercial matters would possess a force which those of a merely civic board could not be expected to have. Another illustration of the benefit which a Chamber of Commerce would confer on Car- diff, may be derived' from the bills now printed and soon to be laid before Parliament for the extension of the Bute Docks. In all matters like this, the merits of which have to be dis- cussed before a committee of Parliament, the opposition or advocacy of such a body as we mention would be a powerful element in supporting or in defeating the measure. Last year the Town Council effected nothing for or against the Bute Dock Bill; its public du- ties in regard to the sewers and public pro- perty affected by the Bill, were found to so tie its hands that, with every apparent desire to aid the bill, it rather hindered it than otherwise. Then again there are numerous matters constantly arising in a port like this, which no individual effort can justly deal with. Sometimes we find an epidemic, of desertion among sailors. The magistrates can do some- thing to put a stop to this by severe punish- ment, in those cases where the captain is for- tunate enough to be able to apprehend his run- aways before his ship leaves the port. But in most cases of desertion the sailor has only to lie by until his ship is off, and then he walks forth a free man-free again to take some other ship's advance," and again to desert. On the other hand, the port is in-, fested by crimps, who entice the sailors to de- sert by men who steal their clothes and deal extortionately with them. Then again there are frequent inevitable disputes arising between captains and shipping merchants, as to the terms of bargains they have made with each other. In all these and many other contin- gencies, the services of a permanently existing court of appeal like a Chamber of Commerce, would be of very great value. Abuses could readily be corrected, disputes settled, and evil practices abated, by the public opinion and general sentiment "at the docks" being brought to a focus, as it would be in a Chamber of Commerce, by which such matters as we have mentioned could be investigated. At present, also, there is no agency (if we except the recently-formed Steam Coal Pro- prietors' Association) which has any concern in ensuring fair play for Cardiff in its neces- 0 sary rivalry with other ports. Changes the most detrimental in their effect on the future commerce of the port may be maturing, yet there is no agency to warn us of the danger, or to ward off the threatened infliction. Of course it is not our province to suggest any details for the establishment of such an Association; but clearly it is our function to point out that the port of Cardiff has grown to such importance as demands the combina- tion of its commercial elements into a form which can give effect to their sentiments, wishes, and opinions, on matters of trade inte- rest to the town.

    HOWELL'S SCHOOL-ITS HISTORY. MR. FALCONER, the County Court judge, has issued a pamphlet containing the history of Howell's Charity, out of which arose the fine edifice which crowns the heights of Llandaff. It would appear from his narrative, first, that an Act of Parliament has expanded the charity into something very different from what Mr. Howell intended next, that the Court of Chancery has shaped the charity differently from what Parliament intended; and lastly, that the Governors by whom the branch of the charity which exists at Llandaff is managed, j are carrying it on in systematic evasion of the intention expressed by the Court of Chancery. So here we find three degrees of divergence from the original intention of the founder. These changes were well meant, no doubt; but still they need to have public attention called to them, for though the Act of Parlia- ment plan was an improvement on the intent of the deceased Howell, it is more than doubt- ful whether the Court of Chancery did not fall below the intention of Parliament in its scheme, and it is certain that the Governors are making of the charity such a narrow, con- tracted affair as not even the Court of Chan- cery, much less Parliament, designed. The origin of the charity is as follows:- Thomas Howell, by his will dated in 1510, and made by him daring his residence at Seville, in Spain, bequeathed 12,000 duckats to buy therewith 400 duckats of rent yearly for evermore. And it was his will that the said 400 duckats be disposed unto four maydens, being orphanes—next of my kynpe and bludde—to thoire marriage-if they can be founde-every one of them to have 100 duckats—and if they cannot be founde of my Iynnage, then to be geven to other foure maydens, though they be not of my lynnage, so that they be orphanes, honnest, of gocde fame and every of them 100 duckats—and se, every yere, for to marry four maydens for ever. And if the said 12,000,duckats will, buy more lande, then the said 12,000 duckats to be spente to the marriage of maydens, being orphanes, increasing the foure maydens aforesaide, as shall seme by the discretion aforesaide of the Master and Wardeynes of the said House of Draper's Hall." The money so bequeathed was laid out by the Drapers' Company in the purchase of house property in London, which greatly in- creased in value. In the year 1559, some orphan girls in Monmouthsliire, claiming to be of the.blood of the testator, sued the Drapers' Company for refusing them the benefit of the charity. A decree was made that the Bishop of the diocese for the time being should be required to cer- tify whether applicants for the charity were really of Howell's blood. This was how the Bishops of Llandaff first became connected with the charity. In 1833 the Bishop pro- moted legal proceedings against the Drapers' Company for monopolising the charity. It appeared that the company were devoting £84 a year to four poor maidens, and appropriat- ing nearly £ 2,000 a year for themselves, besides occupying much of the house property rent free. An Act of Parliament was applied for, some years later. Among its provisions is the fol- lowing :-r- "That the Court of Chancery may extend the charity to the establishment, maintenance, and benefit of schools in Wales, for the instruction of girls and the maintaining, clothing, and providing portions for the orphan inmates educated in the schools to be so established." Evidently the Act contemplated either the formation of a number of schools in benighted parts of Wales, or a fund for the assistance and relief of poor schools in the principality. The fund then consisted of nearly £40,000 in hand, besides a perpetual income of over £ 2,000 a year. Quite a number of good plain school edifices could have been built with the former sum, and the teachers salaried out of the revenue. An enormous and much needed impulse could have been given to female education throughout the diocese Instead of this, the Court of Chancery drew up a scheme (or rather, we presume, ap- proved one drawn and submitted by the parties to the suit before it) which limits the number of schools to two, one at Llandaff and one at Denbigh each school to be managed by fif- teen governors, of whom the Bishop should be one, and the Drapers' Company should ap- point the others, and fill vacancies. There- upon the Governors erected two magnificent and costly buildings, and thus expended the whole forty thousand pounds in doing so. Now, it is clear that Thomas Howell did not leave his money as a religious, but as a secular bequest. He did not contemplate furthering churchism or dissent by his legacy. Nor did Parliament, in its extensions of the bequest. It was education in Wales, not de- nominationalism, that the Legislature in- tended to foster. And as for the Court of Chancery, it evidently did not desire, either, to restrict the benefits of Howell's bequest to the Church. The Bishop three centuries ago 0 had accidentally become associated with the bequest, and now had rendered a service to the charity by compelling the Drapers' Company to disgorge, which justified the Court _On in placing him at the head of the educational' institutions to be established. But at the same time the Court carefully provided for the admission of children of dissenters, and for their being educated as such, as well as for the admission of the daughters or or- o phans of churchmen. For though the Exa- miner is required to be a clergyman, and the matron and assistants to be Church- women, it is distinctly provided that no girl whose parents or guardians, from conscientious scruples, to be duly notified in writing to the Governors, shall object to the attendance of such girl at church, shall be required to at- tend and that the said schools shall be open to children of parents of all religious tenets, and no child shall be required to learn the catechism of the Church of England in case her parents or next friends shall express in writing to the Governors their objection." These provisions clearly show that the charity was not intended by the Court of Chancery, much less by Parliament, or by the Founder, to be a purely church one yet in practice it has become so. The child of a dissenter has never been admitted, no girl has ever been ex- cused from learning the church catechism, or from going to church. By a coincidence which seems extraordinary in the case of a purely j secular corporation like the Drapers' Com- pany, every single governor they have' ap- pointed, has been not only a Churchman, but one after the most straitest sect of that „ religion."

THE POSTAL SERVICE.-It will be seen that the Cardiff Town Council have agreed, to join with the authorities of other towns in remonstrating against the proposed change in the postal arrangements between South Wales and the West of England. The objectionable part of the change is, that the mail from London to Cardiff is to leave Gloucester before the mail from Bristol arrives at Gloucester. The post.office authorities, through Mr. Scndamore, an unaer-secretary, or something of the kind, have now, in reply to a memorial from the Town Council of Haverfordwest, made a concession which to a great extent will remove the evil complained of. They promise that all letters for South Wales which are posted at, or arrive in, Bristol, in time for the 6.50 train to Gloucester, will be despatched by that train, instead of being retained in Bristol till 12.45, as they are at present. These letters will then remain ia Gloucester about four hours aud a half, and will be conveyed by the accelerated London mail into South Wales. This is a decided boon as far as it goes. Meanwhile the up train to London will be sent off half an hour sooner every night, and the down mail from London will arrive an hour and a half sooner. Of neither of these changes can we complain. The only cause of objection remaining, therefore, is ill regard to Bristol and West of England letters for South Wales, which are posted at Bristol after the departure of the 6.50 train to Gloucester. For these will have to leave Bristol by the 12.45 a.m. train, which will make them too late at Gloucester for the down London train, and hence they will not reach Cardiff until the arrival of the North mail at 9.5. a.m. On Thursday, at the Bristol Post-office, a notice on the subject was exhibited, and as the matter is one of great importance, we subjoin it:—" On and after the 2nd of January next the mails for South Wales will be despatched from this office at 7.30 p.m. The box will be closed at 7 p.m. Letters received with fee of Id. till 7.25 p.m. On Sundays (commencing 1st of January next), the above mails will be de- spatched with those for the North.
