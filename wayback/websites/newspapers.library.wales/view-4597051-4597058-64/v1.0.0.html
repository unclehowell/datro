    STRIKE AT BARRY GRAVING DOCK. BOILERMAKERS' HELPERS SEEK AN ADVANCE OF WAGES. The whole of the boilermaker's helpers em. ployed at Barry Graving Dock and engineering works came out on strike on Tuesday last in con- sequence of their unsuccessful demand for an advance in wages. The men directly affected number about forty, and their demand was for an increase of sixpence per day. Mr Thomas Davies, of Cardiff, one of the men's Union representatives, had an interview with the general manager of the Graving Dock, Mr J. Lowdon, J.P., on Tuesday afternoon, but the latter gentleman declined to grant their request. The men on strike held a meeting at Andrews' Coffee Tavern on Tuesday evening, and after a lengthy discussion decided to return to work till Saturday on piece work, and at the close of the week they will again come out unless the demand is conceded in the meanwhile. Under the system which the men are working for the remainder of this week they receive an additional sixpence per day allowed for what is known in the trade as dirty money," and they feel they are fairly entitled to the' extra sixpence in wages which they are seeking.

    CARRIAGE ACCIDENT NEAR DINAS POWIS. DMVBR SURMED iENTO THE DITCH AND INJURED. -I At an early hour on Friday morning last a cab belonging to Mr W. Chick, Angel Mews, Cardiff, was being driven along the road towards Dinas Powis, on the return journey from Barry, when the horse became affrighted and in attempting to run furiously off the animal fell heavily on the frozen road, throwing the driver headlong into the ditch. The driver was severely injured about the bead and other parts ot the body, and was quite stunned. The carriage, too, was much damaged. The horse and vehicle were afterwards found on the road by some persons passing by, and on looking for the driver he was found lying on the roadside, and in a dangerous condition of exhaustion owing to the excessive cold and his injuries. The unfortunate fellow was conveyed to the Three Horse Shoes Inn, Dinas Powis, where he lay under kindly treat- ment till Sunday afternoon, when he was taken home. The man's name is Pomeroy, and on recovering consciousness he attributed the accident to collision with what he describes as a Barry boarding-master's trap, which drove by after midnight carrying one light only, the accident taking place in the narrow part of the road near the Arms River. The man was dis- covered in his perilous position lying in a state of insensibility on the ice by William Moore, in the employ of Mrs Harry, Southra Farm, and great praise is due to Mr and Mrs J. Rimron, of the Three Horse Shoes, for their tender solicitude towards the unfortunate sufferer during Sunday.

    RUFFIANISM AT BARRY DOCKS. TWO MEN SENTENCED FOR HIGHWAY ROBBERY WITH VIOLENCE. At the Glamorganshire Assizes on Saturday last, William Daley, 25, single, and William Doyle, 25, married, both painters, were indicted for robbery with violence at Barry Docks, on the 26th January. Mr Pepyat Evans prosecuted, and Mr C. J. Jackson appeared for the defence. The evidence was to the effect that an old man. an engine driver, named William Knox, of 23, Brook- street, was returning home through Holton-road, Barry Docks, on the night of January 26th, when he was knocked down by three men, whom he did not know. Subsequently the two prisoners dragged him into a back lane and rifled his pockets of two half-sovereigns, seven shillings, and other artices.—The defence of Mr Jackson was that prisoners were very drunk on the night in question, and remembered nothing more than that they heard a scuffle and saw some men run away, and then they found themselves arrested. The prisoners had hitherto borne excellent characters. —The jury found the prisoners guilty, and the learned Judge took into consideration their previous good characters, but sentenced them to nine months' imprisonment with hard labour.

O'Donnell, president of the branch, and amongst those present were the Rev Father D'Hulst, Rev W. Williams (the official Liberal candidate for County Council for the Cadoxton division), Mr 0. MoCann, Mr J. Mo'Donnell (secretary of the branch), Mr E. J. D. Irish, Mr R. G. Russell (secretary of the Liberal Association), Mr F. Walls, &c. The Chairman, in opening, extended on behalf of the meeting a cordial welcome to Mr McHugh, and spoke of the determination of the Irish party to urge in Parliament the claims of Ireland for legislative independence, notwithstanding the misrepresentations continually published in the Tory press as to dissensions in their ranks. The Irish National party, he maintained, were as determined and steadfast as ever in their adherence to the policy of Home Rule, and appealed to Irish. men all over the country to strengthen the hands of their representatives in Parliament. Dr O'Donnell ooncluded by proposing the following resolution:— That this meeting expresses its unabated confidence in the Irish Parliamentary Party, led by Mr Justin M Carthy, and pledges itself to support it by every means in its power till Home Rule is obtained, and cordially acknowledges the devotion of the Liberal Party to the Home Rule policy as advocated by Mr Gladstone. Mr O. McCann seconded. Mr P. A. McHugh, M.P., supported the resolu- tion in a stirring and reasoning political address, in the course of which the hon. gentleman dealt in a vigorous and exhaustive manner with the leading features of the Radical programme, and urged the uncompromising claims of the Irish party to Home Rule. Mr McHugh felt gratified in paying a visit to Barry to address a meeting of the local branch of the Irish National League and of Liberals, the friends of the Nationalist party. He was instructed to come to Barry on that occa- [name] by Mr J. F. X. O'Brieu, the secretary of the league, and he considered it was the duty of the Irish members to meet their fellow-countrymen as often as possible, and to impress them that the party lay as much stress upon the votes of Irish- men in all parts of the country as upon the votes of their own kith and kin at home, for in this way they would eventually have the voice of the nation generally in favour of Home Rule. (Cheers.) The Prime Minister, on the previous Tuesday evening in the House of Lords, gave them the assurance that Home Rule still occupied a place in the fore- front of the proeramme of the Liberal party and if they wished to see the cause of Irish nationality and independence realised, they mast do ai they did in the past, organise and Btand by the Irish party in the j °L ^°n"Q0l,8> and, until this question was settled, keep themselves absolutely distinct from any other political party in the country, not, however, in a spirit of distrust of the Liberal party, but to impress them with the determination of Irishmen all over the country to secure for their kinsmen at home that position of indepen- dence for which they had so long struggled. (Cheers.) The Irish party entered into a honour- able alliance with the Liberel party, on the understanding that Home Rule would be kept in the forefront of the Liberal programme, and he was of opinion that the Liberal party would perform its part honourably. In his opinion, there was a close relationship between the people of Ireland and the people of Wales, and he felt that none had proved more faithful to the Irish party in the House of Commons than the Welsh members. The Welsh members stood by the Irish members in the struggle for their cause, and the Irish members would now in return stand by their friends in the present movement for the disestabliehment and disendowment of the State Church in Wales. (Cheers.) While they felt that^ the interests of the Irish party were pre* dominant, still they were quite willing to auow the Welsh Bill to be considered, and the Irish members would help the Government to pass the Disestablishment Bill this session, and in return he was confident they would receive the assistance of the Welsh members to pass useful measures of reform for Ireland. (Cheers.) The Irish people had led the way in the matter of British legis- lation. The Irish Church had been disestablished, and now a Bill for a similar measure for Wales was being introduced. The Irish members had introduced a Home Rule agitation, and he was glad to find that one of the members for Wales (Mr Burnie) had given notice of a similar measure for the Principality. (Hear, hear.) He claimed, therefore, the Irish party should have precedence in the matter of the settlement of the question of legislative independence for Ireland. (Cheers.) Mr McHugh, referring to the smallness of the majority of the Government in the House of Commons on the previous night, said he admitted it was small, but it was quite sum. oient, and the bulk of the Irish party would stand by the Liberal party, in spite of the Tories, the Redmondites, and the Unionists. (Cheers.) In referring to the measures which occupied a prominent place on the pro- gramme of the Liberal party, namely, Welsh Disestablishment, the Amendment of the Land Acts for Ireland, and the amendment of the Sufferage System, the hon. gentleman said he hoped there would be introduced into the latter measure of One Man One Vote a clause which would enable a second ballot to be taken in the case of three-cornered fights, and advocated a shorter period of registration qualification, thus giving the poor equal chance with the rich in the matter of the exercise of imperial sufferage. The motto of the Irish Party was "Ireland for all," and if they had not been satisfied that the Government were sincere in their promises they would not come back from Ireland to vote for reforms for the benefit of England, Scotland, and Wales. (Cheers.) Mr McHugh referred to the Welsh Land agitation, and said that in this respect, too, the Welsh people were following the example set by Ireland some years ago. There were 7,500 evicted tenants in Ireland, and simply because they joined in an agricultural trades' unionism they were cast ignominiously into prison under the terms of the cruel and despotic Coercion Act for which Mr Balfour was responsible. (Hisses.) He (the hon. gentleman) had himself experienced a little of Mr Balfour's tyranny, and under the same Act had been lodged in the prison of which he was a visiting justice in the town of which he was mayor; but when liberated he he was a visiting justice in the town of which he was mayor; but when liberated he felt more convinced than ever of the justice of the cause of which he was an exponent. (Ap- plause.) He felt it was a great anomaly that a system of boycotting, which was permitted in London, was declared illegal in Ireland. Criticis- ing the House of Lords, Mr McHugh said Mr Chamberlain, consistently with the traditions of his political career now, strongly supported a system which some yeard ago he vigorously opposed; and added that the House of Lordlr sitting an it was as a kind of Tory Committee, prevented the country from being properly governed. The Irish party were satisfied with the terms of Mr Gladstone's Home Rule Bill, and nothing short of this would they accept, and until the Home Rule Bill was passed the Irish party would block legislation for the country. (Cheers.) Local Veto, Unification of London, and other measures which had been suggested were, not, he considered, urgent questions, and it would not be fair to ask the Irish party to allow precedence to be given to measures of this description over the question of Home Rule, (Cheers.) Mr McHugh described as a base calumny the accusatioki that the Catholic party would persecute their Protec- tant neighbours under a system of Home Rule, and maintained, on the contrary, that at the present moment an almost intolerable system of persecution was being indulged in towards Catholics by Protestants, notably in his native country. The hon. gentleman concluded a highly practical political address with an appeal to his fellow-countrymen in the Barry district to join theftnks of the National League, and tbus assist in advancing the claims of their native country for Home Rule. (Cheers.) The resolution was put to the meeting, and carried unanimously. Mr J. McDonnell proposed a vote of thanks to Mr McHugh for his admirable and instructive address, and expressed a hope that it would have the effect of combining his fellow-countrymen in this district and inducing them to join the branch of the National League to help the party which Mr McHugh so ably represented on that occasion. (Cheers.) The Rev W. Williams seconded, and having complimented Mr McHugh upon his excellent address, said he felt in entire sympathy with the remarks of the hon. gentleman as to Home Rule and other political questions dealt with in his address. Mr McHugh had stated that the Irish party had led the way in great reforms, and he (Mr Williams) assured Mr McHugh the Welsh were determined to follow, and he was glad that the Irish party had decided to assist the [Government in securing the passing of the Dises- tablishment Bill. The speaker also referred to the need for unity, and commented upon the necessity for religious equality. The motion having been passed {with acclama- tion, Mr McHugh, M.P., in returning thanks, said that the term Protestant, which applied to the ascendency class in Ireland, would not apply to the Protestants or Puritans of the Principality. He considered that a better term for the Protestant he had referred te would be Tory Orangemen. He concurred with the Rev W. Williams that religion was a question which should be left between matt and his God, and assured his hearers that the Irish Nationalists did not object to any person in Ireland holding office on account of religion. As a matter of fact, a number of members of the Irish party were conspicuous Protestants, notably Mr Jordan, Mr Knox, Captain Donovan, and the late Mr Parnell. Having reiterated the intention of the Irish party to support the measure for Welsh disestablishment, Mr McHugh said the Irish members, though poor in some respects, were rich in the confidence of the people whom they represented. (Cheers.) The hon. gentleman, referring to the Independent Labour Party with Mr Keir Hardie at their head, said there was no doubt the Home Rule and Liberal party would suffer considerably at the next general election owing to the attitude of this new industrial organisation, and he would ask the members of this party was it to their interest to strike against the Liberal party and the Govern- ment that had up to the present initiated and carried out all the measures for the alleviation of distress and the amelioration of the condition of the artisans and working classes generally of the United Kingdom, and had done more to raise the dignity of labour than any previous Govern- ment. (Cheers.) The Liberal party were the driving force of reforms in the country, and he failed, therefore, to understand the extraordinary attitude taken up by the Independent Laboar Party. Mr Keir Hardie, however, was not a leader of labour in the House of Commons, and he was confident his friend Mr John Burns would not countenance the policy of the new movement. (Cheers.) Whatever the Independent Labour Party may do he (Mr McHugh) might assure the country that the Irish party were determined to stand, and fight and defeat any party-whether Liberal, or Tory, or Labour-that would attempt to prevent the passing of salutary reforms for Ireland. (Loud applause.) The meeting closed with a vote of thanks to the chairman for presiding.
