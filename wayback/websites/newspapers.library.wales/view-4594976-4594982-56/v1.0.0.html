Through- out the whole of this unfortunate business the directors were careful not to issue sparkling and vile manifestoes, and they strove to avoid doing anything which could ruffle the temper of the men. Their conduct in this respect had been greatly misconstrued, and many people outside had come to hasty and prompt conclusions as to what turn the strike ought to have taken and what the directors ought to have done. They sat on many occasions for hours together, and he was very much startled to find people putting him right in two minutes on points which they had taken seven or eight hours to discuss. He thought a very wise and happy settlement had been arrived at. (Hear, hear.) There were those who thought they had now finished with strikes, but he was sorry to say he did not share that view. How. ever, the shareholders might rely on the directors, should any such emergency arise, doing all they could to protect the interests of the company, and meet the difficulty in the best spirit they could. He thanked them for their kind expressions. (Applause.) This concluded the meeting.

    THE LATE MR DAVID DAVIES, LLANDINAM. BEFITTING TRIBUTE BY WELSH CAL- VINISTIC METHODISTS. At the quarterly meetings of the Smith Wales and Monmouthshire Calvinistic Methodist Associ- ation, held at, Tregaron, on Thursday week last, the Rev Alderman Aaron Davies, Pontlottyn, presiding, the Rev J. Cynddylan Jones, D.D., by special request made feeling references to the loss sustained by the death of Mr David Davies, Llan- dinam. The deceased, Mr Jones went on to say, was an example of the verse, The hand of the diligent maketh rich," but what was special to him was that he was about the first in Wales to give embodiment to the idea of the uconsecration, of wealth "—its consecration to the services of religion. Besides, he was a total abstainer from the beginning of life to the close, and if the rich men of Wales imitated him in this, instead ofaell- ing their estates, they, like him, would be able to buy estates. But the chief feature on which they ought to dwell as a religious body was the religi- ousness ef his character. He was essentially a good man, walking humbly with his God, and showing.benevolence to men, and weeks before his end came he was resigned to the Divine will, having released his hold on this world, and, strange to say, his conversation at the last was chiefly in the Welsh language, Welsh verses, and Welsh hymns, showing that the first instincts of the spiritual man were rising above aU his surround*- ings and speaking the languaga, by .which.he probably found everlasting life. He (the speaker) would propose that a vote of condolence be passed with Mrs Davies and the family in their bereave- ment.—The Rev Thomas Rees (Merthyr) seconded the vote, which was unanimously agreed to. At the quarterly meeting of the Montgomery- shire County Council, held on Friday last, the chairman (Mr Humphreys-Owen, president of the North Wales Liberal Federation) referred to the death of Mr David Davies, late member for Cardigan. They all respected an d were proud of him as a Montgomeryshire man bred and born, who, by his great abilities, un- varying kindness, and sterling integrity, had won < the esteem and admiration of all who knew him. It was a great thing to see a man from the humblest classes raising himself by his own un- aided qualities to so high a position. They could not hope to have many such men, but he hoped that Montgomeryshire and Wales would, not be wanting in men of the same capacity, the same integrity, the same love for their country. He moved that the Council expressed its regret at the loss which it had sustained, and that this ex- pression of regret should be forwarded to the family.—Mr John Jenkins, Llanidloes (vice-chair- man), seconded the motion as an intimfate friend of Mr Davies, and referred to the great mineral discoveries made in South Wales by Mr Davies.— The motion was supported by Mr H. Howell (a shareholder of the Barry Company), and carried unanimously.

    ROBBERY OF FOWLS AND APPLES AT LLANDOUGH. THE OFFENDER JUSTLY PUNISHED. At Penarth Police Court, on Monday last (before Mr. James Ware, in the chair, and Col. Gutherie), Frederick Jones, a farm labourer, was charged, under arrest, with stealing fowls and apples belonging to his employer (Mr. Evan Rees, farmer, Llandough), on the 14th instant. P.C. Charles Gregory (74), a member of the Cardiff Borough Police Force, said he was on duty near the Custom House Bridge, Cardiff, about half-past one o'clock on the morning of the 14th inst., when he saw the prisoner coming from the direction of Grangetown. He had a bag on his shoulder. The conatSble questioned the man as to what he had in the bag, and he said clothes. Gregory proceeded to search the bag, and took out a quilt. The accused then dropped the bag and ran away. The constable went in pursuit, and caught him, and at the bottom of the bag he found seven fowls, which were quite warm, having just been killed. In prisoner's pockets he found a quantity of apples. Jones was consequently de- tained in custody. Eliza White said she was in service with Mr. Evan Rees, Llandough. The quilt produced belonged to her master. It bad been lent to the prisoner about a fortnight ago in order to lie on in an outhouse on the farm. The fowls produced were also her master's. They were missed on the 14th instant. Inspector King stated that when charged at Cardiff Police Station, prisoner admitted the offence. P.C. Charles H. Morgan said the apples produced were similar to those he had picked up under the tree from which the stolen apples had been taken. Mr. Evan Rees gave evidence to show that prisoner had been in his employ as labourer. He missed a number of fowls from his farm similar to those produced, which he valued at 16s. Prisoner had no questions to ask, and pleaded guilty to the charge. Mr. Ware said the case was a very bad one, and the Bench sent Jones to prison for two months with hard labour, without the option of a fine.

    BARRY RAILWAY PORTERS AND THEIR WAGES. FAVOURABLE INTERVIEW WITH THE! GENERAL MANAGER. On Monday afternoon last Mr Edward Harford had an interview at the General Offices, Barry Dock, with Mr R. Evans, the general manager, respecting the conditions of labour at present exis,tingon the line with respect to the Sunday duty of the porters. The number of men required to work on the Barry Railway is very small, but hitherto there has been no recognition of the fact that they are entitled to extra remunera- tion for such service. Mr Evans, we are given to understand, received the suggestions of Mr Har- ford on this point very favourably, and intimated hit intention of further inquiring into the subject with a view to increasing the wages of those porters who make a week of seven days.     A GOOD STORY OF AN ARABIAN LAWYER. When I was. in Arabia," said Alexander Kbnta, whose home is under his hat," I'was told, a story which illustrates the resources of the Arabian lawyer, and which may offer somesugges- tion to the members of the legal profession in this country. An English merchant, staying at an inn in one of the smaller towns, placed on deposit with the landlord JB500. He neglected to take a receipt for the money, and when a few weeks' later, having been on a short journey, he had occasion to ask for it, the landlord opehed his eyes with well-feigned astonishment and pretended to have forgotten the transaction. The Englishman was naturally enraged, but he had no proof or no witness, and in his extremity he sought the advice of a native lawyer. The wise man of the east re- flected a moment, and then gravely said: 'Entrust another JB500 to the keeping of the landlord, but, be sure you have a witness on hand.' The Englishman was puzzled to know how this would help him, but he did as he was directed. Now,' said the lawyer the next day, go and claim the money, but don't take your witness with you.' This was done, and the innkeeper, ill-pre- pared to practice the same deceit the second time, handed over the notes. The traveller once more sought his legal advisor, who said to him, It is well. Approaah your host again, accompanied by your witness, and demand your money.' The Englishman followed his instructions. But I gave you your money,' protested his host. Come, now,' replied the merchant, 'here is a man who was present at the time. He will tell you he was not a witness of what you claim. He saw me hand you the money. Be quick, please I must leave the city.' The landlord was outwitted. He saw the trap, but it was too late, and without more ado he made good the sum."

    SERIOUS ILLNESS OF THE REV. GLANFFRWD THOMAS. 7- That brilliant and popular Welsh poet, the Rev. Glanffrwd Thomas, whose dangerous illness has caused so much regret throughout the Principality, is now stated on medical authority to be in a hope- less condition. Even if he survives for a few years longer, he will be quite incapable of ever taking up the pen again or entering the pulpit. His mental and physical energies are hopelessly shattered, and partial paralysis has set in. This is all the more sad when it is remembered that he has a wife and children who must suffer sorely from his unfitness to pursue his ordinary vocations.

    *THE BARRY AND BRISTOL CHANNEL STEAM PACKET CARRYING COMPANY. In our last issue we announced the registration of the Barry and Bristol Channel Steam Packet Carrying Company, Limited. This company, as will be seen by our advertising columns this week, has been formed for the purpose of carrying goods and merchandise from the Bristol Channel ports (including Bristol, Cardiff* Newport, Bridg. water, &c.,) to and from Brrry Dock by sea. The company will have a capital of JB5,000, in £ 1 shares, and the subscription list closes on Tuesday next. It is anticipated ,by the directors, who are all business men of Barry and Cardiff, that a very profitable business will be done in carrying from the several ports of the Channel the great and varied stock of materials required in ever-in- creasing districts l'ke Barry, Cadoxtoh, and Barry Dock. The company will at first charter steamers, but at an early date, as the trade develops, they intend to purchase or build others specially adapted for work of the kind, and suit- able for carrying passengers. Provisional arrangements have been made for erecting a suit- able warehouse at Barry Dock for the reception of general goods, and application has been made to the Board of Trade for power to erect a wharf and landing stage at Barry in such a position as will save a large sum each year in dock and wharfage dues. It is stated that the company has already had promises of extensive support from mercantile firms, and it is intended to add to the undertaking the business of coal, hay, corn, and straw merchants. We, therefore, invite our speculative readers to secure a sub- stantial interest) in this promising concern at once, because applications have already been received for a large proportion of the shares.

From childhood he felt that God had something for him to do, and he filled the office of Sunday School teacher and tract distributor, and a year after he accompanied his superintendent to a country village, where he preached for the first time. He was proposed as a candidate at the Portsmouth District meeting in 1827, and entered upon his first circuit in Jersey in 1828, and from that time till 1868, when he became a supernumerary, he passed through all the vicissitudes of an early Methodist preacher's life. He entered the ministry at 22 years of age, and laboured 40 years, and for 22 years further in connection with the Loudoun- aquMe Circuit. Having been in the ministry 62 years, he had only three seniors and two contem- poraries. An attack of rheumatic gout in July last laid him aside completely, and on the 29th of July he had a seizure, during which the heart's action almost stopped. From that time his mind ceased to be clear. On Friday last he was again suddenly taken ill, and eventually passed away.—The party left the chapel for the churchyard, where the burial service was concluded by the Revs. D. Young, J.     A LATE BARRY DIRECTOR ON "CHURCH UNION." Mr Robert W. Perks, the Liberal candidate for the Louth division of L incolnshire, and till recencly a director of the Barry Dock and Railways Company, opening a Wesleyan bazaar at Donning- ton-on-Bain, Lincolnshire, on Thursday week last, spoke at some length upon the relation of Non- conformists, especially Methodism, to the Church of England in rural districts; He expressed his pleasure that the recent Wesleyan Conference at Bristol had displayed a greatly increased interest on the part of laymen in the proceedings of that religious body, and declared that, as they had shown by their vote to admit the public press to the proceedings of the conference next year at Nottingham, the action of the Methodist laity in shaping the policy of the connexion on public and social questions would be marked by a liber- ality of spirit, a breadth of view, and knowledge of the requirements of the people which would hardly be attained by a purely ecclesiastical assembly. (Hear, hear.) Referring to the answer which the conference had sent to the Archbishop of Canter- bury on the subject of Church Union, Mr Perks said that practical men must see that any such discussion as Dr Benson suggested could lead to no result. (Hear. hear.) The bishop and clergy of the Establishment looked upon themselves as the apostolic successors of the early Church, while modern Methodists, like their founder, rejected this strange theory as an idle and pretentious dream. (Hear, hear.) Nor were the relations of Nonconformists and the Church in the villages of England sufficiently fraternal to lead anyone to think that the decisions of the bishops and a select body of Nonconformist clergy on any such questions would be accepted by their respective followers. An eminent Wesleyan minister had recently circulated all over the country a pamphlet called "The Cry of the Villages A Call to the Nonconformist Churches of Great Britain," which endeavoured to show that there was an organised attempt being made by the Established Church to stamp out Nonconformity in rural England. Now he (the speaker) did not agree with this notion, and he felt perfectly persuaded the attempt, if made, could not succeed. (Hear, hear.) What, however, Nonconformists did need, and what they must urgently and constantly demand, was a fare and legitimate share in the management of village elementary Church schools, where at present the children of Nonconformists formed sp large a pro- portion, and frequently, indeed, the majority of the scholars. At Sunk Island, a Wesleyan school- master had just been turned out of the elementary school, which was practically under the control and management of the Woods and Forests Com- missioners, and was on Crown property, simply because he could not teach the children, who were largely Methodists, the Church catechism and initiate them into all the mysterious observances of the saints' day of the Church calendar. Here and there no doubt they met with some eccentric anachronism among the Church clergy, who believed, as the bishops did in the last century, that Methodism was a very horrid thing, for he had, for example, a letter from a Lincolnshire farmer complaining that the vicar had come into his house during a Methodist revival in the village, and after telling him that a revival was something akin to St. Vitus's or St. Anthony's dance, had proceeded to caper round the room to show how men acted when they were convicted of sin." (Laughter.) This man reminded him (the speaker) of an eloquent clergyman whom he heard many years ago preach at Winchelsea, in Sussex, who said that if he saw thp devil running across his churchyard with a Methodist on his back he would not call Stop thief," because he should con- sider he had got his own property. (Laughter.) Happily these quser geniuses were rare in the Church, and indeed in the world nowadays. (Hear, hear.) Methodists were still the friends of all and the enemies of none, but they must be let alone to do their work in rural England in their own way. (Applause.)
