The Land Registry issue

The applicable statutory framework in 1982/83 was the Land Registration Act
1925 and the Land Registration Rules 1925.

The Rules expressly contained provisions dealing with first registration,
including *rule 20, requiring the applicant to deliver the original deeds
and documents relating to the title which were in its possession or control*.
The operation of that rule in the first-registration context is confirmed
in *Walker v Burton*, where the High Court described the requirement in
those terms.

The Rules also contained a specific provision headed *"Examination of title
by Registrar"* and provisions concerning searches and enquiries by the
Registrar.

This means that the question cannot simply be reduced to "Land Registry
registers what it is given".

The relevant question is whether, in this case:

   1.

   the applicant supplied the material title documents in its possession or
   control;
   2.

   the documents supplied actually demonstrated that the House Plot formed
   part of the estate being registered;
   3.

   the Registrar examined the documentary chain sufficiently to identify
   any inconsistency between the deeds and the land claimed for registration;
   4.

   any discrepancy between the deeds, plans, possession orders and the
   proposed registration plan generated a requisition or further enquiry;
   5.

   the registration was completed as an absolute title despite a material
   uncertainty as to the extent of the documentary title; and
   6.

   the Registrar's own records contain any warning, requisition, note,
   correspondence or other indication that the separate House Plot issue was
   considered.

There is a particularly important point concerning *registration in error*.

The Law Commission's review of the 1925 system records that the Land
Registration Rules 1925 contained a specific power in *rule 14* dealing
with "registration in error", including registration of too large a part of
a title, and explained that the registrar had an express power to rectify
registration arising from mistake.

That is significant because HM Land Registry is currently saying, in
effect, that there was no mistake. That conclusion should not be treated as
established unless HM Land Registry has actually examined the surviving
1982/83 registration file and established what documentary evidence
justified the extent registered.

HM Land Registry's present published guidance also expressly recognises
that *including too much land in a registered title can constitute a
mistake in the register*, and gives as an example a title being mapped to
include land for which the registered proprietor has no documentary title.
It also recognises that failure by a solicitor to lodge documents in their
possession can amount to lack of proper care. I appreciate that this is
modern guidance and I do not suggest that it can simply be imposed
retrospectively as the law of 1983. It is nevertheless directly relevant to
the distinction HM Land Registry now draws between a genuine registration
mistake and a mere disagreement about the title boundary.

Likewise, HM Land Registry's current guidance states that a title plan
cannot describe the land more accurately than the pre-registration deeds
allow, and that the pre-registration deeds are crucial to resolving
questions about the extent of a registered title. Again, I cite this as an
expression of the documentary basis of registration, not as a claim that a
2025/26 practice guide was itself the governing rule in 1983.
