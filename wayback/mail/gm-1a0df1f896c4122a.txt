Dear Sirs,

I am seeking an independent review of HM Land Registry's handling of my
complaint concerning the historic registration of land belonging to the
Williams/Buckler family at Great House Farm / Tŷ Mawr, Llandough, Cardiff,
and the subsequent registered title history, including WA231076, WA240304
and the later woodland title WA735527.

HM Land Registry has now replied to my MP and has maintained, in essence,
that it does not accept that a mistake was made.

My concern is not simply that HM Land Registry reached a conclusion with
which I disagree. It is that the conclusion appears to leave fundamental
documentary questions unanswered and relies heavily upon historical
proceedings which did not determine the underlying documentary root of the
family's ownership claim.

I have spent approximately twenty years researching this matter and have
assembled a substantial body of documentary, archival, court, Land Registry
and family evidence. I am not asking ICR to determine ownership itself. I
am asking it to review whether HM Land Registry properly investigated and
answered the complaint put before it, whether it addressed the evidence I
supplied, and whether the reasons given for its conclusion adequately
explain how the disputed registration was accepted and subsequently
maintained.
1. The underlying family ownership claim

The family's position is that our historic Parcel A was a distinct parcel
of land around the farmhouse and woodland and that our underlying freehold
interest was never lawfully extinguished.

My grandmother, Mary Williams, consistently asserted ownership of the
family parcel. The documentary history presently available to me raises a
substantial question as to whether Parcel A was ever conveyed to Western
Ground Rents in the manner subsequently assumed.

There is an important distinction between:

   - the family-occupied Parcel A surrounding the farmhouse; and
   - the eastern agricultural Parcel B which was the subject of the later
   agricultural tenancy arrangements.

The 1939 revised agricultural tenancy documentation which I have located
appears to concern Parcel B and its associated land. The accompanying plan
does not appear to include the family-occupied Parcel A.

This raises a basic documentary question:

What instrument actually conveyed Parcel A to Western Ground Rents, if it
was ever conveyed to them at all?

I have not yet obtained a document which satisfactorily answers that
question.
2. The 1928 / 1938 / 1939 documentary sequence

The present reconstruction begins with the 1877 arrangements involving the
Marquess of Bute and Daniel Thomas & Son and the subsequent quarrying
arrangements.

My understanding from the evidence presently assembled is that the relevant
quarrying arrangements were completed by 1928 and that the family
thereafter continued in occupation of Parcel A as its own land.

The later 1938 conveyancing from the Bute estate to Western Ground Rents
therefore requires close examination as to precisely what land was conveyed.

This is particularly important because the 1939 revised agricultural
tenancy record naming Western Ground Rents as landlord appears, from its
plan, to concern Parcel B rather than Parcel A.

That record was brought to my attention by the clerk for Llandough Town,
who directed me to the archive material shortly before his retirement
earlier this year.

If Parcel A was not conveyed to Western Ground Rents in 1938, and was not
included in the 1939 agricultural tenancy, the subsequent legal narrative
by which the family's occupation came to be treated as permissive requires
considerably more examination than HM Land Registry's present response
appears to have given it.
3. The successive attempts to characterise the family occupation as
permissive

The historical evidence reveals a sequence of events which I believe needs
to be examined as a whole rather than in isolation.

These include:

*1949* — an attempted tenancy arrangement concerning Parcel B which appears
to have sought to include my grandmother in the tenancy and thereby connect
her occupation of Parcel A with the agricultural tenancy of Parcel B;

*1951* — the disappearance of important family papers, including the
family's title documentation, which my grandmother reported as stolen;

*1955* — possession proceedings in which the language concerning the “whole
farm” requires comparison with the actual definition of the agricultural
holding and its separate parcels. The order also expressly excluded the
“house and garden” and referred to my grandmother's “condition and
resistance”;

*1959* — an offer of a tenancy of the farmhouse and garden, which my
grandmother rejected while continuing to assert an ownership claim;

*1962* — a possession order concerning the farmhouse and garden, the legal
effect and subsequent enforcement history of which require examination;

*1965* — a further attempt to place “Mrs Williams” into a weekly tenancy,
again rejected;

*1974* — an ownership action was adjourned; leave to enforce the 1962
possession order was obtained but the warrant was not pursued; and BP
subsequently relied upon the two letters of 31 October 1974 as establishing
a licence relationship.

The family interpretation is that these were not independent, isolated
events but a succession of attempts to convert or present an allegedly
proprietary occupation as permissive.

I appreciate that this is an interpretation requiring documentary testing.
That is precisely why I believe HM Land Registry should have engaged with
the underlying documents rather than treating the later registered position
and the 1987 Court of Appeal judgment as effectively resolving the matter.
4. The 1987 judgment does not answer the documentary provenance question

A particularly important part of my complaint concerns the way HM Land
Registry has relied upon *BP Properties Ltd v Buckler* [1987] EWCA Civ 2.

I do not dispute that this was an important Court of Appeal decision.

However, the case concerned the adverse-possession and possession issues
concerning the farmhouse and garden. The Court considered the
registered/paper-title position relied upon by BP, the previous possession
proceedings and the legal effect of the 1974 letters upon the character of
Mrs Buckler's occupation.

The judgment itself records that Mary Williams had asserted that she had a
title to Great House Farm through her grandfather and believed that
documents existed establishing that title, but that such documents were not
produced.

The point is therefore not that the Court of Appeal “never touched title”.
It plainly considered BP's registered/paper-title position.

The point is narrower and, in my view, fundamental:

The 1987 judgment is not itself the conveyance, plan, root deed or other
documentary instrument establishing how the woodland and the family's
historic Parcel A entered BP's registered title.

Nor did it adjudicate the validity of Forest of Cardiff's separate 1994
acquisition of the woodland.

I am concerned that HM Land Registry's present response treats the 1987
judgment as though it answers a documentary provenance question which it
did not determine.
5. The 1969 conveyance and 1971 abstract

HM Land Registry has also told me that BP supplied a title chain extending
back to 1969.

That raises another substantial documentary issue.

There were only thirteen years between the 1969 conveyance and BP's first
registration in 1982.

The 1969 conveyance from Western Ground Rents to BP Pension Trust therefore
requires examination as to exactly what proprietary interest was being
conveyed.

I understand the later title documentation to recite the 1969 conveyance as
a root document, while the 1971 abstract refers to the 1962 possession
order and an OS plan.

If the underlying title to Parcel A was never conveyed to Western Ground
Rents, the critical question is how an asserted interest arising from
possession proceedings could subsequently be treated as though it were
documentary title to the parcel itself.

The 1971 abstract is therefore particularly important.

HM Land Registry's own correspondence has recorded that such an abstract
was lodged. The 30 July 2026 schedule of surviving first-registration
documents does not identify the 31 December 1969 conveyance itself, nor
does it resolve the evidential gap concerning the 1971 abstract.

I am continuing to pursue those underlying records.
6. The 1982–83 registrations

My principal concern is that BP may have registered not the family's actual
historic Parcel A, supported by a lawful root title, but a later
constructed or “synthetic” parcel derived from the succession of
possession, tenancy and licence documents which had been used to
characterise the family occupation as permissive.

I use “synthetic parcel” as a description of the issue I am investigating,
not as a statement that this is already legally established.

The essential question is:

What precise land did BP apply to register in 1982–83, what documentary
instrument entitled BP to register that land, and did that land correspond
to the land actually conveyed to BP's predecessors?

The later amalgamation of the BP titles is also significant because what
may have originated as separate parcels or interests appears subsequently
to have been brought together within the registered estate.

It is necessary to establish whether that process merely recorded an
existing lawful title or whether it caused an underlying proprietary
boundary to disappear within a later registered estate.
7. HM Land Registry's response

HM Land Registry has now maintained that it was correct to register the
land and has relied, in part, upon the 1987 judgment.

I respectfully submit that this does not answer the complaint.

An administrative conclusion that a registration was “correct” cannot
itself reconstruct a missing conveyance, establish the precise boundary of
land conveyed decades earlier, or demonstrate that a later registered
proprietor had documentary title to a distinct parcel where the underlying
conveyancing remains disputed.

Nor does the existence of the 1987 judgment establish the documentary
provenance of WA735527 or the validity of the separate 1994 transfer to
Forest of Cardiff.

I am particularly concerned that HM Land Registry appears to regard its
reliance upon the 1987 judgment as effectively validating the 1982–83
registration, when the registration itself pre-dated the judgment.

The chronology matters.

The registration came first.

The litigation came afterwards.

I therefore cannot see how a later judgment concerning possession and
adverse possession can, without further analysis, resolve whether the
earlier registration accurately represented the documentary title existing
at the date of registration.
8. Notification and my grandmother's death

Another issue which requires consideration is notification.

To my knowledge, the family was not notified that BP had applied to
register the relevant land in 1982.

My grandmother died on 14 March 1983, shortly after the first registration
and within the period in which the registration would have been capable of
being challenged had she known of it.

She had spent decades rejecting attempts to characterise her occupation as
permissive and consistently maintaining an ownership claim.

The fact that she died shortly after the registration is not itself proof
of wrongdoing. It is, however, a material chronological fact when
considering whether she had a meaningful opportunity to challenge the
registration and whether HM Land Registry appropriately considered the
notification history.

I ask the ICR to consider whether HM Land Registry has adequately addressed:

   - what notice was given of the 1982 application;
   - to whom it was given;
   - what information was supplied to the persons potentially affected;
   - whether any competing ownership claim was disclosed by the applicant;
   - and what records exist showing how HM Land Registry assessed those
   matters.

9. Possible fraud, concealment and institutional involvement

Through my research I have also identified matters which I consider may
warrant investigation for possible fraud, deliberate concealment,
misrepresentation, mistake and institutional involvement.

I want to be precise about this.

I am not asking the ICR to find that fraud occurred.

I am asking the ICR to consider whether HM Land Registry adequately
investigated evidence raising those questions, particularly where the
evidence concerns missing historic documents, the distinction between
Parcel A and Parcel B, the provenance of the 1969 title, the 1971 abstract,
the 1982–83 registrations and the subsequent amalgamation of titles.

I have deliberately distinguished established documentary facts from
matters which remain hypotheses requiring investigation.

There are also serious questions concerning the destruction or absence of
historical records and the later treatment of the land. Those matters are
being pursued through separate archival and documentary channels.
10. The human and public-interest dimension

This is not merely an abstract registration dispute.

My grandmother was a disabled widow and amputee who maintained an ownership
claim to the family land through decades of litigation and repeated
attempts to characterise her occupation as permissive.

The family was ultimately removed from the property in 1988. The historic
farmhouse, which had been our family home and which we believe had
significant architectural and historic importance, was subsequently
demolished.

I was a child when we were removed.

Family possessions and historic material were lost or dispersed, while the
land was subsequently developed and altered.

The family's connection with the site is recorded as extending back to the
seventeenth century, and I was born at Great House Farm.

There are also wider heritage and archaeological questions concerning the
site and its subsequent development, which I am investigating separately.

I mention these matters because they explain why I have continued the
investigation for approximately twenty years and why the accuracy of the
underlying land record matters so profoundly to my family.

My professional background is in military cyber and electronic warfare, and
much of my research has involved reconstructing fragmented documentary
histories and identifying discrepancies between records. I have approached
this as an evidential investigation rather than simply as a family
grievance.

I therefore ask the ICR to review HM Land Registry's handling of my

   2.

   adequately considered the 1938 conveyance and 1939 revised tenancy plan;

   adequately addressed the documentary provenance of the 1969 conveyance

I am not asking the ICR to adjudicate the family's ultimate title claim.

I am asking for an independent examination of whether HM Land Registry's
investigation and reasoning were adequate, proportionate and properly
