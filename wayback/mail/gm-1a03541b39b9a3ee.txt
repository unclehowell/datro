Ms Davidson,

Thank you for your further response. Very much appreciate your time in this
matter.

I have considered it carefully. I now need to make one fundamental point
clear. This is not any animosity or prejudice but a matter of upmost
importance to my family, many of whome are women and too frightened to
speak out. I must do my ahsolute best and part here and take the following
position on this matter.

I do not accept the characterisation of my complaint as being simply that a
councillor failed to answer a number of emails.

That formulation removes the very circumstances which made the conduct
complained of potentially relevant to the standards expected of an elected
member.

Councillor Carroll initially responded to me and asked how he could help. I
then provided the information and evidence requested. What followed was not
an isolated unanswered email, but a documented pattern of non-engagement
over approximately five months, notwithstanding repeated approaches
concerning matters affecting his own ward, including planning, development,
historical records and a serious concern that the title upon which current
development is proceeding may itself require examination.

I am not asking the Monitoring Officer to determine the ownership of Great
House Farm by correspondence. Nor am I asking the Council to accept my
allegations as established fact.

I am asking whether the conduct of an elected member, in the circumstances
actually presented, was properly assessed against the full range of
standards applicable to that member.
1. The Code cannot be reduced to an obligation to answer emails

I accept the proposition that the Members' Code does not create a general
rule requiring a councillor to answer every email received from a member of
the public within a prescribed period.

That is not the proposition I have advanced.

The question is whether a sustained course of conduct can engage provisions
of the Code concerning respect and consideration, conduct capable of
bringing the member's office or authority into disrepute, misuse of
position, and the broader principles governing the conduct of elected
representatives.

The Council's current Constitution places the Members' Code within Section
19 and separately identifies the principles governing members' conduct.

The Public Services Ombudsman for Wales itself describes the Code as
imposing enforceable minimum standards on councillors and identifies,
amongst other matters, conduct which negatively affects an authority's
reputation, unfair use of position, bullying, and failures to treat people
equally as matters which can potentially constitute breaches.

I therefore ask you to identify precisely which provisions you considered
and, more importantly, why the particular factual circumstances I supplied
were considered incapable of engaging any of them.

A conclusion that "failure to respond is not itself a breach" does not
answer that question.
2. The actual chronology matters

Please record the chronology accurately.

This was not:

five emails were sent and a councillor did not reply.

It was:

   - I approached my elected ward councillor concerning matters affecting
   his ward;
   - Councillor Carroll responded and asked how he could help;
   - I then supplied substantive information and evidence;
   - further correspondence followed on 16 February, 4 May, 7 June, 25 June
   and 13 July 2026;
   - the correspondence concerned, amongst other things, the historical
   planning and development of Great House Farm / Church View Close, access to
   relevant public records, and an apparent defect or inconsistency in the
   historical title chain;
   - despite the initial invitation to explain how he could assist, there
   was subsequently no substantive engagement with the matters raised.

That is the conduct I asked you to assess.

I therefore ask you to confirm whether you considered the actual underlying
emails and attachments, rather than merely the summary chronology.
3. Was Councillor Carroll's account obtained?

You have now confirmed that you did not consider it necessary to seek
comments from Councillor Carroll.

I appreciate that you do not have to conduct a full investigation at this
stage.

Nevertheless, that makes the precise nature of your decision particularly
important.

You have reached a conclusion that the information provided did not
disclose evidence capable of amounting to a breach, whilst apparently not
obtaining the explanation of the person whose conduct was complained of.

That does not necessarily make the decision unlawful. But it does mean that
I am entitled to understand precisely what factual inference you drew from
the material before you.

In particular, if there was a reason why Councillor Carroll did not respond
after initially offering to help, I do not know what that reason was.

If the reason was that he considered the matter outside his role, please
say so.

If the reason was that he considered the correspondence excessive,
vexatious or otherwise inappropriate, please say so.

If the reason was connected with material I had published or discussed on
social media, please say so.

If there was another reason, please identify it.

I am not alleging any particular explanation. I am asking that the factual
basis of the conduct not simply be assumed.
4. Social media and equal treatment

I also wish to place one specific issue on record.

I understand that the Code does not simply regulate everything a member or
member of the public says on social media. The Ombudsman itself has
separate guidance concerning social media and the Code.

Accordingly, if social-media material has influenced Councillor Carroll's
decision to disengage from me, I would ask that this be disclosed rather
than silently becoming the background justification for his conduct.

It would be an obvious inconsistency if material said or published by a
constituent outside the relevant Code framework were treated as a reason
for refusing to engage with that constituent, while the resulting conduct
of the elected member were then assessed in isolation as merely "a failure
to respond".

Again, I am not asserting that this occurred. I am asking that it not be
assumed away.
5. This is also a question of democratic accountability

There is a much wider issue here which I do not believe has been addressed.

I am raising a documented public-interest concern concerning land within
the Council's area, historical planning and enforcement records, the
demolition and subsequent development of Great House Farm / Church View
Close, and an apparent problem in the title history of land now associated
with substantial residential development.

I am not asking the Council to decide my private ownership claim.

I am asking that the concern be capable of being placed before the
democratic institutions of the Council and scrutinised by the appropriate
people.

The Council itself expressly operates mechanisms for public questions at
Full Council, and its published meeting material demonstrates that
questions from the public are part of its democratic process. The Council
also states that its meetings are conducted to support and promote
democratic engagement and public interest.

Your response says that no further action will be taken by you. It does
not, however, tell me what democratic route remains available for me to
place the underlying public-interest concern before elected members.

I therefore ask you to identify that route.

Specifically, please confirm how I may:

   1.

   submit a public question to Full Council concerning the Council's
   records and handling of this matter;
   2.

   submit a petition or e-petition;
   3.

   seek consideration by the appropriate Scrutiny Committee;
   4.

   raise the adequacy of the Council's handling of the matter with the
   Governance and Audit Committee or other appropriate constitutional body; and
   5.

   request inspection of relevant historical background papers associated
   with the planning and demolition history of the site.

The answer cannot simply be that the Monitoring Officer has decided not to
take further action. That determines one particular route. It does not
extinguish democratic accountability.
6. The public-record issue needs to be separated from the ownership dispute

I also want to avoid any further misunderstanding concerning the planning
records.

I have repeatedly encountered historical material which is not presently
accessible to me through the Council's online planning system.

I am not presently asserting that this proves deliberate concealment.

There may be perfectly legitimate explanations: age of the records,
digitisation, migration between systems, retention arrangements, technical
problems, or the fact that historical material was never placed online.

But the important point is that the Council's own planning-register
arrangements acknowledge that the online register is not necessarily a
complete database of everything held by the Council.

That makes the appropriate question one of transparency:

*What records does the Council hold, what records does it no longer hold,
where are the surviving records, and how can they be inspected?*

That is a straightforward public-record question.

It should not depend upon the Council first accepting my interpretation of
the title history.

I therefore intend to pursue the records question separately and formally,
including under the applicable information-access legislation where
appropriate.
7. The historical planning record is itself relevant

The historical demolition of Great House Farm is not an invented issue
arising solely from my family account.

The historic environment record identifies Great House Farm as a
historically significant site and records its sudden and complete
demolition in December 1988 amid considerable local controversy.

Whatever conclusion is ultimately reached concerning ownership, title or
the historical allegations, the existence of a significant historical
record means that it is reasonable to ask what planning, enforcement,
demolition, committee and officer records survive and what decisions were
made.

I am therefore not asking the Council to rewrite history in accordance with
my family's interpretation.

I am asking the Council to disclose its own documentary history.
8. Monitoring Officer responsibilities

I also ask you to clarify the extent to which you have considered the
Council's own potential obligations as an authority, as distinct from your
assessment of Councillor Carroll's individual conduct.

Section 5 of the Local Government and Housing Act 1989 places a statutory
reporting function upon a Monitoring Officer where it appears that a
proposal, decision or omission by the authority, its committees,
sub-committees, officers or other specified authority actors gives rise to
the statutory circumstances identified in that provision.

I am not asserting that section 5 automatically requires you to issue a
report merely because I have asked you to do so.

I am asking a narrower and more reasonable question:

*Have you considered whether any separate omission or decision by the
Council itself — particularly concerning access to public records,
democratic engagement or the handling of this matter — engages your
statutory Monitoring Officer responsibilities?*

If you have considered that question and concluded that section 5 is not
engaged, please say so and give the reason.

If you have not considered it, please confirm that.
9. The Ombudsman

Your response expressly directs me to the Public Services Ombudsman for
Wales.

I will therefore consider making a direct Code of Conduct complaint
supported by the underlying correspondence rather than relying upon the
Council's characterisation of the matter.

That is particularly appropriate because the Ombudsman states that its
initial assessment considers whether evidence suggests that a Code breach
may have occurred and, separately, whether investigation would be
sufficiently serious and in the public interest. The Ombudsman specifically
identifies maintaining public confidence in local councillors as one factor
relevant to that assessment.

I will therefore allow the Ombudsman to make its own assessment of the
evidence.

I will provide it with the actual chronology, the original correspondence,
the initial response from Councillor Carroll asking how he could assist,
the subsequent correspondence, and your decision.
10. One final point about the characterisation of my conduct

I have been persistent because I consider the underlying matter important.

Persistence should not be confused with misconduct.

The Ombudsman itself expressly states that it does not regard behaviour as
unacceptable merely because a complainant is forceful or determined, while
also making clear that correspondence should remain polite and courteous.

I accept the latter completely.

I am therefore going to continue to pursue these matters firmly, but
through the proper channels and on the evidence.

What I will not accept is the repeated administrative reframing of a
substantive public-interest concern as nothing more than an unhappy
constituent sending too many emails.
Accordingly, I ask you to confirm:

   1.

   The exact provisions of the Members' Code of Conduct which you
   considered.
   2.

   Whether you considered the five original emails and their attachments in
   full.
   3.

   Whether you considered Councillor Carroll's initial response asking how
   he could assist.
   4.

   Whether Councillor Carroll was asked for an explanation. If not, please
   confirm that.
   5.

   Whether you are aware of any reason why he subsequently ceased
   substantive engagement.
   6.

   Whether social-media activity formed any part of the reason for his
   non-engagement.
   7.

   Whether you have considered any potential omission or decision by the
   Council itself under your statutory Monitoring Officer responsibilities.
   8.

   What democratic mechanism you say is available for me to place the
   underlying public-interest concern before elected members.
   9.

   How I can obtain or inspect the surviving planning, enforcement,
   demolition, committee and officer records relating to Great House Farm /
   Church View Close which are not available through the online planning
   system.
   10.

   Whether any relevant records are known to have been destroyed, disposed
   of, transferred, archived elsewhere or otherwise become unavailable, and,
   if so, under what records-management arrangements.
   11.

   Whether the historical demolition and subsequent development were
   considered by a Council committee and, if so, where the relevant agendas,
   reports, minutes and background papers can be inspected.

I am deliberately keeping these questions precise because I want a proper
evidential record rather than another exchange in which the substance of
the issue is lost in administrative categorisation.

I am also not asking you to endorse my conclusions concerning title.

The democratic question is much simpler:

*Where a constituent places before his elected representative a documented
concern that land within the ward may be affected by a defective or
unresolved title history, and where the historical public records needed to
examine that concern are not readily accessible, what mechanism exists for
that concern to be transparently examined rather than simply closed
administratively?*

That question remains unanswered.

I would therefore be grateful for a substantive response to the points
above.

Yours sincerely,
