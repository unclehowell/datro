I am also asking the Senedd to consider whether the circumstances surrounding the demolition of Great House Farm, Llandough-juxta-Penarth, and the subsequent planning and heritage history of the site warrant independent scrutiny and, ultimately, a public inquiry or Senedd committee inquiry.

I am not asking the Senedd to determine a private property dispute or to decide legal questions that properly belong before the courts. My concern is the conduct, decision-making, record-keeping and accountability of public bodies responsible for heritage protection, planning and local government.

The surviving evidence presents a series of unusually important questions.

Great House Farm was professionally surveyed by the Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales in 1974. In December 1988, Cadw was considering the building for emergency spot-listing, with a contemporary report stating that inspectors were due to visit and consider the case. The European Commission of Human Rights records that the Convention application had been introduced on 3 May 1988; BP obtained possession on 29 November 1988; demolition began on 6 December 1988; and the application was not registered by the Commission until 12 December 1988.

The evidence therefore establishes a very compressed sequence in which emergency heritage protection was being considered, domestic interim relief was refused, possession was obtained and the building was then destroyed within days.

That chronology does not, by itself, establish coordination, deliberate suppression or unlawful conduct. It does, however, warrant investigation.

There are several specific questions which I believe require answers from an authority independent of the present local authority:

  1.  What information and records were actually considered by Cadw when the 1988 spot-listing decision was made?
  2.  Was the 1974 RCAHMW architectural survey considered, and if not, why not?
  3.  What communications took place between Cadw, South Glamorgan County Council, BP Properties Ltd, the courts and any other public bodies during the critical period immediately preceding demolition?
  4.  What consideration was given by the local planning authority to the available statutory powers of preservation in circumstances where demolition was imminent?
  5.  Why was the building destroyed before there could be an independent retrospective architectural examination of the fabric?
  6.  How should the later archaeological evidence, including the discovery of approximately 1,026 burials in 1994, be understood in assessing the historic significance of the site and the adequacy of the earlier decision-making? I recognise that this later discovery cannot retrospectively prove that the 1988 listing decision was unlawful.
  7.  A 1989 planning document appears to describe the site as having formerly contained Great House Farm “(a listed building)”. I ask that this apparent discrepancy be investigated and reconciled with the 1988 Cadw decision.
  8.  What records relating to these decisions still exist, where are they held, and have reasonable steps been taken to preserve and disclose them?

I am particularly concerned that the destruction of the physical building in December 1988 permanently removed primary architectural evidence which could otherwise have been examined independently.

I also ask the Senedd to consider the wider public-interest question: whether the present mechanisms for accountability are adequate where a historic building is destroyed during a period of rapidly developing legal, planning and heritage proceedings and significant questions remain about what information was available to decision-makers.

I would therefore ask the Petitions Committee to consider accepting a petition on this subject and, where appropriate, referring the matter to the relevant Senedd committee or committees for investigation. I would also welcome consideration of whether an independent public inquiry, committee inquiry or other form of external investigation would be appropriate.

I am prepared to provide the underlying documents, chronology, court material, heritage records, planning documents and correspondence supporting these questions.

I am not asking the Senedd to adopt my conclusions. I am asking that the evidence and unanswered questions be examined independently and that the relevant public bodies be required to account for what they did, what they knew, and what records exist.

Its stated purpose is historical record correction, archival explanation and administrative acknowledgement. The central issue therefore extends beyond the merits of the individual 1988 listing assessment.

Cadw's 2026 FOI response confirms that an assessment took place, but that only two exterior photographs now survive; the assessment report, reasons for refusal, site notes, internal recommendations and consultation correspondence are no longer available. The report does not assert that this proves wrongdoing. Rather, it identifies a straightforward public-administration question: how can the adequacy and evidential basis of an irreversible public heritage decision be understood or scrutinised when the underlying administrative record no longer survives?

That seems potentially relevant to the Committee's stated function of scrutinising the administration and accountability of Welsh Government and associated public bodies, even if the Committee cannot itself determine whether the 1988 decision was correct.

I am therefore not asking the Committee to investigate the Buckler family's historical dispute with BP, adjudicate upon the 1988 decision, or direct Cadw to reverse anything. The specific administrative questions raised in the report are instead:

  1.  What record-retention arrangements applied to the 1988 assessment?
  2.  Why do the assessment report, reasons, site notes and consultation records no longer survive?
  3.  Were the records destroyed under an applicable retention policy or other authority, and were they considered for transfer to the appropriate archival institution?
  4.  Is the present position consistent with Welsh Government/Cadw expectations for the preservation of records concerning significant heritage decisions?
  5.  Are current arrangements sufficient to ensure that comparable heritage assessments, particularly where a building is under imminent threat of demolition, are properly documented and retained?

These are not questions that require the Committee to determine the rights of either party to the original dispute. They concern administrative practice, institutional record-keeping and the ability of the Senedd and the public to scrutinise decisions taken by public bodies. For completeness, I should also emphasise that the report itself deliberately recognises the limits of the surviving evidence. It does not claim that Cadw acted unlawfully or in bad faith, and it expressly acknowledges that the absence of the 1988 working papers means that the assessment cannot now be reconstructed definitively.

My objective is therefore quite limited: to ensure that the surviving evidence is preserved, that the archival gap is properly explained, and that the public record accurately distinguishes what is established from what can no longer be verified.
