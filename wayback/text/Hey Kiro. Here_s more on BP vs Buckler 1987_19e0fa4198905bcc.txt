2. If She Held Title as "Mrs Williams," the Licence May Have Been with the Wrong Person

Under the Married Women's Property Act 1882, a married woman could hold real property independently of her husband. If Mary Williams inherited superior title from her father in her own name — as Mary Williams or Mrs Williams — and BP's 1974 letters were addressed only to "Mrs Buckler" (the wife of a squatter/occupier), then BP may have granted a licence to the wrong legal person.
The Court of Appeal held that the 1974 letters created a unilateral licence that stopped adverse possession time running.

But a licence is a personal permission. If it was granted to Mrs Buckler (as BP perceived her — a trespasser's wife) and not to Mrs Williams (as the independent title-holder), then:

- The person who actually held the equitable or legal title never received the licence.
- The person who received the licence may not have had authority to accept on behalf of the title-holder.
- Therefore, the true owner's possession may never have been converted into a licence.

This is not a "legal fiction" argument. It is a misnomer / wrong party argument: the litigation finger was pointed at the occupier's wife, not at the independent title-holder.

4. Possession Does Not Prove Title

A possession order is not a declaration that the claimant has the best title in the kingdom. It only determines who has the immediate right to possession against the defendant. If the defendant never raised a competing title, the court does not investigate the claimant's root of title.
