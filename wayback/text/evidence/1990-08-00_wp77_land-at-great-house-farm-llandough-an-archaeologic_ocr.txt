1) INTRODUCTION AND ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

Following the demolition of Great House Farm, Llandough in December 1988
the Glamorgan-Gwent Archaeological Trust (GGAT) entered into discussion
with Debenham Tewson, agents for the site owners B P Properties Ltd, on the
possible archaeological implications of redevelopment. The Trust, in its
capacity as archaeological advisor to the Vale of Glamorgan Borough Council
(VGBC), informed the local planning authority of the archaeological
sensitivity of the site, and also kept in close contact with the Llandough
Community Council, who initially contacted the Trust about the
redevelopment.

It was agreed that tne potential archaeological interest should be
considered in the light of Welsh Office planning guidance and the County
and Local Plans. Accordingly the VGBC determined that, in granting outline
planning consent, ‘no development shall take place until a field evaluation
survey and archaeological assessment report has been carried out by 2
qualified archaeologist to the satisfaction of the tLocal Planning
Authority' and that ‘the detail plans to be sumbitted shali pay full regare
to the results and findings of the archaeological investigations'. A three
month time-limit was set for the archaeological work, and on the
recommendation of Morgan Bruce, Solicitors for Debenham Tewson, it was
agreed that the report should be completed as soon as possible thereafter

B P Properties commissioned GGAT to undertake the fieldwork and the repert
of which this introduction forms a part. It will be evident therefore that
GGAT has throughout played a dual role; on the one hand it has advised the
Local Planning Authority, and on the other provided comparable advice to
the applicant. It should be stressed that GGAT made this clear to beth
parties at the outset, in order that either of them could seek alternative
qualified advice if it was felt a conflict of interest might arise

The assistance of a number of individuals in the above organisations is
gratefully acknowledged by GGAT. In particular Mr Joy of Debenham Tewson
Mesers Dylan and Maitland Evans of VGBC, Mr Cann of Morgan Bruce and Mr
Egan of Llandough Community Council have all afforded avery co-operation to
GGAT. :

3) ARCHAEOLOGICAL BACKGROUND AND POTENTIAL

The church of St Dochdwy and the surrounding area is of considerable
archaeological interest. The churchyard is likely to be on the site of an
earlier foundation, perhaps dating back to the seventh century AD (Davies
1978). The cross shaft to the south of the west end of the church dates to
the tenth or eleventh century and is of a scale and style which could mark
an important religious site (RCAHMW 1976, Inv No 828).

To the south of the church an Iron Age farmstead, Roman Villa, Dark Age
cemetery, medieval monastic grange, late medieval village and post medieval
mill and farm are known to have existed (Qwen-Tohn 1988), while the
presence of a Norman earthwork castle is presumed (Thomas 1988).

Prior to the assessment the principal recorded archaeoicgical interest o
the redevelopment site itself was Great House Farm (SMP 2034s). Owing t
an ownership dispute and the subsequent sudden des:ruction of the propert
in 1983 the building was incompietely understood. The ECAHMW nevertheles
hold a certain amount of information on the farmnouse and barn. In
summary, although both structures appeared outwardly to be of nineteentn
century date, the farmhouse may have incorporated medieval fabric within
parts of a seventeenth century house which had survived nineteenth century
reconstruction.

The seventeenth century phase is associated with the

of architectural features were recorded in situ in 1974, (Fig 2) and others
were observed in the rubble following demolition. Sherds of twelfth to

In addition to the recorded evidence noted above there was potential for
further archaeological interest. The possibility that deposits relating to
the early monastic settlement might survive in this area could not be ruled
out, and it was possible that the churchyard may formerly have been more
extensive. Although the main focus of Iron Age and Roman activity appeared
to lie to the south it was possible that further evidence might have
survived in this area. A medieval well, Ffynnon y Drindod, was originally
located to the north of the church, and aithough its exact position is
unknown it may have been located within the development site (RCAHMW 1988).

4) ARCHAEOLOGICAL FIELDWORK: OBJECTIVES AND METHODOLOGY

The aim of the archaeological fieldwork was two-fold. In the first
instance the investigation was aimed at astablishing whether deposits
relating to any of the features described in 3 above (or indeed any other
type of archaeological activity) were present. The second aim was ‘o
assess the likely impact of redevelopment on the identified archaeological
interests and to recommend to B P Properties methods by which these
interests could best be protected. In addition to the fieldwork other
relevant sources (eg, cartographic) would be consuited as appropriate.

In view of the size and character of the development site it was decided
that the most appropriate way to test the archaeological potential cf the
site was by a series of machine cut trenches, the position of which was
determined either by the likelihood of archaeological deposits being
encountered (eg, near the churchyard wall), or at random. It was agreed
that the trenches should mostly be cut to the level of the substrate, but
in some areas this proved impractical. The machine trenches were closely
observed and subject to basic archaeological recording.

The system of machine trenching was in effect a compromise between a more
extensive assessment of the archaeological potential of the site, and the
pre-existing state of knowledge, which in this case was insufficient to
enable informed archaeological decisions to be taken. The results
therefore give a much more reliable indication of the likely archaeological
implications of redevelopment, yet by their nature cannot provide total
assurance on the presence and value of archaeological deposits across the
development site as a whole.

5) ARCHAEOLOGICAL FIELDWORK RESULTS

The approximate location of the machine cut trenches is shown on Figure 3,
and the trench numbers given below also correspond with Figure 3.

It was originally intended that a test pit should be dug manually in the
south east sector of the site where machine access was problematic, but it
was felt that the stability of the higher ground to the north, in the area
of 'The Gardens', was questionable and that such an excavation would be ill
advised.

The build up of demolition material in the vicinity of ‘The Gardens’ also
led to a modification of the original plan to cut a trench adjacent to the
north part of the east wall of the churchyard. In the event this cut (6)
was made nearby at the east end of the north wall of the churchyard, The
results from trenches 1 and 5 suggested that the other proposed excavations
in the vicinity of these trenches were unnecessary. With the above
exceptions the investigation was undertaken on the basis of the agreed
sketch plan which formed part of the contract between the Trust and B P
Properties Ltd.

Trench 1
Thie wae cut approximately north west - south east across the line of a
depression, shown on the OS map. The ground surface had clearly been

disturbed by the recent levelling operations.

For most of its length the trench cut through a layer of soil containing
considerable quantities of gravel, brick and stone, to an average depth of
im. This overlay the clay substrate.

Towards the south east end of the trench its character changed. The top c
0.2 m- c 0.3 m consisted of soil and stone and brick rubble, which overlay
c 1 m depth of grey/brown soil with brick-dust, bricks and stone rubble.
Below this the clay substrate was cut by a large feature aligned north east
- south west, in excess of 1 min depth. The north side was angled at about
452 while the southern edge of the feature sloped at a much shallower
angle. The feature was over 8 m wide and extended beyond the southern end
of the trench and below the depth to which the trench could be cut safely.
Its fill consisted of very dark grey clay and a considerable quantity of
steel wire.

In the southern part of this feature c 0.6 m below its top, there was a
lens, up to 0.15 m thick, of brown clay with charcoal flecks, brick
fragments and brick-dust. This layer extended beyond the south eastern end
of the excavated trench.

The principal feature was almost certainly the depression indicated on the
OS map, which was formerly a quarry road. The depth of the road confirmed
that it would have cut any earlier archaeological features which may have
existed. The absence of any archaeological deposits between the substrate
and the modern material in the north west part of the trench suggests that
this area is likely to be sterile. Although based on a small sample we
therefore beiieve that the area to the north west of and including the
quarry road is unlikeiy tc contain significant archaeological interest.

Trench 2

Trench 2 was cut on an approximate north - south alignment and excavated to
a depth of up to 2m The lowest 0.5 m of this depth was probably the clay
substrate within which what appeared to be a thin bank of lias bedrock was
observed. This deposit was overlain by c 0.5 m of a grey clayey soil
containing bricks and lumps of mortar. The top 1 m of material was formed
by a sequence of deposits containing modern debris including plastic, anda
thin skim of topsoil.

As in Trench 1 the absence of any archaeological layers between the
substrate and comparatively recent deposits indicates low archaeological
potential in the vicinity of Trench 2, thus extending the probable
archaeologically sterile zone to the south of the quarry road identified in
Trench 1.

Trench 3

Trench 3 took the form of a T with the long arm cut on a north-south
alignment, and the short arm extended to the west. These excavations were
located in the vicinity of the farm outbuildings shown on the OS map.

The north - south trench revealed a sequence of post medieval and modern
deposits of loose clayey soil with brick, stone, charcoal flecks and
mortar. At the north end of the trench these deposits covered the clay
substrate to a depth of up to 2 m, but towards the south end there was a
marked increase in the depth of overburden. Here the deposits became
significantly wetter, with a high proportion of redeposited clay and lias,
all of which appeared to be loose infill. The exact limits of this infill
were unclear nor could its depth be recorded as excavation was halted for
safety reasons at a depth of c 3.5 m below the ground surface.

In the western arm of the trench the deposits described above overlay the
clay substrate to a depth of c 1.5 m, but in certain areas intervening
deposits were recorded. On the south side of the cut c 2 m from its
junction with the north south trench a lens of brown soil overlying the
substrate was found to contain fragments of human skull and long bones. At
the west end of the trench a thicker deposit (c 0.2 m) of brown clayey soil
was recorded; this contained large quantities of charcoal and some animal
bone. On the north side of the cut c 4 m from the trench junction an ill
defined feature was sealed by the post medieval deposit, although it
appeared to be cut into identical material at a depth of c 1 m below the
present ground surface. The feature was c 0.2 m deep by c 04m- 0.5 m
wide and was filled with inter alia hard sandy mortar similar to that used
in the area during the medieval period.

The post medieval clay and rubble deposit was defined in the western arm by
the foundations of a north - south post medieval wall which abutted the
layer. This wall and a parallel example c 6.5 m to the west doubt less
represent the footings of the barn which stood on the site until 1988.
Some traces of the mortar floor of the structure survived. Between the two
wall footings the place of the clay and rubble depsoit was taken by a dark
grey brown rather clayey soil sealed beneath the barn floor. The barn
floor, walls, etc were overlain by c 0.2 m of disturbed topsoil etc
evidently redeposited during the demolition process.

In the part of the site surrounding the west arm of trench 3 we therefore
recommend two options for further action:

(14) the protection of the archaeological interest by the minimization of
ground disturbance,

(2) if (1) is not possible the archaeological layers should be excavated
and recorded prior to development.

Option (1) is to be preferred if at all possible.

The archaeological deposits identified in trench 4 were only shallowly
buried. Even if the area were set aside as garden space within the
development design there would be a risk of deposits, including human
skeletal remains being disturbed by routine gardening activities. The only

effective way of protecting the archaeological interest in this area would
_be to prevent any further ground disturbance and raise the ground level by
c 0.5 m, an operation which might present problems for the churchyard wall.
If this raising of ground level is impractical there will be little
alternative to the excavation of the archaeological deposits in advance of
development. We therefore recommend two options for further action in the
area of and surrounding trench 4:

(1) the protection of the archaeological interest by the prevention of any
further ground disturbance and the raising of the ground level by at least

0.5 m,

(2) if (1) is not possible the archaeological layers should be excavated

and recorded prior to development.

Option (1) is to be preferred if at all possible.

In both the areas referred to above (ie, in and around trenches 3 and 4)
there may be an additional legal complication. If the presence of human
bone is taken to denote the existence of a disused burial ground the
provisions of the Disused Burial Grounds (Amendment) Act 1981 may apply.
Section 2 (1) (a) of this Act requires the removal and re-internment of
human remains prior to the erection of buildings, although section 2 (2)
does make provision for the Secretary of State to over-rule this
requirement if he is satisfied that the remains will not be disturbed. We
therefore recommend that before any decision on the options given above is
taken, advice should be sought by BP Properties Ltd om any legal
requirements which may pertain to the human skeletal remains.

Finally there is the potentially sensitive area to the east of the
churchyard, Although the archaeological interest is not proven in this
area it is possible that similar layers to those identified in trench 4 may
be present. As they would be off the line of the driveway of Great House
Farm it is likely that any such deposits will be protected by a greater
depth of overburden, but this likelihood cannot be guaranteed. The
protection of the possible archaeological interest could be achieved by the
spreading of the rubble from ‘The Gardens’ over the sensitive area
preparatory to the laying of garden topsoil. Such an operation might cause
problems for the churchyard wall. Two options for further action are
therefore presented:

1) the protection of the possible archaeological interest by the
prevention of ground disturbance and the raising of the ground level,

(2) if (1) is not possible any archaeological layers which may be present
