Ms Smith,

Thanks for your reply and for explaining The National Archives’ position
regarding annotation and review of records.

I fully accept that archives do not resolve disputes and that records are
often incomplete or patchwork in character. My purpose in writing is not to
seek amendment, adjudication, or retrospective commentary, but to raise a
specific concern about how absence, silence, and fragmentation within the
archival record should be understood where material public consequences
clearly followed.

By way of clarification, my concern relates to the historical record
surrounding Great House Farm, Llandough, and the proceedings culminating in *BP
Properties Ltd v Buckler* [1987] EWCA Civ 2. In particular:

   - Ownership and occupation of the farmhouse and garden were actively
   contested in 1974, with a defence asserting adverse possession filed on
   behalf of Mary Williams, and the matter adjourned without a final
   determination of title.
   - Enforcement of earlier possession orders was deferred, a warrant was
   withdrawn on 31 October 1974, and the occupier remained in possession until
   her death in 1983.
   - Despite this, land registration, demolition (1988), and archaeological
   intervention proceeded without a High Court determination of ownership and
   without disclosure of earlier, locally known archaeological discoveries.
   - A Roman burial reportedly discovered beneath the farmhouse in 1888 by
   the occupying family appears not to have been reflected in later “rescue”
   excavations (1978–1980) or the major 1994 excavation, which treated Roman
   and early medieval burials as newly revealed.

Taken together, this produces an archival landscape in which:

   - court files relating to decisive events appear absent or incomplete;
   - later records imply finality where proceedings were in fact unresolved;
   - and the destruction of a long-occupied medieval structure occurred
   before the site’s known archaeological significance was formally
   acknowledged.

I note and appreciate your confirmation that the principal judicial series
(J 157, J 83, J 84) contain no relevant material. Given the scale of the
consequences flowing from the dispute—forced removal, demolition of an
occupied dwelling, and loss of heritage fabric—the absence of supporting
court or administrative files is itself significant for researchers.

Accordingly, I would be grateful for guidance on the following procedural
points:

   - how researchers are expected to interpret gaps or silence in the
   archival record where substantial actions plainly occurred;
   - whether related non-judicial records (for example, departmental,
   planning, regulatory, or corporate records connected to land registration,
   planning consent, or archaeological works) are more likely to sit outside
   the obvious court series; and
   - whether there is an appropriate mechanism for flagging concerns about
   record completeness or contextual risk, without seeking alteration of the
   records themselves.

For the avoidance of doubt, I am not alleging impropriety on the part of
The National Archives or its staff. My concern is to ensure that the
historical record is approached with appropriate caution where absence may
unintentionally convey certainty or closure that did not exist at the time.

Thank you again for your time and professionalism.

Yours sincerely,

Sion Buckler
