Sirs/Ma'am,

I am seeking representation and initial advice regarding the 1987 case BP
Properties Ltd v Buckler, which concerned Great House Farm, Llandough —
property long occupied by my family. I believe, among a myriad of
arguments, that the judgment was reached through a procedural inconsistency
amounting to bias or error in law, which may justify reopening under CPR
52.30 and Limitations Act Section 32.


---

Summary of the issue

In the 1987 case, the court ruled against my grandmother on the basis that
she did not formally reject an offer made by BP Properties Ltd, and treated
that as tacit acceptance. However, she had already explicitly rejected
prior licence attempts by BP Pension Trust Ltd, a related BP entity, before
BP Properties Ltd appeared as claimant.

The judge ruled that BP Properties Ltd was a distinct legal entity, and
therefore her prior rejection of BP Pension Trust Ltd’s offer was legally
irrelevant.

Later, in my father’s appeal, he argued that the wrong BP company had been
the claimant — that BP Pension Trust Ltd, not BP Properties Ltd, held the
relevant interest.
The appellate court dismissed his appeal, ruling that “BP companies are all
effectively one and the same” for the purposes of upholding the judgment.

This is a clear double standard:

When it benefited BP, the court treated the companies as separate.

When it benefited our family, they were treated as the same entity.


This contradictory reasoning — “heads BP wins, tails the Bucklers lose” —
represents either judicial bias, procedural irregularity, or corruption by
omission, and undermines the fairness of the original proceedings.


---

Grounds for reopening

Under CPR 52.30, a final judgment can be reopened if necessary to avoid
real injustice, in exceptional circumstances, where no alternative remedy
exists.
My argument is that:

The 1987 decision and subsequent appeal were tainted by inconsistent
application of corporate identity principles, creating real injustice.

The appeal court’s own language evidences bias and unequal treatment
between parties.

This meets the “exceptional circumstances” threshold.


Additionally, the Supreme Court ruling in Takhar v Gracefield Developments
Ltd [2019] UKSC 13 confirms that a judgment obtained through fraud or
concealment can be set aside even decades later — “fraud unravels all.”


---

Limitation Act 1980 – Section 32

Pursuant to s.32(1)(b):

> “Where any fact relevant to the plaintiff’s right of action has been
deliberately concealed by the defendant, the period of limitation shall not
begin to run until the plaintiff has discovered the concealment or could
with reasonable diligence have discovered it.”



I have only recently obtained and cross-referenced records revealing these
contradictions in corporate treatment and procedural handling — thus,
limitation does not bar action.
