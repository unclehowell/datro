Prospective fraud triggers to reopen the case (autoresearch, updated 2026-10-21T19:27:22Z)

The record is described as it reads; where it may support a ground, that is said as a possibility. No living private individual is said to have acted dishonestly. Conduct by companies and officials is described only by what the documents show.

Test used: setting aside a judgment for fraud, per Takhar v Gracefield Developments [2019] UKSC 13, para 56, adopting RBS v Highland Financial Partners [2013] EWCA Civ 328, para 106. That needs (a) conscious and deliberate dishonesty in evidence given, a statement made or a matter concealed; (b) that this was material, meaning “an operative cause of the court’s decision”, judged “by reference to its impact on the evidence supporting the original decision”; (c) fresh evidence (no reasonable-diligence bar where fraud was not raised at trial: Takhar para 54); and (d) credible evidence that would probably have had an important influence. Each limb is scored 0–5. Court-readiness bar: 4 or more on every limb. Usual route for a Court of Appeal judgment: a fresh claim to set it aside. CPR 52.30 reopening needs “no alternative effective remedy”. Counsel must confirm.

R3: detail

  • (a) Conscious and deliberate dishonesty: 2/5. HM Land Registry 30 Jul 2026: WA240304 "(11) Form 1C application form from Linklaters & Paines", "(10) Copy of Register edition date 23 February 1983", "(7) Application form D23 (inside exam) and letter dated 8 April 1983". Form 1C (Co.) is titled 'Application for Registration of Freehold Land on Behalf of a Company ... Not on a Recent Purchase - with Statutory Declaration in Support' (HMSO 1967 bibliographic record; the 1983 text not seen). [1987] EWCA Civ 2: the 1974 action "has never been finally heard". HM Land Registry, 25 May 2026: "did not receive or were aware of any interest ... unaware of any dispute" (H05).
  • (b) Material: an operative cause: 1/5. [1987] EWCA Civ 2: if she had acquired title by then, "she would have had an overriding interest to which the registration of BP Properties Ltd was necessarily subject". The registration date was not why she lost.
  • (c) Fresh evidence (no diligence bar, Takhar para 54): 3/5. HMLR's 2026 schedule and complaint replies are new.
  • (d) Credible; would probably have had an important influence: 2/5. An official anomaly, but the declaration itself has not been read.
  • What cuts against it: On BP's own case she was its licensee, so a declarant could honestly treat her occupation as BP's possession. The court's words about November 1982 match the register as merged on 2 Feb 1987 (cycle 3 audit). Better routes than reopening are the Land Registration Act 2002 Sch 4 / Sch 8 and, if a declaration proves false, the Perjury Act 1911 s.5, but each still runs into the 1987 judgment. [1987] EWCA Civ 2 opens: BP 'have the paper title to the farm, and indeed since November 1982 they have been the registered proprietors'. Paper title was never in issue (the defence was adverse possession only), so the registration did not found the court's title premise (E26). Run 8 (E49): the 'small window' point fails in law. Land Registration Act 1925 s.70(1)(f) made rights 'acquired or in course of being acquired under the Limitation Acts' overriding and (g) protected persons in actual occupation; s.82 rectification has no time limit (including s.82(1)(d) fraud) and s.82(3) gives no protection against an overriding interest. Her death on 26 Mar 1983 closed no challenge period; the registration could not have cost her the case.
  • The one record that would most raise the score: WA240304 item 11 (Form 1C with its statutory declaration) and item 7 (D23 and the 8 Apr 1983 letter). Already requested in the HM Land Registry subject-access/EIR email (due 28 Oct). PENDING: do not re-ask.

R1: detail

  • (a) Conscious and deliberate dishonesty: 1/5. No document yet shows anyone knew the 'agreed to buy' words were untrue. Record: BP Pension Trust letter of 31 Oct 1974 (quoted in the judgment): "we have found such a purchaser in BP Properties Ltd, who have agreed to buy the property from the Trust". BP Properties letter, same date: "You will be hearing separately from our Pension Trust that they are selling Great House Farm to BP Properties Ltd."
  • (b) Material: an operative cause: 3/5. BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 (Dillon LJ), on argument 3: "the letters record sufficiently that BP Properties Ltd had agreed to buy the property from the Pension Trust, and BP Properties Ltd was therefore prima facie in a position to grant a licence to Mrs Buckler." The whole result then rests on BP Properties' licence: "from the time of her receipt of the letters, Mrs Buckler was in possession ... by the licence of BP Properties Ltd".
  • (c) Fresh evidence (no diligence bar, Takhar para 54): 2/5. HM Land Registry letter of 30 July 2026 (ref N610CGV, S. G. Haste), WA240304 schedule: "(1) Plan to Conveyance dated 23 May 1975 ... (12) Conveyance 23 May 1975 (13) Plan to conveyance dated 23 February 1975". But the May 1975 completion was before the Court of Appeal: [1987] EWCA Civ 2 records the third argument as deriving "from the fact that the actual conveyance of the farmhouse and garden by BP Pension Trust Ltd to BP Properties Ltd did not take place until May 1975". So only the 23 Feb 1975 plan and the conveyance's recitals (unread) could be fresh. A plan prepared between an agreement and completion is ordinary conveyancing and does not by itself show that no agreement existed on 31 Oct 1974 (run 2, E22: down from 3).
  • (d) Credible; would probably have had an important influence: 1/5. Catalogue entries only; the conveyance itself (and any recital of the contract date) has not been read.
  • What cuts against it: The judge called it "this rather technical point". BP Pension Trust (the legal owner) wrote at the same time and plainly agreed, so a court may treat the licence as given with the owner's authority. An agreement inside the BP group need not have been written to be honest. Item 13 may be a typing slip (wiki A-071). Fraud must be shown to have been an operative cause, judged against the evidence the court relied on. The Court of Appeal already knew the conveyance was not completed until May 1975 and still held that the letters' words were enough. Law of Property Act 1925 s.40(1) (as enacted, read 20 Oct 2026) bars only an ACTION on an unwritten land contract; it does not make an oral agreement void. So an oral intra-group agreement to buy on 31 Oct 1974, written up as a conveyance in 1975, is lawful and does not by itself show the 'agreed to buy' words were untrue (E28).
  • The one record that would most raise the score: WA240304 item 12 (Conveyance 23 May 1975), for any recital of the date of the contract for sale. NOT pending: the HMLR subject-access/EIR email sent 20 Oct 2026 00:30 UTC (thread D260928) asks for "Form 1C as lodged; the 1971 abstract of title; all plans and the original title plans; form D23" and correspondence, which covers item 13 (the 23 Feb 1975 plan) and item 1, but not item 12.
